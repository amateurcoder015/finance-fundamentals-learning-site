# Analyzing Balance Sheets

The **balance sheet** (statement of financial position) is a snapshot of what a company **owns** (assets), what it **owes** (liabilities) and what is left for its owners (equity) **at one date**. It rests on the accounting equation:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$$

An analyst reads it to judge **liquidity** (can the firm pay near-term bills?), **solvency** (can it survive its debt over the long run?) and the **quality** of the numbers (how were the items measured?).

---

## 1. Format and Classification

Two presentation styles exist:

- **Classified balance sheet**: separates **current** items (expected to be converted to cash, sold or settled within **one year or the operating cycle**, whichever is longer) from **non-current** items. This is the common format for manufacturers and retailers.
- **Liquidity-based presentation**: items are listed in order of liquidity, with no current/non-current split. It is common for banks and other financial institutions.

IFRS does not prescribe a rigid layout but requires certain line items; U.S. GAAP is similar in practice. Limitations to remember: the balance sheet mixes **different measurement bases** (historical cost, amortized cost, fair value), so the total does not represent market value, and many valuable items (internally built brands, a skilled workforce) are not recorded at all.

## 2. Assets

### Current assets

| Item | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| **Cash and cash equivalents** | Highly liquid investments with short original maturities (typically 3 months or less) |
| **Marketable securities** | Traded financial instruments held for short-term gain; measured at fair value |
| **Trade receivables** | Reported **net of an allowance for doubtful accounts**: Net receivables = Gross receivables − Allowance |
| **Inventories** | Raw materials, work in process, finished goods; carried at the **lower of cost and net realizable value (NRV)**. Writedowns reduce income; **IFRS** allows reversal (up to original cost) if NRV recovers, **U.S. GAAP** does not |
| **Other current assets** | Prepaid expenses, accrued income |

### Non-current assets

- **Property, plant and equipment (PP&E)**: tangible assets used in operations. Carried at **cost less accumulated depreciation** (cost model). **IFRS** also allows the **revaluation model** (fair value); U.S. GAAP permits the cost model only. Land is not depreciated.
- **Investment property** (IFRS): property held to earn rent or for appreciation, carried at cost or fair value.
- **Intangible assets**: non-physical resources. **Identifiable** intangibles (patents, licenses, trademarks) can be separated from the business and may have a finite life (amortized) or an indefinite life (not amortized, but tested for **impairment**). **Internally generated** intangibles are mostly expensed as incurred (under IFRS, development costs meeting criteria are capitalized; under U.S. GAAP, most R&D is expensed).
- **Goodwill**: arises only in an acquisition, as the excess of the purchase price over the **fair value of net identifiable assets** acquired:

$$\text{Goodwill} = \text{Purchase price} - \text{Fair value of identifiable net assets}$$

Goodwill is **not amortized**; it is tested for impairment at least annually, and an impairment loss reduces assets and net income. Analysts often exclude goodwill when judging asset quality, since it cannot be sold separately.

### Financial assets

How a financial instrument is measured depends on the firm's business model.

| Category | Measurement | Where changes in value go |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Amortized cost** (IFRS) / held-to-maturity debt (U.S. GAAP) | Amortized cost | Not recognized |
| **Fair value through OCI** (IFRS) / available-for-sale debt (U.S. GAAP) | Fair value | Other comprehensive income (OCI) |
| **Fair value through profit or loss** (IFRS) / trading (U.S. GAAP) | Fair value | Net income |

### Fair value hierarchy

Fair values are ranked by input quality:

1. **Level 1**: quoted prices for identical assets in active markets (most reliable).
2. **Level 2**: observable inputs other than Level 1 (prices of similar assets, yield curves).
3. **Level 3**: unobservable inputs from management's own models (least reliable; scrutinize).

## 3. Liabilities

**Current liabilities** are due within a year or the operating cycle: **accounts payable**, **short-term debt**, the **current portion of long-term debt**, **accrued liabilities** and **unearned (deferred) revenue**, which is cash received for goods or services not yet delivered.

**Non-current liabilities** include **long-term debt** (bonds and loans, at amortized cost), **deferred tax liabilities** (taxes payable in the future because taxable income was lower than accounting income so far), **provisions** and long-term lease liabilities.

- A **provision** (IFRS) is a liability of uncertain timing or amount that is probable and can be estimated; a **contingent liability** that fails this test is only disclosed.
- Analysts watch debt closely: total debt equals short-term borrowings plus the current portion of long-term debt plus long-term debt.

---

## 4. Equity

**Shareholders' equity** is the residual claim: assets minus liabilities. Its components:

| Component | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Contributed capital** | Common and preferred stock at par plus **additional paid-in capital (share premium)** |
| **Treasury stock** | Shares the company bought back; a **deduction** from equity, with no gain or loss on the income statement |
| **Retained earnings** | Cumulative net income not paid out as dividends |
| **Accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI)** | Gains and losses that bypass net income (e.g., FVOCI securities, foreign-currency translation) |
| **Non-controlling interest** | The share of consolidated subsidiaries owned by outside investors |

Retained earnings roll forward each period:

$$RE_{\text{end}} = RE_{\text{begin}} + \text{Net income} - \text{Dividends}$$

A **statement of changes in equity** reconciles each component from the start to the end of the period.

---

## 5. Common-Size Analysis

A **vertical common-size balance sheet** expresses every line as a percentage of **total assets** (assets, liabilities and equity are each divided by total assets). It makes firms of different size comparable and reveals changes in structure over time, such as rising receivables or growing reliance on debt.

A **horizontal** analysis compares each line with the same line in an earlier period, as a percentage change.

## 6. Liquidity and Solvency Ratios

### Liquidity ratios (short-term)

$$\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$

$$\text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$

$$\text{Cash ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$

The ordering is current ≥ quick ≥ cash. The quick and cash ratios exclude inventory, which may be hard to sell quickly.

### Solvency ratios (long-term)

| Ratio | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| **Debt-to-assets** | Total debt ÷ Total assets |
| **Debt-to-capital** | Total debt ÷ (Total debt + Total shareholders' equity) |
| **Debt-to-equity** | Total debt ÷ Total shareholders' equity |
| **Financial leverage** | Average total assets ÷ Average total equity (period-end figures are used if averages are not available) |

Here **total debt** means interest-bearing borrowings (short-term debt, current portion of long-term debt, and long-term debt). Some analysts use total liabilities instead, so always check the definition. Higher ratios mean more leverage and more risk, though the right level depends on the industry and on how stable the firm's cash flows are.

## 7. Worked Examples

### Example A: Full balance sheet, common-size and ratios

A company reports (in millions):

| Assets | | Liabilities and equity | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cash | 150 | Accounts payable | 200 |
| Marketable securities | 50 | Short-term debt | 100 |
| Receivables (net) | 200 | Accrued liabilities | 100 |
| Inventories | 300 | **Current liabilities** | **400** |
| **Current assets** | **700** | Long-term debt | 600 |
| PP&E (net) | 1,000 | Deferred tax liability | 100 |
| Goodwill | 150 | **Total liabilities** | **1,100** |
| Other intangibles | 100 | Common stock and APIC | 400 |
| Long-term investments | 50 | Retained earnings | 550 |
| **Total assets** | **2,000** | AOCI | (30) |
| | | Treasury stock | (20) |
| | | **Total equity** | **900** |

Check: total liabilities and equity $= 1{,}100 + 900 = 2{,}000$, equal to total assets.

**Liquidity**

1. Current ratio $= 700 / 400 = 1.75$
2. Quick ratio $= (150 + 50 + 200) / 400 = 1.00$
3. Cash ratio $= (150 + 50) / 400 = 0.50$

**Solvency.** Total debt $= 100 + 600 = 700$.

1. Debt-to-assets $= 700 / 2{,}000 = 0.35$
2. Debt-to-capital $= 700 / (700 + 900) = 0.4375$
3. Debt-to-equity $= 700 / 900 = 0.78$
4. Financial leverage $= 2{,}000 / 900 = 2.22$

**Common-size (% of total assets).** Cash 7.5%, receivables 10.0%, inventories 15.0%, PP&E 50.0%, goodwill 7.5%; accounts payable 10.0%, long-term debt 30.0%, retained earnings 27.5%.

### Example B: Goodwill from an acquisition

A company buys 100% of a target for 500. The fair value of the target's identifiable assets is 620 and of its liabilities is 240.

1. Fair value of net identifiable assets $= 620 - 240 = 380$
2. Goodwill $= 500 - 380 = 120$

If the goodwill is later judged impaired by 40, assets and pre-tax income both fall by 40, and the carrying amount becomes 80.

### Example C: Inventory writedown

Inventory has a cost of 120 and a net realizable value of 105.

1. Carrying amount $= \min(120, 105) = 105$
2. Writedown $= 120 - 105 = 15$, charged to the income statement (usually cost of goods sold)

If NRV later recovers to 130, IFRS allows a reversal of 15, back to the original cost of 120 (not to 130); U.S. GAAP does not permit reversal.

### Example D: Retained earnings roll-forward

Beginning retained earnings are 480, net income is 150 and dividends are 80.

$$RE_{\text{end}} = 480 + 150 - 80 = 550$$

### Example E: Effect of a share buyback on ratios

Using Example A, the company uses 100 of cash to repurchase shares.

1. Cash falls to 50, so current assets fall to 600; total assets fall to 1,900.
2. Treasury stock grows by 100, so equity falls to 800.
3. Current ratio $= 600 / 400 = 1.50$ (down from 1.75).
4. Debt-to-assets $= 700 / 1{,}900 = 0.37$ and financial leverage $= 1{,}900 / 800 = 2.38$: both **rise**.

A buyback reduces liquidity and increases leverage, even though no new debt was raised.

---

## Key takeaways

- Assets $=$ liabilities $+$ equity; a classified balance sheet separates current from non-current, a liquidity-based one orders items by liquidity.
- Measurement bases mix cost, amortized cost and fair value; **Level 3** fair values deserve the most scrutiny.
- Inventory is carried at the lower of cost and NRV; **goodwill** is purchase price minus fair value of net identifiable assets, is not amortized, and is tested for impairment.
- Equity includes contributed capital, retained earnings, AOCI, non-controlling interest and **treasury stock** (a deduction).
- **Common-size** statements divide each item by total assets.
- Liquidity: current ≥ quick ≥ cash ratios. Solvency: debt-to-assets, debt-to-capital, debt-to-equity and financial leverage; check whether "debt" means borrowings or all liabilities.
