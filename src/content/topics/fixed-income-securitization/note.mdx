# Fixed-Income Securitization

**Securitization** is the process by which a company (the **originator**) pools financial assets that generate cash flows, such as mortgages, auto loans, credit-card receivables or student loans, transfers them to a separate legal entity, and that entity issues **securities** whose payments are funded by the pool. Investors buy the securities and are repaid from the cash flows of the **collateral**, not from the originator's general credit.

Securities created this way are called **asset-backed securities (ABS)** in the broad sense. When the collateral is a pool of mortgage loans they are **mortgage-backed securities (MBS)**.

---

## 1. Why Securitize?

Securitization turns illiquid assets into liquid, tradable bonds. It benefits several groups:

| Party | Benefit |
| --- | --- |
| **Originator / seller** | Moves assets off its balance sheet (when the sale qualifies as a true sale), freeing capital, getting funding that is often **cheaper** than its own bond yield, and recycling capital into new lending |
| **Investors** | Access to credit exposures not otherwise available, a wide choice of risk and maturity profiles through tranching, and often a yield pick-up over similarly rated corporate bonds |
| **Financial markets** | Greater liquidity, a broader investor base for credit risk, and more efficient allocation of capital and risk |

Funding can be cheaper because investors price the securities on the **quality of the collateral and the structure**, which may be better than the originator's own credit. A weak originator can therefore raise funds at a rate tied to the pool, not to itself.

---

## 2. The Securitization Process and Its Parties

### 2.1 Parties

- **Seller (originator / depositor)**: the entity that originated the loans or receivables and sells them to the issuer. Examples: a bank, an auto finance company, a credit-card issuer.
- **Special purpose entity (SPE)**, also called a **special purpose vehicle (SPV)** or special purpose company: the legal entity that buys the pool and issues the securities. It is created only for this purpose.
- **Servicer**: collects payments from the borrowers, passes them to the SPE/trustee, handles delinquencies and defaults, and keeps records. The servicer is often the originator and earns a servicing fee. The servicer's quality matters because poor servicing raises losses.
- **Trustee** (or **trustee agent**): holds the assets for the benefit of investors, monitors the servicer's compliance with the terms, and makes sure payments follow the priority of payments.
- **Other parties**: credit rating agencies, underwriters, legal counsel, and external providers of credit enhancement (insurers, guarantors).

### 2.2 The transaction

1. The seller **sells the pool of assets to the SPE** in exchange for cash.
2. The SPE issues securities (usually in several **tranches**) to investors through underwriters.
3. Proceeds from the securities fund the SPE's purchase price paid to the seller.
4. The servicer collects borrower payments; they flow through the SPE's **waterfall** to investors.

### 2.3 Why the SPE matters: bankruptcy remoteness

The sale to the SPE must be a **true sale**: legally, the assets leave the seller's estate. The SPE is **bankruptcy remote**, meaning that if the originator becomes insolvent, its creditors **cannot claim** the transferred assets, and the SPE cannot go bankrupt from the seller's other liabilities. As a result:

- Investors' risk depends on the **collateral and structure**, not on the originator's credit risk (apart from servicing and certain representations and warranties).
- The securities can earn a higher credit rating than the originator, which lowers the funding cost.

If the sale is not a true sale, the pool could be pulled back into the originator's bankruptcy and the whole benefit of the structure is lost.

<Note>
Investors are exposed to the originator in only two ways: the **servicer** role (if the originator is the servicer) and any **repurchase obligations** for loans that breach the stated eligibility criteria.
</Note>

## 3. Structures: Amortizing, Non-Amortizing and Waterfalls

The collateral determines the shape of the cash flows:

- **Amortizing assets** (mortgages, auto loans) make scheduled payments of **interest plus principal**, so the pool balance falls over time. They may also **prepay**.
- **Non-amortizing assets** (credit-card receivables) have no fixed principal schedule. Balances revolve: borrowers repay and re-borrow.

For non-amortizing pools the securities have:

1. a **lockout / revolving period** during which principal collected is used to buy **new receivables**, so investors receive only interest and the pool balance stays level; then
2. an **amortization period** (the principal is passed to investors) that begins at the end of the revolving period or earlier if an **early amortization trigger** is hit (for example, collateral performance deteriorates or credit enhancement is breached).

### 3.1 Single class (pass-through) vs. multiple classes

- A **pass-through** structure has one class of security; every holder shares **pro rata** in interest and principal collected, net of fees.
- A **multi-class (tranched)** structure splits the securities into classes with different priority in receiving cash and absorbing losses. This is the standard approach for ABS and for structured MBS.

The priority is set out in the **waterfall** (priority of payments): fees and expenses first, then interest and principal to the most senior class, and so on down to the most junior tranche (often called the **equity** or **residual** tranche).

## 4. Tranching: Reallocating Risk

Tranching creates securities with different risk and return from the same pool. Two basic dimensions exist.

### 4.1 Credit tranching (senior / subordinated)

In a **senior-subordinated structure** (also called credit tranching), losses are allocated from the bottom up:

- **Senior tranche**: first to be paid, last to absorb losses; highest rating and lowest yield.
- **Mezzanine / subordinated tranches**: absorb losses after the junior tranche is exhausted.
- **Junior / equity (residual) tranche**: absorbs the first losses; unrated or lowest rated and offers the highest yield.

The amount of the pool that ranks below a tranche is its **subordination**, which acts as built-in **credit enhancement** (Section 5). The tranche's loss exposure begins only after all junior tranches are wiped out.

**Worked example: loss allocation.** A pool of 100 million is financed with:

| Tranche | Size (million) | % of pool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Senior | 70 | 70% |
| Mezzanine | 20 | 20% |
| Equity (junior) | 10 | 10% |

Suppose cumulative collateral losses over the life of the deal total 12 million, and no excess spread or other enhancement is available.

1. The equity tranche absorbs the first 10 million: it is **wiped out** (100% loss).
2. The remaining 2 million falls on the mezzanine tranche: loss $= 2/20 = 10\%$ of its principal.
3. The senior tranche is untouched: loss $= 0$.

The senior tranche is protected by **30%** subordination (20 + 10). It starts to lose only if cumulative losses exceed 30 million, i.e. 30% of the pool.

### 4.2 Time tranching (sequential-pay structures)

**Time tranching** divides principal repayment by **timing**, mainly to manage **prepayment risk** in pools of amortizing assets. In a **sequential-pay** structure:

- All principal collected (scheduled and prepaid) goes first to **Tranche A** until it is fully repaid;
- then to **Tranche B**, then **Tranche C**, and so on;
- interest is paid to each tranche on its outstanding balance.

Tranche A has the shortest expected life and Tranche C the longest, so investors can choose their preferred maturity. Time tranching **redistributes** prepayment risk, but does not remove it: if prepayments change, the average lives of the tranches change (**contraction risk** when prepayments speed up, **extension risk** when they slow down).

Real deals often combine both: senior and subordinate classes (credit tranching), and within the senior class several sequential-pay tranches (time tranching).

## 5. Credit Enhancement

**Credit enhancement** reduces the credit risk of the securities, mainly for the senior tranches. It is divided into two kinds.

### 5.1 Internal credit enhancement

Provided within the deal's own structure:

- **Subordination** (senior-subordinated structure): junior tranches absorb losses first, as in Section 4.1.
- **Overcollateralization (OC)**: the collateral pool's balance **exceeds** the face value of securities issued. The excess is a cushion for losses. Example: collateral 105 million vs. securities 100 million gives OC of 5 million. As losses occur, the cushion shrinks first.
- **Excess spread**: the pool's interest income exceeds the interest paid on the securities plus servicing fees and other costs. The surplus can absorb losses or be trapped in a reserve account.
- **Reserve account**: cash held back to cover shortfalls. A **cash reserve fund** is funded at the start from deal proceeds; an **excess spread account** is built up from the excess spread over time.

**Worked example: excess spread.** A pool of 100 million earns a weighted average coupon of 7.00% (7.0 million a year). The securities have a weighted average coupon of 5.00% on 100 million (5.0 million). The servicing fee is 0.50% of the pool (0.5 million).

$$\text{Excess spread} = 7.0 - 5.0 - 0.5 = 1.5\ \text{million}$$

which is $1.5\%$ of the pool per year. If the pool loses 1.0 million to defaults in a year, the excess spread absorbs it and 0.5 million is left over to trap in a reserve account. Investors are not harmed, even though the pool has defaults.

### 5.2 External credit enhancement

Provided by a **third party**, which adds counterparty risk:

- **Bond insurance / financial guarantees** (a "wrap") from a monoline insurer, which guarantees timely payment of interest and principal.
- **Letters of credit** from a bank.
- **Corporate guarantees** from the sponsor or another entity.
- **Cash collateral accounts** funded by a third party.

External enhancement is only as good as the credit of the provider: if the guarantor is downgraded, the securities' rating may also fall. Internal enhancement avoids that dependence.

## 6. Covered Bonds vs. Securitized Bonds

A **covered bond** is a senior debt obligation of a bank or financial institution that is backed by a segregated **cover pool** of high-quality assets (typically residential mortgages or public-sector loans). It resembles securitization but differs in important respects.

| Feature | Covered bond | Securitization (ABS/MBS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Issuer | The **bank itself** (or a subsidiary); assets stay on the bank's balance sheet | A separate **SPE**; assets are legally sold |
| Investor recourse | **Dual recourse**: first to the cover pool, then (if the pool is insufficient) a claim against the issuer as an unsecured senior creditor | Recourse is **only** to the collateral pool (and the structure) |
| Cover pool | **Dynamic**: the issuer must replace non-performing or prepaid loans so the pool keeps its quality and size | **Static** (or fixed during the revolving period): defaults and prepayments are borne by investors through the structure |
| Tranching | Typically a single class | Often multiple tranches |
| Prepayment/extension | Investors are insulated from prepayment risk since bad or prepaid assets are replaced | Investors bear prepayment and default risk |
| Structure | Bullet payments, often with a soft or hard maturity extension | Amortizing or revolving; sequential or pro-rata |

Because of the dual recourse and the dynamic pool, covered bonds are generally rated **higher** than the issuer's own unsecured debt and trade tighter than comparable ABS. The cover pool is usually protected by law or contract if the issuer fails.

---

## 7. Putting the Pieces Together

The typical credit-card ABS illustrates the whole architecture:

1. A bank (**seller**) sells credit-card receivables to a **master trust SPE**.
2. The trust issues **tranched notes** (AAA, A, BBB) plus a residual interest.
3. During the **revolving period**, principal collected buys new receivables; investors get interest and the pool balance stays constant.
4. Internal enhancement: subordination, excess spread, reserve account, and a seller's interest (overcollateralization).
5. If performance drops below thresholds, an **early amortization trigger** switches the deal to repay principal to investors, starting with the senior note.

---

## Key takeaways

- **Securitization** transfers a pool of assets to a **bankruptcy-remote SPE**, which issues securities repaid from the pool's cash flows. The sale must be a **true sale**.
- Parties: **seller/originator**, **SPE**, **servicer**, **trustee**, plus rating agencies, underwriters and credit-enhancement providers.
- Benefits: lower funding cost and balance-sheet relief for the originator; liquidity and customized risk for investors.
- **Amortizing** collateral passes through scheduled principal and prepayments; **non-amortizing** collateral (credit cards) has a revolving period followed by amortization, which may begin early under a **trigger**.
- **Credit tranching** (senior/subordinated) allocates losses bottom-up; **time tranching** (sequential pay) redistributes **prepayment** risk, creating contraction and extension risk in different tranches.
- **Internal enhancement**: subordination, overcollateralization, excess spread, reserve accounts. **External enhancement**: guarantees, bond insurance, letters of credit, which add provider credit risk.
- **Covered bonds** stay on the issuer's balance sheet, give **dual recourse**, and have a **dynamic** cover pool; ABS/MBS have recourse only to a **static** pool via an SPE.
