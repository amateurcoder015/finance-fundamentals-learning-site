Supply and demand is the foundational model of how markets set prices.
Almost everything else in economics — inflation, interest rates,
unemployment, even how a company prices a product — traces back to this
one relationship.

## 1. The Two Curves

**Demand** describes how much of a good buyers want to purchase at each
possible price. As price rises, quantity demanded falls — this is the
**law of demand**, and it's why the demand curve slopes downward.

**Supply** describes how much of a good producers are willing to sell at
each possible price. As price rises, producers are willing to supply more
— the **law of supply** — so the supply curve slopes upward.

## 2. Equilibrium

Where the two curves intersect is the **equilibrium price** and
**equilibrium quantity** — the price at which the quantity buyers want to
buy exactly equals the quantity sellers want to sell.

- If price is set **above** equilibrium: quantity supplied exceeds
  quantity demanded → a **surplus** → sellers cut prices to clear
  inventory, pushing price back down toward equilibrium.
- If price is set **below** equilibrium: quantity demanded exceeds
  quantity supplied → a **shortage** → buyers compete for scarce goods,
  bidding price back up toward equilibrium.

This self-correcting tendency is why markets are said to "clear."

## 3. Shifts vs. Movements

A key distinction that trips people up:

- A **movement along** a curve happens when price changes and quantity
  responds — this happens automatically as part of the supply/demand
  relationship itself.
- A **shift of** a curve happens when something *other than price*
  changes the whole relationship — the entire curve moves to a new
  position.

**Demand shifts right** (increases) when: incomes rise, the good becomes
more fashionable/preferred, the price of a substitute rises, or the price
of a complement falls. It shifts left for the opposite reasons.

**Supply shifts right** (increases) when: input costs fall, technology
improves production, or more producers enter the market. It shifts left
for the opposite reasons.

## 4. Elasticity — how sensitive is the response?

**Price elasticity of demand** measures how much quantity demanded changes
when price changes:

$$
E_d = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}
$$

- If $|E_d| > 1$: demand is **elastic** — buyers are quite sensitive to
  price (e.g. luxury goods, goods with many substitutes).
- If $|E_d| < 1$: demand is **inelastic** — buyers keep buying even as
  price changes (e.g. insulin, gasoline in the short run).

The same logic applies to supply elasticity, measuring how responsive
producers are to price changes.

## 5. Why this matters beyond the textbook

This model explains real phenomena: why rent control (a price ceiling
below equilibrium) creates housing shortages, why minimum wage (a price
floor above equilibrium) can create labor surpluses (unemployment) if set
too high, and why oil price shocks ripple through an economy — supply
shifts left, price rises, and everything using oil as an input responds.
