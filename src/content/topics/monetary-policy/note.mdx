# Monetary Policy

**Monetary policy** is the central bank's management of the money supply and short-term interest rates to influence inflation, employment and growth. Together with fiscal policy it is the main way policymakers steer the economy, and it is the single largest driver of short-term interest rates, bond yields, currency values and risk appetite in financial markets. This chapter covers the functions of money and how banks create it, the quantity theory and Fisher effect, what central banks do and want, their policy tools, the neutral interest rate, the transmission mechanism, and the limits of monetary policy.

---

## 1. Money and the Money Creation Process

### Functions of money

Money is anything generally accepted as payment. It serves three functions:

| Function | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Medium of exchange** | Accepted in payment for goods, services and debts, removing the need for barter |
| **Unit of account** | A common measure in which prices, wages and debts are quoted |
| **Store of value** | Preserves purchasing power over time (imperfectly, because inflation erodes it) |

### Narrow and broad money

**Narrow money** is the most liquid form: notes and coins in circulation plus overnight deposits and other deposits that can be withdrawn on demand. **Broad money** adds less liquid holdings such as savings and time deposits and money-market funds. Definitions differ by country.

### Fractional reserve banking and the money multiplier

Banks accept deposits and lend most of them out, keeping only a fraction as **reserves**. The **reserve requirement** is the minimum fraction of deposits that must be held in reserve. When a loan is spent, the funds are deposited in another bank, which lends out most of them again, and so on. Deposits are created at each round.

If all loans are redeposited in the banking system and banks hold no excess reserves, the **money multiplier** is:

$$\text{Money multiplier} = \frac{1}{\text{Reserve requirement}}$$

and the maximum increase in deposits from a new deposit (new reserves) is:

$$\Delta \text{Deposits} = \frac{\Delta \text{Reserves}}{\text{Reserve requirement}}$$

### Worked example: money creation

The reserve requirement is 10%. A customer deposits 1,000 in Bank A.

1. Bank A keeps $0.10 \times 1{,}000 = 100$ and lends **900**.
2. The 900 is spent and deposited in Bank B, which keeps 90 and lends **810**.
3. Bank C receives 810, keeps 81 and lends **729**, and so on. Each round of lending is 90% of the previous round.
4. Multiplier: $\dfrac{1}{0.10} = 10$.
5. Maximum total deposits supported by the initial 1,000 of reserves: $1{,}000 \times 10 = 10{,}000$.
6. Of that, the initial 1,000 was the original deposit, so the banking system has created **9,000** of new deposits (equal to the new lending: $900 + 810 + 729 + \dots = 900/0.10 = 9{,}000$).

<Note>
The money multiplier is a ceiling. In practice it is smaller because banks hold excess reserves, households hold some money as cash, and some loans are not redeposited. Where central banks now pay interest on reserves and lending depends on capital and demand for credit, the reserve requirement is a weak constraint.
</Note>

## 2. The Quantity Theory and the Fisher Effect

### Quantity theory of money

The **equation of exchange** links the money supply to nominal spending:

$$M \times V = P \times Y$$

where $M$ is the money supply, $V$ the **velocity of money** (how often a unit of money is spent per period), $P$ the price level and $Y$ real output. In growth-rate form:

$$\%\Delta M + \%\Delta V = \%\Delta P + \%\Delta Y$$

The **quantity theory** assumes $V$ and $Y$ are stable in the long run, so that $\%\Delta P \approx \%\Delta M$: **inflation is a monetary phenomenon**, and in the long run faster money growth than output growth produces inflation. In the short run, velocity and output can change, so the link is looser.

**Example.** Money supply grows 6%, velocity is rising 1%, and real output grows 3%. Then $\%\Delta P = 6 + 1 - 3 = 4\%$.

### The Fisher effect

The **Fisher effect** says the nominal interest rate equals the real rate plus expected inflation:

$$R_{\text{nominal}} \approx R_{\text{real}} + E[\pi]$$

An investor demands compensation for expected loss of purchasing power. If the real rate is 2% and expected inflation is 3%, the nominal rate is about 5%. Exactly, $(1+R_{\text{nom}}) = (1+R_{\text{real}})(1+E[\pi])$, giving $1.02 \times 1.03 - 1 = 5.06\%$. A rise in expected inflation therefore lifts nominal yields even if the real rate is unchanged, which is why credible central bank inflation control matters to bond investors.

## 3. Central Banks: Roles and Objectives

### Roles

- **Sole supplier of currency** (notes and coins) and issuer of bank reserves.
- **Banker to the government and to banks**, operating settlement and payment systems.
- **Lender of last resort**: providing liquidity to solvent banks in a crisis to prevent bank runs and contagion.
- **Regulator and supervisor** of banks (in many countries), promoting financial stability.
- **Holder of the nation's gold and foreign-exchange reserves**, and in some countries manager of the exchange rate.
- **Conductor of monetary policy.**

### Objectives

The dominant objective is **price stability**, usually defined as low and stable inflation, because high or volatile inflation distorts decisions, redistributes wealth arbitrarily and raises uncertainty. Other objectives include:

- Stability in the currency exchange rate.
- Full employment and maximum sustainable growth.
- Moderate long-term interest rates.
- Financial stability.

Some central banks have a **single mandate** (price stability), others a **dual mandate** (inflation and employment).

### Why some inflation is targeted

Most central banks aim for low positive inflation (commonly 2%), not zero. Reasons: measured inflation overstates true inflation (quality improvements), a cushion against **deflation** (falling prices raise real debt burdens and encourage postponed spending), and room to cut the real interest rate below zero when the nominal rate cannot go much below zero.

### Inflation targeting and independence

Under **inflation targeting** the central bank announces a numerical target (a point such as 2%, or a range such as 2% to 4%) and adjusts policy to achieve it, with a published forecast. It improves transparency and anchors inflation expectations.

| Concept | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Operational independence** | The central bank is free to choose its tools and settings to hit the target |
| **Target independence** | The central bank also chooses the target itself (rarer) |
| **Credibility** | The public believes the bank will deliver; it anchors expectations and makes disinflation less costly |
| **Transparency** | Publishing forecasts, minutes and reasoning improves accountability |

A central bank that is not independent may be pressed to finance deficits or over-stimulate before elections, which leads to higher inflation.

## 4. Monetary Policy Tools

### Policy rate

Most central banks now steer a short-term **policy rate** (for example the rate on overnight interbank lending or on borrowing from the central bank). Banks pass it through to lending and deposit rates, and it anchors short-term market rates across the economy.

### Reserve requirements

Raising the reserve requirement cuts the amount banks can lend and the money multiplier, **tightening** policy; lowering it **loosens** policy. It is a blunt tool and used rarely in developed markets.

### Open market operations

**Open market operations** are purchases and sales of securities by the central bank.

| Operation | Effect on bank reserves | Effect on short-term rates | Stance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Buy securities** | Reserves rise | Fall | Expansionary |
| **Sell securities** | Reserves fall | Rise | Contractionary |

These are used to hit the policy rate by changing the supply of reserves.

### Lending facility (discount rate)

The rate at which banks can borrow directly from the central bank. It acts as a ceiling for the interbank rate and supports the lender-of-last-resort function.

### Unconventional tools

When the policy rate is at or near zero, central banks turn to other tools:

- **Quantitative easing (QE)**: large-scale purchases of government bonds and sometimes private assets, creating reserves, lowering longer-term yields and encouraging risk-taking. The reverse is **quantitative tightening**.
- **Forward guidance**: communicating the expected path of policy to shape market expectations of future rates.
- **Negative interest rate policy**: setting the policy rate below zero to penalise holding reserves and encourage lending.
- **Targeted lending and asset purchase programmes** to support specific markets.

## 5. The Neutral Rate and the Policy Stance

The **neutral interest rate** is the rate at which policy neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. It is approximately the sum of the trend real growth rate and the inflation target:

$$\text{Neutral rate} = \text{Trend real growth} + \text{Inflation target}$$

| Policy rate vs neutral rate | Stance | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Above neutral | **Contractionary** (restrictive) | Slows demand and inflation |
| Below neutral | **Expansionary** (accommodative) | Boosts demand and inflation |

**Example.** Trend growth is 2.5% and the inflation target is 2.0%, so the neutral rate is $2.5 + 2.0 = 4.5\%$. If the policy rate is 3.5%, policy is expansionary (1 percentage point below neutral). If it were 6.0%, policy would be contractionary.

It is also useful to speak of the **real policy rate**, the policy rate minus expected inflation. Policy is expansionary when the real policy rate is below the real neutral rate (the trend growth rate).

### The Taylor rule

A common rule of thumb for setting the policy rate links it to inflation and the output gap:

$$i = r_n + \pi + 0.5(\pi - \pi^*) + 0.5(Y - Y^*)$$

where $r_n$ is the neutral real rate, $\pi$ current inflation, $\pi^*$ the inflation target and $(Y-Y^*)$ the output gap in percent of potential. The bank raises the rate when inflation is above target or output above potential, and lowers it in the opposite case.

**Example.** $r_n = 2\%$, $\pi = 3\%$, $\pi^* = 2\%$ and output gap $= -2\%$:

$$i = 2 + 3 + 0.5(3-2) + 0.5(-2) = 2 + 3 + 0.5 - 1.0 = 4.5\%$$

## 6. The Transmission Mechanism

A change in the policy rate works through several channels:

1. **Short-term market rates**: banks quickly change deposit and lending rates, and money-market yields follow the policy rate.
2. **Asset prices**: lower rates raise the present value of future cash flows, lifting bond and equity prices and household wealth.
3. **Expectations**: a credible shift changes expected inflation and expected future rates, moving longer-term yields and wage and price setting.
4. **Credit and spending**: cheaper borrowing raises business investment, housing and durable-goods consumption, and eases credit conditions.
5. **Exchange rate**: lower rates make domestic assets less attractive, so the currency tends to **depreciate**, boosting exports and raising import prices.

Expansionary policy therefore raises aggregate demand through consumption, investment and net exports, increasing output and inflation. Contractionary policy does the reverse. Long-term yields do not always follow the policy rate: if easing raises expected inflation, long yields can **rise** even as short rates fall.

## 7. Limitations of Monetary Policy

- **Long and variable lags**: policy affects the economy only after a delay of several quarters, and the delay changes over time, so it must be set on forecasts.
- **Liquidity trap and the zero lower bound**: when rates are near zero and households and firms hoard money, extra reserves do not translate into lending or spending, and rate cuts lose force.
- **Weak credit demand or supply**: if banks are impaired or borrowers are over-indebted, cheaper money may not be lent or borrowed.
- **Unanchored expectations**: if the public doubts the central bank, rate changes may not alter inflation expectations as intended, or may raise long-term yields (**bond market vigilantes**).
- **Imperfect measurement**: potential output and the neutral rate cannot be observed and are estimated with error.
- **Side effects**: prolonged low rates can inflate asset prices, encourage excess risk-taking and strain pension and insurance business.
- **Conflict with other goals**: the exchange rate target, fiscal dominance and financial stability can limit the room to raise rates.

<Hl>Investors watch the policy rate, forward guidance and the central bank's balance sheet because they move short rates, curve shape, equity valuations and currencies.</Hl>

## Key takeaways

- Money is a **medium of exchange**, **unit of account** and **store of value**. With fractional reserves the money multiplier is $1/\text{reserve requirement}$ (a maximum).
- The **quantity theory** ($MV = PY$) implies inflation is a monetary phenomenon in the long run; the **Fisher effect** gives $R_{\text{nominal}} \approx R_{\text{real}} + E[\pi]$.
- The central bank's main objective is **price stability**; credibility, transparency and independence help it deliver. Targets are usually positive (about 2%) to guard against deflation.
- Tools: **policy rate**, **reserve requirements**, **open market operations**, **lending facility**; unconventional tools include **QE**, **forward guidance** and **negative rates**.
- **Neutral rate** = trend real growth + inflation target. Policy rate above neutral is contractionary; below is expansionary.
- Transmission works through short rates, asset prices, expectations, credit and the exchange rate.
- Limits include lags, the liquidity trap and zero lower bound, unanchored expectations and measurement error.
