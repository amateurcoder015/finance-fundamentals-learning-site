# Working Capital and Liquidity

A profitable company can still fail if it cannot pay its bills on time. **Working capital management** is the day-to-day management of a company's short-term assets and liabilities so that it can meet obligations as they fall due, while not tying up more cash than necessary. **Liquidity** is the ability to turn assets into cash quickly, at a price close to fair value, and to pay obligations when due.

---

## 1. Working Capital and Liquidity Basics

$$\text{Net working capital} = \text{Current assets} - \text{Current liabilities}$$

- **Current assets**: cash and equivalents, marketable securities, accounts receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses.
- **Current liabilities**: accounts payable, accrued expenses, short-term debt and the current portion of long-term debt.
- Working capital can be viewed as the investment the company must make in its operating cycle. Too little risks stock-outs and missed payments; too much ties up cash that could earn a return elsewhere.

**Operating working capital** often excludes cash and short-term debt, focusing on receivables, inventory and payables, which are the items directly tied to operations.

### Primary and secondary sources of liquidity

| Type | Sources | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary** | **Ready cash** and marketable securities; **short-term funds** such as a bank line of credit or commercial paper; **cash flow management** (speeding collections, controlling disbursements) | Available without changing normal operations |
| **Secondary** | **Liquidating assets** (selling inventory or equipment); **renegotiating debt** contracts; **filing for bankruptcy protection and reorganization** | Costly, and may signal financial weakness to the market |

### Drags and pulls on liquidity

- **Drags on liquidity** slow cash **inflows**: uncollected receivables, obsolete or slow-moving inventory, tight credit that makes borrowing harder.
- **Pulls on liquidity** speed cash **outflows**: paying suppliers early, reduced credit limits from suppliers, limits on short-term borrowing, and a deteriorating credit rating.

Both reduce the company's cash availability. Managers monitor them through the ratios in the next section.

## 2. Measuring Liquidity

| Ratio | Formula | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Current ratio** | $\dfrac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$ | Coverage of short-term obligations by all current assets |
| **Quick ratio** | $\dfrac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$ | Stricter test that excludes inventory and prepaid items |
| **Cash ratio** | $\dfrac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$ | Strictest test: only the most liquid assets |
| **Defensive interval** | $\dfrac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{Daily cash expenditures}}$ | Number of days the company can pay its expenses without new revenue |

$$\text{Daily cash expenditures} = \frac{\text{Annual cash expenses}}{365}$$

Higher ratios mean stronger liquidity, but a ratio that is too high can mean idle assets that earn little. Interpretation should consider industry norms and the quality of the assets (are the receivables collectible, is the inventory saleable?).

## 3. The Operating Cycle and the Cash Conversion Cycle

The **operating cycle** is the time from buying inventory until the cash from selling it is collected. The **cash conversion cycle (CCC)**, also called the net operating cycle, is that period less the time the company takes to pay its suppliers.

$$\text{DIO} = \frac{\text{Average inventory}}{\text{COGS}} \times 365 \qquad \text{DSO} = \frac{\text{Average receivables}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 365 \qquad \text{DPO} = \frac{\text{Average payables}}{\text{COGS}} \times 365$$

$$\text{Operating cycle} = \text{DIO} + \text{DSO}$$

$$\text{Cash conversion cycle} = \text{DIO} + \text{DSO} - \text{DPO}$$

- **DIO** (days of inventory on hand) is the average number of days inventory is held before sale.
- **DSO** (days sales outstanding) is the average collection period.
- **DPO** (days payables outstanding) is the average number of days the company takes to pay suppliers.
- A **shorter CCC** means less cash is tied up in operations. A negative CCC is possible (customers pay before suppliers must be paid), as at some retailers.
- Lengthening DPO improves the CCC, but stretching payables too far can damage supplier relationships and forfeit early-payment discounts.

<Note>
Compare the CCC with the company's own history and with peers. A rising CCC with flat sales often signals slower collections or building inventory, which are drags on liquidity.
</Note>

## 4. Managing the Components

### Cash management

- **Forecast** cash inflows and outflows to know when surpluses and shortfalls will occur.
- **Collect faster and pay on time**: lockboxes and electronic payments speed receipts, and centralized control reduces idle balances.
- **Invest surplus cash** in short-term instruments (treasury bills, bank deposits, commercial paper, money market funds) consistent with a written **investment policy** that sets priorities: **safety, liquidity, then yield**.

### Receivables management

- **Credit policy**: set standards for whom to extend credit, on what **terms**, and with what limits.
- Monitor with **DSO** and an **aging schedule** (receivables sorted by days outstanding) to spot slow payers.
- Tighter credit lowers bad debts and DSO but may reduce sales; looser credit does the opposite.

### Inventory management

- Holding inventory ties up cash and brings storage, obsolescence and insurance costs, but too little causes stock-outs.
- Approaches include **just-in-time (JIT)**, which minimizes holdings through close coordination with suppliers, versus holding **safety stock** against supply or demand shocks.
- Monitor with DIO and inventory turnover.

### Accounts payable management

- Use trade credit as a source of financing, but compare the cost of **forgoing early-payment discounts** with the cost of other financing.
- A supplier that offers terms of **"2/10 net 30"** gives a 2% discount if paid within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due in 30 days.

The **cost of trade credit** is the effective annual rate of **not** taking the discount (that is, borrowing from the supplier for the extra days). With discount $d$ (as a decimal), discount period $T$ days and net due date $N$ days:

$$\text{Cost of trade credit} = \left(1 + \frac{d}{1-d}\right)^{\frac{365}{N-T}} - 1$$

## 5. Short-Term Financing

When internal cash is insufficient, companies turn to short-term sources.

| Source | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Uncommitted line of credit** | Bank may decline to lend; no commitment fee; least certain |
| **Committed (regular) line of credit** | Bank is obligated to lend up to a limit for a fee; typically 1 year |
| **Revolving credit facility** | Committed, multi-year; borrow, repay and re-borrow; pays a **commitment fee** on the unused portion |
| **Commercial paper** | Short-term unsecured notes sold by large, creditworthy firms; low cost; usually backed by a bank line |
| **Secured / asset-based loans** | Backed by receivables or inventory as collateral; cheaper but encumbers assets |
| **Factoring** | Selling receivables at a discount for immediate cash |
| **Trade credit** | Supplier financing through payment terms, as above |

Factors in choice: cost, flexibility, availability (credit rating), and the company's need for **committed** funding in a crisis. Reliance on short-term funding for long-term needs creates **refinancing (rollover) risk**.

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example A: Liquidity ratios

A company reports: cash 40, marketable securities 20, receivables 90, inventory 150 (all in millions), current liabilities 200, and annual cash operating expenses of 1,460.

1. Current assets $= 40 + 20 + 90 + 150 = 300$
2. Current ratio $= 300 / 200 = \mathbf{1.50}$
3. Quick ratio $= (40 + 20 + 90) / 200 = 150 / 200 = \mathbf{0.75}$
4. Cash ratio $= (40 + 20) / 200 = 60 / 200 = \mathbf{0.30}$
5. Daily cash expenditures $= 1{,}460 / 365 = 4.0$; defensive interval $= 150 / 4.0 = \mathbf{37.5}$ days

The current ratio looks comfortable, but the quick ratio of 0.75 shows the company relies on selling inventory to meet current liabilities.

### Example B: Cash conversion cycle

Revenue 1,200; COGS 900; average inventory 150; average receivables 100; average payables 90.

1. DIO $= 150 / 900 \times 365 = 60.83$ days
2. DSO $= 100 / 1{,}200 \times 365 = 30.42$ days
3. DPO $= 90 / 900 \times 365 = 36.50$ days
4. Operating cycle $= 60.83 + 30.42 = 91.25$ days
5. CCC $= 91.25 - 36.50 = \mathbf{54.75}$ days

Cash is tied up for about 55 days between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. If the company negotiated DPO to 45 days, the CCC would fall to 46.25 days.

### Example C: Cost of forgoing a trade discount

Supplier terms are **2/10 net 30**. Not taking the discount lets the company use the money for $30 - 10 = 20$ extra days.

1. Periodic cost $= 0.02 / 0.98 = 2.0408\%$
2. Number of 20-day periods in a year $= 365 / 20 = 18.25$
3. Effective annual cost $= (1.020408)^{18.25} - 1 = \mathbf{44.59\%}$

Unless the company cannot borrow at less than about 44.6%, it should take the discount, even if it must borrow short term to do so.

### Example D: Effective cost of a bank loan

A company borrows 1,000,000 for 90 days at 6% a year (interest on a 360-day basis) and pays a 0.5% upfront fee (5,000).

1. Interest $= 1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.06 \times 90/360 = 15{,}000$
2. Usable proceeds $= 1{,}000{,}000 - 5{,}000 = 995{,}000$; repayment $= 1{,}015{,}000$
3. 90-day cost $= 1{,}015{,}000 / 995{,}000 - 1 = 2.0101\%$
4. Effective annual rate $= (1.020101)^{365/90} - 1 = \mathbf{8.41\%}$

The fee lifts the effective cost above the 6% quoted rate. Fees and the compounding frequency must be included when comparing sources of financing.

## Key takeaways

- **Net working capital** = current assets − current liabilities; the goal is enough liquidity at the lowest carrying cost.
- **Primary** liquidity sources are cash, short-term funds and cash flow management; **secondary** sources (asset sales, debt renegotiation, bankruptcy protection) are costly and signal weakness.
- **Drags** slow inflows; **pulls** speed outflows.
- Liquidity ratios in increasing strictness: **current**, **quick**, **cash**; the **defensive interval** expresses coverage in days.
- **Operating cycle** = DIO + DSO; **CCC** = DIO + DSO − DPO. Shorter is generally better.
- **Cost of trade credit** = $(1 + d/(1-d))^{365/(N-T)} - 1$; a discount like 2/10 net 30 is usually very expensive to forgo.
- Short-term financing options differ in **commitment, cost, collateral** and refinancing risk; always compare on an **effective annual** basis.
