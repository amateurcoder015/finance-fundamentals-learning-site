# The Behavioral Biases of Individuals

Traditional finance assumes that investors are rational: they use all available information, update their beliefs correctly, and choose the portfolio that maximizes their expected utility. **Behavioral finance** asks how people actually decide. It finds that investors often depart from this ideal in systematic, predictable ways, and that those departures can affect both individual portfolios and market prices.

---

## 1. Traditional Versus Behavioral Finance

| | Traditional finance | Behavioral finance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Decision maker** | "Rational economic man": fully informed, self-interested, maximizes utility | Normal people with limited attention, imperfect memory and emotions |
| **Risk** | Investors are **risk averse** and measure risk across the whole portfolio (variance, correlations) | Investors judge each gain and loss on its own against a reference point |
| **Markets** | Efficient: prices reflect all available information; anomalies are temporary or noise | Prices can deviate from fundamentals for long periods; some anomalies persist |
| **Portfolio** | Mean-variance optimal portfolio on the efficient frontier | Portfolio assembled in layers or "mental accounts" |

Key behavioral frameworks:

- **Bounded rationality**: people satisfice (choose an acceptable answer) because time, information and processing power are limited, rather than optimize.
- **Prospect theory** (Kahneman and Tversky): people evaluate outcomes as **gains or losses relative to a reference point**, not as final wealth. The value function is **concave for gains** (risk averse when ahead), **convex for losses** (risk seeking when behind) and **steeper for losses than for gains** (loss aversion).
- **Behavioral portfolio theory** (Shefrin and Statman): investors build a **pyramid of layers**, from downside protection at the base (safe assets for security) to upside potential at the top (speculative assets, a lottery-like chance of getting rich). Each layer has its own goal and risk attitude, and correlations between layers are largely ignored.
- **Adaptive markets hypothesis** (Lo): efficiency is not a constant. Behavior and market efficiency evolve as participants learn and compete, so biases can matter more in some periods than in others.

## 2. Cognitive Errors Versus Emotional Biases

| | **Cognitive errors** | **Emotional biases** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source** | Faulty reasoning, flawed memory or errors in processing information | Feelings, impulses and intuition rather than analysis |
| **Nature** | Basic statistical, information-processing or memory mistakes | Arise spontaneously from attitudes and feelings |
| **Correctability** | **Easier**: better information, education and advice can reduce them | **Harder**: often cannot be corrected, so the adviser **adapts** the plan to the bias |

Cognitive errors fall into two groups: **belief perseverance** biases (clinging to prior beliefs) and **information-processing** biases (processing data in an illogical or irrational way).

<Note>
The distinction guides the remedy. For a cognitive error, ask "what is the information or reasoning mistake, and how do we fix it?" For an emotional bias, ask "how do we build a plan the client can stick with, given that the feeling will not go away?"
</Note>

---

## 3. Cognitive Errors: Belief Perseverance

People who hold a belief tend to defend it. Belief perseverance biases arise partly from the discomfort of **cognitive dissonance**, the tension between new information and an existing view.

| Bias | Description | Typical consequence for investors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conservatism** | Clinging to a prior view or forecast and **updating too slowly** when new information arrives | Underreaction to news; sticking with outdated earnings forecasts |
| **Confirmation** | Seeking and overweighting evidence that **supports** a belief while discounting evidence that contradicts it | Concentrated, under-diversified positions; one-sided research |
| **Representativeness** | Classifying new information by how closely it resembles a familiar category, ignoring **base rates** and **sample size** | Treating a recent hot fund or "good company" as a good investment; extrapolating from short track records |
| **Illusion of control** | Believing one can influence outcomes that are largely random | Excessive trading, concentrated portfolios, underestimating risk |
| **Hindsight** | Remembering past events as having been predictable ("I knew it all along") | Overestimating one's forecasting skill, taking too much risk, blaming advisers unfairly |

Two forms of representativeness matter for exams:

- **Base-rate neglect**: classifying an item using its resemblance to a category while ignoring how common the category is.
- **Sample-size neglect** (the "law of small numbers"): treating a small sample as representative of the population, for example judging a manager skilled after three good years.

---

## 4. Cognitive Errors: Information Processing

| Bias | Description | Typical consequence for investors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Anchoring and adjustment** | Fixing on an initial value (an anchor) such as a purchase price or a prior forecast and **adjusting too little** from it | Reluctance to sell until the price returns to cost; analysts' estimates stuck near an earlier level |
| **Mental accounting** | Dividing money into separate accounts and treating each differently (for example "income" versus "capital gains", "safe money" versus "play money") | Ignoring correlations across assets; holding low-yield cash while carrying high-cost debt; "house money" risk taking |
| **Framing** | Decisions change with how identical information is **presented** (as a gain or as a loss) | Choosing differently when the same outcome is described as "90% success" or "10% failure" |
| **Availability** | Estimating the likelihood of an event by how **easily examples come to mind** | Overestimating risks that are recent, vivid or heavily reported; home bias; buying stocks that are in the news |

---

## 5. Emotional Biases

| Bias | Description | Typical consequence for investors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Loss aversion** | Feeling losses more strongly than equal-sized gains. Linked to the **disposition effect**: selling winners too early and holding losers too long | Hold losing positions hoping to break even; trim winners prematurely; sub-optimal, often too conservative, allocation |
| **Overconfidence** | Overestimating one's own knowledge, skill or the precision of forecasts. Includes **prediction overconfidence** (forecast ranges too narrow) and **certainty overconfidence** (too sure of being right); related to **self-attribution bias** (credit success to skill, blame failure on luck) | Excessive trading, under-diversification, underestimating risk, lower net returns after costs |
| **Self-control** | Favoring immediate gratification over long-term goals | Under-saving for retirement; chasing high current income; taking on too much risk to "catch up" |
| **Status quo** | Preferring to leave things as they are, from inertia or comfort | Failing to rebalance or review; keeping an unsuitable allocation or a default option |
| **Endowment** | Valuing an asset more **because you own it** (willingness to accept exceeds willingness to pay) | Holding inherited or employer stock regardless of risk or suitability; hard to rebalance |
| **Regret aversion** | Avoiding actions that could lead to regret about a poor outcome | Staying out after losses (too conservative); following the crowd, or holding a "safe" popular stock to avoid regret |

<Note>
Loss aversion and prospect theory are related but not identical. Prospect theory is the broader framework (reference points, diminishing sensitivity, loss aversion); loss aversion is the specific finding that a loss weighs roughly twice as much as an equal gain.
</Note>

## 6. Implications for Investors and Advisers

- **Diversification and risk**: overconfidence, confirmation, illusion of control, endowment and familiarity effects all tend to produce **concentrated** portfolios and excessive trading.
- **Trading behavior**: the disposition effect and overconfidence raise turnover and costs, and can reduce after-tax returns.
- **Planning**: self-control and status quo biases lead to under-saving and inertia. Automatic enrollment, scheduled contributions and periodic rebalancing rules work with these biases rather than against them.
- **Adviser approach**: for **cognitive** errors, **moderate** the bias through education and better information. For **emotional** biases, **adapt** the portfolio and process to the client's behavior, because the bias is less likely to change.
- **Self-awareness**: written investment policy statements, pre-set decision rules and checklists reduce reliance on in-the-moment judgment.

## 7. Behavioral Biases and Market Outcomes

If many investors share the same bias, the effect can show up in prices. Behavioral explanations for market features that traditional finance struggles with include:

- **Momentum**: winners keep winning and losers keep losing over intermediate horizons. Conservatism and underreaction (information is absorbed slowly), followed by herding and overreaction, are proposed explanations.
- **Bubbles and crashes**: prices rise far above fundamentals during a boom and then collapse. Overconfidence, confirmation, availability (recent gains are vivid), **herding** (copying others) and regret aversion can reinforce each other. Bubbles are usually recognized only in hindsight.
- **Value effect** (value stocks outperforming growth stocks over long horizons): representativeness may lead investors to extrapolate past growth too far, overpaying for "glamour" stocks and underpricing out-of-favor ones.
- **Disposition-driven price patterns**: widespread selling of winners and holding of losers can slow the incorporation of good news into prices.
- **Home bias** and **familiarity**: holding mostly domestic or employer stock, driven by familiarity and availability, reduces diversification.

Behavioral finance does not by itself prove markets are inefficient. Many anomalies are hard to exploit after costs and risk, and some may reflect compensation for risk or data mining.

## 8. Worked Examples

### Example A: Loss aversion changes a decision

An investor is offered a coin-flip gamble: win 120 or lose 100, each with probability 0.5. (The loss-aversion coefficient $\lambda = 2$ is an illustrative assumption: a loss is felt twice as strongly as an equal gain.)

1. Expected monetary value $= 0.5(120) + 0.5(-100) = 60 - 50 = +10$. A rational risk-neutral investor accepts.
2. Perceived value for the loss-averse investor $= 0.5(120) + 0.5(-\lambda \times 100) = 60 - 0.5(200) = 60 - 100 = \mathbf{-40}$.
3. The gamble feels negative, so the investor **declines** a bet with positive expected value.

For the investor to accept, the potential gain $G$ must satisfy $0.5\,G - 0.5(2 \times 100) \geq 0$, so $G \geq \mathbf{200}$. The gain must be twice the loss.

### Example B: Base-rate neglect (representativeness)

In an industry, 2% of firms go bankrupt within a year. An analyst's screen flags 90% of the firms that will go bankrupt, but also flags 10% of the firms that will not. A firm is flagged. What is the probability it goes bankrupt?

1. Flagged and will go bankrupt: $0.02 \times 0.90 = 0.018$
2. Flagged and will survive: $0.98 \times 0.10 = 0.098$
3. Total flagged $= 0.018 + 0.098 = 0.116$
4. $P(\text{bankrupt} \mid \text{flagged}) = 0.018 / 0.116 = 0.1552 = \mathbf{15.5\%}$

An investor guided by representativeness ("the screen is 90% accurate, so this firm is 90% likely to fail") ignores the 2% base rate and overstates the risk by a wide margin.

### Example C: Anchoring and conservatism

An analyst forecasts EPS of 2.00. New information implies a fair forecast of 2.60. The analyst revises to only 2.20.

1. Required adjustment $= 2.60 - 2.00 = 0.60$
2. Actual adjustment $= 2.20 - 2.00 = 0.20$
3. Share of the adjustment made $= 0.20 / 0.60 = \mathbf{33\%}$

The forecast is still **0.40 (about 15%)** too low relative to 2.60 (0.40 / 2.60 = 15.4%). The analyst's forecast stays too close to the anchor. If many analysts behave this way, prices may underreact to the news and then drift upward as later information confirms it.

## Key takeaways

- Traditional finance assumes rational, utility-maximizing investors and efficient markets; behavioral finance documents systematic departures from this.
- **Prospect theory**: outcomes are judged against a reference point; the value function is concave for gains, convex for losses and steeper for losses (**loss aversion**).
- **Cognitive errors** (faulty reasoning) are easier to correct with information and education; **emotional biases** (feelings) are harder to correct, so the plan is adapted to them.
- Belief perseverance: **conservatism, confirmation, representativeness, illusion of control, hindsight**. Information processing: **anchoring and adjustment, mental accounting, framing, availability**.
- Emotional biases: **loss aversion, overconfidence, self-control, status quo, endowment, regret aversion**.
- Typical portfolio effects are concentration, excess trading, the disposition effect, inertia and under-saving.
- Shared biases can help explain **momentum, bubbles and crashes, value effects and home bias**, though behavioral explanations do not by themselves prove markets are inefficient.
