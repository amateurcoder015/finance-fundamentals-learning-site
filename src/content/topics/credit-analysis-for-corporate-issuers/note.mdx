# Credit Analysis for Corporate Issuers

A corporate bond investor lends money and is paid a fixed, capped amount: coupons and principal. If the company does brilliantly, the bondholder gains nothing extra; if it fails, the bondholder takes the loss. **Credit analysis** therefore concentrates on the **downside**: can the issuer generate enough cash to service its debt on time through a bad year, and what will the creditor recover if it cannot?

The output of credit analysis is a judgment about the issuer's creditworthiness (often expressed as an internal or agency rating) and, ultimately, whether the **yield spread** offered compensates for the risk. Analysts use it to decide whether to buy, hold or sell, and to detect a deteriorating credit before the market or the rating agencies do.

---

## 1. Credit Analysis vs Equity Analysis

| | Credit analysis | Equity analysis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Investor's payoff | Capped (coupons + principal) | Uncapped upside |
| Main concern | Downside: probability of default and loss severity | Growth, earnings and valuation |
| Key cash flow | Cash available to service debt | Cash available to shareholders |
| Time horizon | Until the bond matures | Often open-ended |
| Typical focus | Leverage, coverage, liquidity, covenants, recovery | Margins, growth, return on capital, multiples |

Both start from the same financial statements, but the creditor asks "how bad can it get and will I still be paid?", whereas the shareholder asks "how good can it get?".

Credit analysis is also done at two levels:

- **Issuer (corporate family) level**: the overall ability of the company to meet its debt obligations.
- **Issue level**: how a specific bond ranks in the capital structure (seniority, security, guarantees), which determines recovery and can make one issue's rating differ from another's by the same issuer.

---

## 2. The Four Cs of Credit Analysis

The traditional framework has four elements. Capacity is analysed in the most depth; the other three refine the picture.

| C | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| **Capacity** | Can the borrower generate enough cash flow to make the debt payments on time? |
| **Collateral** | What assets stand behind the debt, and what are they worth if the borrower fails? |
| **Covenants** | What contractual promises protect creditors and restrict the borrower? |
| **Character** | How trustworthy and capable are management, and how do they behave toward creditors? |

### 2.1 Capacity

Capacity is the ability to service debt from operations. It has three layers.

**Industry structure.** Porter's five forces are a common framework:
1. Threat of entry (barriers to entry protect incumbents' margins).
2. Power of suppliers.
3. Power of buyers/customers.
4. Threat of substitutes.
5. Intensity of rivalry among existing competitors.

**Industry fundamentals.**
- **Cyclicality**: cyclical industries (autos, steel, construction) see cash flow collapse in downturns, so they need lower leverage than stable ones (utilities, consumer staples, healthcare) for the same rating.
- **Growth prospects**: mature, declining or disrupted industries are riskier than growing ones.
- **Published industry statistics**: used to benchmark margins, leverage and cost structure.

**Company fundamentals.**
- Competitive position and market share.
- Operating efficiency, cost structure and operating leverage.
- Management's strategy and track record of execution.
- Quality of earnings and consistency of cash flow through the cycle.

Analysts compare the company with peers using the same ratios and consider several scenarios (base case and stress case), because the question is whether debt can be serviced when conditions are poor.

### 2.2 Collateral

Collateral is the quality and value of assets that support the debt. It matters most for **secured** debt and for the recovery analysis if default occurs.

- A **secured** bond has a claim on specific assets; an **unsecured** bond relies on the general credit of the issuer.
- Tangible assets that can be sold (property, plant, receivables) provide more reliable cover than intangibles such as goodwill, brands and software, whose value tends to fall precisely when the company is in trouble.
- Analysts examine asset **quality**: whether the book value would be realised in a forced sale, and whether the assets are already pledged to other creditors.
- The value of collateral also depends on whether it is held at the parent or in subsidiaries (see structural subordination below).

### 2.3 Covenants

**Covenants** are contractual terms in the bond indenture that protect creditors by obliging or restricting the issuer. A breach can trigger a default or force renegotiation.

| Type | What it does | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Affirmative (positive)** | Things the issuer **must** do | Pay interest and principal on time; maintain insurance; keep assets in good repair; deliver financial statements; comply with laws; pay taxes |
| **Negative** | Things the issuer **must not** do (or may do only within limits) | Limit additional debt; limit dividends and share repurchases; restrict asset sales; restrict liens on assets (**negative pledge**); restrict mergers and transactions with affiliates |

Other covenant concepts:

- **Maintenance covenants** are tested regularly (e.g. net debt/EBITDA must stay below a stated level every quarter); **incurrence covenants** are tested only when the issuer takes a specified action, such as issuing new debt. Maintenance tests give creditors an earlier warning.
- **Cross-default** provisions mean that a default on one debt obligation can trigger default on others.
- **Pari passu** clauses promise equal ranking with other senior unsecured debt.
- Tighter covenants protect creditors, so a bond with strong covenants can justify a lower spread than an otherwise identical "covenant-lite" one. Covenants are typically stronger in high-yield bonds than in investment-grade bonds.

### 2.4 Character

Character is the quality of management and governance, and how the company treats creditors.

- Management's **strategy**, its record of delivering plans, and its appetite for risk (acquisitions, aggressive leverage, shareholder payouts).
- **Track record** with creditors: prior restructurings, covenant breaches, or fair treatment in distress.
- **Corporate governance** and the independence of the board; the influence of a controlling owner (a private equity sponsor may load a company with debt to fund payouts).
- **Quality of financial reporting**: aggressive accounting choices, frequent restatements or opaque disclosures signal higher risk.
- Use of off-balance-sheet structures, related-party transactions and the extent of reliance on adjusted ("non-GAAP") measures.

## 3. Financial Ratios Used in Credit Analysis

Analysts adjust reported figures first (for example capitalising operating leases, treating underfunded pensions as debt-like, and removing one-off items) and then compute ratios from three groups.

### 3.1 Key cash-flow measures

| Measure | Calculation |
| --- | --- |
| **EBITDA** | Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (= EBIT + D&A) |
| **Funds from operations (FFO)** | Net income + depreciation, amortization, deferred taxes and other non-cash items |
| **Cash flow from operations (CFO)** | FFO adjusted for investment in working capital |
| **Free operating cash flow (FOCF)** | CFO minus capital expenditures |
| **Discretionary cash flow (DCF)** | FOCF minus dividends |
| **Retained cash flow (RCF)** | FFO minus dividends |
| **Net debt** | Total debt minus cash and equivalents (and sometimes liquid securities) |

EBITDA is popular because it is a quick proxy for operating cash flow and is not affected by capital structure, tax or depreciation policy. Its weakness is that it **ignores capital expenditure, working-capital needs and taxes**, which a company must pay for out of real cash. FFO and FOCF are less manipulable and closer to the cash actually available to repay debt.

### 3.2 Leverage ratios

$$\text{Debt/EBITDA} = \frac{\text{Total debt}}{\text{EBITDA}} \qquad \text{Net debt/EBITDA} = \frac{\text{Debt} - \text{Cash}}{\text{EBITDA}}$$

$$\text{FFO/Debt} = \frac{\text{FFO}}{\text{Total debt}} \qquad \text{FOCF/Debt} = \frac{\text{FOCF}}{\text{Total debt}}$$

$$\text{RCF/Net debt} = \frac{\text{FFO} - \text{Dividends}}{\text{Debt} - \text{Cash}} \qquad \text{Debt/Capital} = \frac{\text{Debt}}{\text{Debt} + \text{Equity}}$$

Higher debt/EBITDA and debt/capital indicate more risk; higher FFO/debt, FOCF/debt and RCF/net debt indicate more capacity to repay.

### 3.3 Coverage ratios

$$\text{EBITDA/Interest} = \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{Interest expense}} \qquad \text{EBIT/Interest} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest expense}}$$

EBIT/interest is stricter than EBITDA/interest because it deducts depreciation, a proxy for the capital spending that must continue. Coverage ratios show how much operating earnings can fall before interest is no longer covered.

### 3.4 Worked example

A manufacturer reports (millions):

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| EBIT | 220 |
| Depreciation and amortization | 150 |
| Interest expense | 60 |
| Tax rate | 25% |
| Total debt | 1,110 |
| Cash | 110 |
| Equity (book) | 1,665 |
| Increase in working capital | 20 |
| Capital expenditures | 140 |
| Dividends | 50 |

**Step 1: earnings.**
Pre-tax income $= 220 - 60 = 160$; tax $= 25\% \times 160 = 40$; net income $= 160 - 40 = 120$.

**Step 2: cash-flow measures.**
- EBITDA $= 220 + 150 = 370$
- FFO $= 120 + 150 = 270$ (no other non-cash items)
- CFO $= 270 - 20 = 250$
- FOCF $= 250 - 140 = 110$
- DCF $= 110 - 50 = 60$
- RCF $= 270 - 50 = 220$
- Net debt $= 1{,}110 - 110 = 1{,}000$

**Step 3: ratios.**

| Ratio | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Debt/EBITDA | $1{,}110 / 370$ | 3.00x |
| Net debt/EBITDA | $1{,}000 / 370$ | 2.70x |
| EBITDA/Interest | $370 / 60$ | 6.17x |
| EBIT/Interest | $220 / 60$ | 3.67x |
| FFO/Debt | $270 / 1{,}110$ | 24.3% |
| FOCF/Debt | $110 / 1{,}110$ | 9.9% |
| RCF/Net debt | $220 / 1{,}000$ | 22.0% |
| Debt/Capital | $1{,}110 / (1{,}110 + 1{,}665)$ | 40.0% |

**Interpretation.** Leverage of about 3x EBITDA with EBIT covering interest 3.7 times is moderate. FOCF is positive and covers dividends (DCF = 60), so the company is self-funding. The analyst would then test a downturn: if EBITDA fell 30% to 259, debt/EBITDA would rise to $1{,}110/259 = 4.29$x, and EBITDA/interest would fall to $259/60 = 4.32$x, which shows how cyclical the credit is.

## 4. Capital Structure, Priority of Claims and Structural Subordination

Within a company, claims rank by seniority: secured debt, then senior unsecured, subordinated, and finally equity. Recovery rates fall as you move down the ranking, which is why issue ratings are **notched** up or down from the issuer rating (a senior secured bond is often rated one or two notches above the corporate family rating, while a subordinated bond is rated below it).

**Structural subordination** arises in a **holding company** structure:

- The parent (holdco) issues debt, but the operating assets and cash flow sit in subsidiaries (opcos).
- Subsidiary creditors have a direct claim on the subsidiary's assets. The holdco's creditors only have a claim on the **residual equity value** of the subsidiaries after those creditors are paid.
- So holdco debt is effectively **subordinated** to opco debt even if both are "senior unsecured", and holdco bonds usually have lower recovery and a wider spread.
- The holdco also depends on dividends and loan repayments from subsidiaries, which can be restricted by local law or by the subsidiaries' own covenants.

Guarantees from subsidiaries to holdco debt can remove structural subordination.

## 5. Investment-Grade vs High-Yield Credit Analysis

| | Investment grade | High yield (below BBB-/Baa3) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main risk | Spread widening and ratings migration | Default and loss given default |
| Analytical emphasis | Capacity from cash flow; industry and ratio benchmarking | Liquidity, covenants, capital structure, recovery under default |
| Typical covenants | Light | More restrictive (debt incurrence, restricted payments) |
| Behaviour | Spreads driven by rates and credit-cycle sentiment | Behaves more like equity: more sensitive to the economy and the issuer's results |

Special considerations for high-yield issuers:

1. **Liquidity** is critical. Sources are cash, working capital, operating cash flow, bank credit lines and asset sales; uses are working capital, capex, debt maturities and dividends. Analysts check that liquidity covers near-term debt maturities (the **refinancing risk** wall).
2. **Structure** matters: multiple layers of debt, holdco vs opco, and the position of each issue.
3. **Covenants** are scrutinised: the analyst reads the indenture for limits on additional debt, restricted payments and change-of-control (put) protections.
4. **Recovery analysis**: estimate the value of the company or assets in default, subtract senior claims, and calculate the share left for each class. Possible approaches include liquidation value and going-concern (enterprise value) valuation.
5. **Equity sponsor behaviour**: leveraged buyouts raise debt dramatically; sponsor intentions (dividend recaps, asset stripping) are part of character.
6. Because high-yield issues are less liquid and defaults are not rare, **loss severity** matters at least as much as default probability.

## 6. Beyond the Ratios: Qualitative Checks

- **Ratings are a starting point.** Agencies rate through the cycle, so ratings lag and miss fast-moving events; analysts supplement them with their own views and market signals (spreads, equity prices, CDS).
- **Compare spreads with analysis.** If a bond's spread is wider than its credit risk warrants, it may be undervalued, and the reverse holds when spreads are tight.
- **Watch triggers**: ratings triggers in contracts, change-of-control events, large acquisitions, falling interest coverage or missed covenant tests.

---

## Key takeaways

- Creditors have capped upside, so credit analysis focuses on the **downside**: probability of default and loss severity.
- The **four Cs**: **capacity** (ability to pay from cash flow, judged by industry structure and company fundamentals), **collateral** (asset quality and value), **covenants** (affirmative: must do; negative: must not do), **character** (management quality, governance, record with creditors).
- Cash-flow measures: EBITDA = EBIT + D&A; FFO = NI + non-cash items; FOCF = CFO − capex; DCF = FOCF − dividends; RCF = FFO − dividends.
- Leverage ratios (debt/EBITDA, net debt/EBITDA, FFO/debt, FOCF/debt, RCF/net debt, debt/capital) and coverage ratios (EBITDA/interest, EBIT/interest) are compared against peers and stressed in downturn scenarios. EBITDA ignores capex and working capital.
- Cyclical industries need lower leverage than stable ones for the same credit quality.
- **Structural subordination**: holdco creditors rank behind subsidiary creditors; guarantees can remove it. Issue ratings are **notched** for seniority and security.
- **High-yield analysis** puts more weight on liquidity, covenants, capital structure and recovery; investment-grade analysis is more about capacity and spread/migration risk.
