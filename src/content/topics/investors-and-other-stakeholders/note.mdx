# Investors and Other Stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is any person or group with an interest in, or a claim on, a company's performance and decisions. Stakeholders differ in what they want, how they are paid, how much they can influence management, and how much risk they bear, so their interests often conflict. Understanding those conflicts is the basis of corporate governance.

---

## 1. The Main Stakeholder Groups

| Group | Nature of claim | Main interest | Typical concern |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Shareholders** (equity investors) | **Residual claim**: paid after all other claims, in dividends and share price gains | Growth in the value of the shares and dividends | Value creation, returns above the cost of equity |
| **Creditors** (lenders and bondholders) | **Fixed claim**: interest and principal as contracted, paid before equity | Being repaid in full and on time | Default risk, asset quality, leverage |
| **Managers** | Salary, bonus, equity-based pay; run the firm day to day | Pay, job security, reputation, power and perquisites | Empire building, risk avoidance, short-term targets |
| **Board of directors** | Elected by shareholders to oversee management | Fulfil fiduciary duty, oversee strategy and risk | Independence, accountability, conflicts of interest |
| **Employees** | Wages, benefits, job security | Pay, working conditions, career prospects | Layoffs, restructuring, pension funding |
| **Customers** | Products, service, warranties | Quality, price, reliability, after-sales support | Product safety, continuity of supply |
| **Suppliers** | Payment for goods and services, often on credit | Prompt payment, long-term relationship | Customer's solvency, payment terms |
| **Governments and regulators** | Taxes, compliance, public policy | Tax revenue, legal compliance, employment, consumer and environmental protection | Reporting accuracy, regulatory breaches, social impact |
| **Community and society** | Indirect effects of operations | Environmental and social impact | Pollution, local employment |

### Shareholders and creditors: the key contrast

- **Shareholders** own the residual: they receive whatever is left after every other claim is paid. Their upside is unlimited, but their loss is limited to the amount invested (**limited liability**). They normally hold **voting rights** and elect the board.
- **Creditors** receive a contracted amount and nothing more, however well the firm does. They have **priority** in bankruptcy and usually **no vote**, so they protect themselves through contract terms.

**Worked example: priority of claims.** A firm with senior secured debt of 60 and unsecured debt of 40 is liquidated.

| Liquidation proceeds | Secured (60) | Unsecured (40) | Equity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **120** | 60 (100%) | 40 (100%) | **20** |
| **80** | 60 (100%) | 20 (**50%** recovery) | **0** |
| **50** | 50 (83.3%) | 0 | 0 |

Equity absorbs losses first and gets nothing unless creditors are repaid in full. This is why debt is cheaper than equity (lower risk, lower required return) and why creditors care about downside protection while shareholders care about upside.

## 2. Conflicts of Interest

### Shareholders versus managers: the agency problem

When owners (the **principals**) hire managers (the **agents**) to run the firm, the agents may not act purely in the principals' interest. This is the **principal-agent (agency) problem**. Typical symptoms:

- **Empire building:** pursuing size, acquisitions or negative-NPV projects, because pay, status and power grow with firm size.
- **Perquisites:** excessive private benefits (aircraft, offices, spending of free cash flow).
- **Excess caution or excess risk:** managers whose wealth and job depend on the firm may avoid positive-NPV but risky projects; or, with option-based pay that has no downside, may take too much risk.
- **Short-termism:** boosting current earnings or the share price at the expense of long-run value.
- **Information asymmetry:** managers know more about the firm than owners do, so their actions are hard to observe.

**Agency costs** (Jensen and Meckling) have three parts:

1. **Monitoring costs:** audits, board oversight, reporting, rating reviews.
2. **Bonding costs:** costs the agent incurs to assure the principal (for example, contracts, insurance, restrictions on own behaviour).
3. **Residual loss:** value still lost because interests are not perfectly aligned even after monitoring and bonding.

### Shareholders versus creditors

Because equity is a limited-liability residual claim, shareholders gain from risk (large upside, capped downside) while creditors suffer from it (fixed upside, large downside). Typical conflicts:

- **Asset substitution / risk shifting:** swapping safe projects for riskier ones after debt is issued.
- **Excess leverage:** taking on more, or more senior, debt after the original loans were made.
- **Excessive payouts:** large dividends or buybacks that drain assets before creditors are repaid.

**Worked example: risk shifting.** A firm has debt of 80 due in one year. Its assets are worth 100.

| | Safe project | Risky project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asset value at year end | 100 for certain | 150 or 50, each 50% likely |
| Expected asset value | 100 | $0.5(150) + 0.5(50) = 100$ |
| Debt payoff | 80 | $150$: 80; $50$: 50 (only 50 available) |
| Expected debt value | **80** | $0.5(80) + 0.5(50) = $ **65** |
| Equity payoff | $100 - 80 = 20$ | $150$: $70$; $50$: $0$ (limited liability) |
| Expected equity value | **20** | $0.5(70) + 0.5(0) = $ **35** |

Both projects have the same expected asset value of 100, so there is no value created. Switching to the risky project moves **15** of value from creditors (80 down to 65) to shareholders (20 up to 35). Creditors anticipate this behaviour and respond with covenants and higher required yields.

### Conflicts among shareholders

Shareholders are not a single group:

- **Controlling versus minority shareholders:** a controlling holder can steer the board, set pay, and approve related-party transactions that favour itself (for example, buying inputs from its own affiliate at above-market prices, sometimes called **tunneling**), at the minority's cost.
- **Dual-class structures** separate voting power from economic ownership.

**Worked example: voting versus economic ownership.** A company has 10 million shares: 1 million **Class B** (10 votes each, held by the founder) and 9 million **Class A** (1 vote each, held by the public).

- Founder's economic ownership $= 1/10 = 10\%$
- Founder's votes $= 1\text{m} \times 10 = 10\text{m}$; public votes $= 9\text{m} \times 1 = 9\text{m}$; total votes $= 19\text{m}$
- Founder's voting share $= 10/19 = 52.6\%$

The founder controls the company with only 10% of the cash flow rights, so bears 10% of the cost of a poor decision but can approve it alone. Other conflicts: **short-term investors** (trading or activist funds) versus **long-term holders**; and **investor types** with different horizons, tax positions or dividend preferences.

### Other conflicts

- **Managers/shareholders versus employees:** cost cutting and layoffs raise profit but harm employees.
- **Shareholders versus customers or the public:** cutting product quality or safety, or environmental spending, to lift profit.
- **Shareholders versus suppliers:** stretching payables.
- **Governments versus the firm:** aggressive tax planning or evasion of regulation.
- **Directors:** board members with personal ties to management may not oversee it effectively.

## 3. Shareholder Theory versus Stakeholder Theory

| | **Shareholder theory** | **Stakeholder theory** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Objective** | Maximize shareholder value, within the law and ethical norms | Balance the interests of all stakeholders; shareholders are one group among several |
| **Reasoning** | Shareholders hold the residual claim, so maximizing value for them also maximizes the value of the whole firm; other parties are protected by contract and law | Long-run value depends on cooperation from employees, customers, suppliers and society; ignoring them destroys value |
| **Weakness** | Risks short-termism and neglecting costs imposed on others | Harder to define, measure and hold managers accountable, since goals compete |

In practice the two overlap. Treating employees, customers and suppliers well usually supports long-run shareholder value, and the sustainability and ESG agenda reflects the stakeholder view.

## 4. Managing Stakeholder Relationships and Conflicts

**Stakeholder management** means identifying stakeholders, understanding what they want and how much influence they have, and balancing those interests in decision-making. The tools fall into four groups:

| Mechanism | Examples | Mainly addresses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Legal** | Company law and fiduciary duties of directors; securities regulation and disclosure; minority-shareholder protection; bankruptcy law; labour, consumer and environmental law; whistleblower protection | All stakeholders, especially minority holders and creditors |
| **Contractual** | **Debt covenants** (affirmative: maintain insurance, supply financial statements; negative: limit extra debt, dividends, asset sales); managerial **compensation contracts** linked to long-run performance; **clawbacks**; share-ownership requirements for executives; employment and supplier contracts | Shareholder-creditor and shareholder-manager conflicts |
| **Organizational** | An **independent board** and committees (audit, remuneration, nomination); internal audit and control; external auditors; separation of CEO and chair; shareholder voting rights, proxy contests and say-on-pay | Shareholder-manager conflicts, oversight of management |
| **Governmental / market** | Regulators and stock exchanges setting listing and reporting rules; rating agencies; analysts; the **market for corporate control** (takeover threat), **activist investors** | Information asymmetry and weak governance |

### Why compensation design matters

- **Bonus based only on current-year earnings** encourages short-termism.
- **Stock options** reward a rising share price but have no downside for the holder, so can encourage excessive risk (a risk-shifting problem in itself).
- Better designs combine **restricted stock**, multi-year performance targets, ownership requirements and clawbacks.

### Where creditors fit

Creditors' main defences are **covenants**, **collateral and seniority**, monitoring through credit analysis and ratings, and a **higher required yield** on riskier firms. Banks can monitor closely because they hold private information; bondholders mostly rely on covenants and ratings.

## Key takeaways

- Shareholders hold the **residual claim** with unlimited upside and limited liability; creditors hold **fixed, senior claims**. In liquidation, equity is paid only after all debt.
- The **agency problem** arises when managers (agents) pursue their own goals instead of the owners'. Agency costs = monitoring + bonding + residual loss.
- Shareholders gain from risk and creditors lose from it, so a switch to riskier projects transfers value from creditors to shareholders (in the example, 15).
- Controlling shareholders can extract private benefits from minority holders; dual-class shares can give 52.6% of votes for 10% of the economics.
- **Shareholder theory** focuses on shareholder value; **stakeholder theory** balances all groups.
- Conflicts are managed through **legal, contractual, organizational and governmental/market** mechanisms: covenants, independent boards, aligned pay, disclosure rules, auditors, activists and takeover threats.
