# Analyzing Statements of Cash Flows I

The **statement of cash flows** reports the cash a company **received and paid** during a period, grouped by the activity that caused it. The income statement uses accrual accounting, so profit can differ sharply from cash. The cash flow statement shows how the firm generated and used cash, and so whether earnings turn into cash.

It reconciles the opening and closing balance of **cash and cash equivalents**:

$$\text{Ending cash} = \text{Beginning cash} + CFO + CFI + CFF$$

Cash equivalents are highly liquid, short-term investments (original maturity of about three months or less) that are readily convertible to cash.

---

## 1. The Three Categories of Activities

| Category | What it covers | Typical inflows | Typical outflows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Operating (CFO)** | The main revenue-producing activities and other items not classified elsewhere | Cash from customers; interest and dividends received (U.S. GAAP) | Payments to suppliers and employees; taxes; interest paid (U.S. GAAP) |
| **Investing (CFI)** | Acquiring and disposing of long-term assets and non-trading investments | Sale of PP&E; sale of investment securities; loan repayments received | Purchase of PP&E (capital expenditure); purchase of securities; loans made |
| **Financing (CFF)** | Raising and returning capital to owners and lenders | Issuing debt or shares | Repaying debt; repurchasing shares; paying dividends |

Notes:

- Under **U.S. GAAP**, purchases and sales of **trading securities** are operating activities. Other securities purchases and sales are investing.
- A **gain or loss** on selling an asset is not itself a cash flow. The **full proceeds** are an investing inflow, and the gain or loss is removed from operating cash flow (indirect method).
- **Depreciation** is a non-cash expense. It lowers net income but not cash, so it is added back; it is not a "source of cash".

---

## 2. Non-Cash Investing and Financing Transactions

Transactions that **do not involve cash** are left out of the statement but must be **disclosed** (in a note or supplemental schedule), because they affect future cash flows. Examples:

- Acquiring an asset by issuing debt or shares (e.g., buying a building with a mortgage given to the seller).
- Converting bonds or preferred shares into common equity (a **debt-for-equity swap**).
- Exchanging one non-monetary asset for another.
- Obtaining a right-of-use asset by assuming a lease liability.

If a purchase is split into a cash part and a financed part, only the **cash part** appears in the statement.

---

## 3. IFRS versus U.S. GAAP Classification

U.S. GAAP is more prescriptive. IFRS gives choices for interest and dividends, provided the choice is applied consistently and disclosed.

| Item | IFRS | U.S. GAAP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Interest paid** | Operating **or** financing | Operating |
| **Interest received** | Operating **or** investing | Operating |
| **Dividends paid** | Financing **or** operating | Financing |
| **Dividends received** | Operating **or** investing | Operating |
| **Income taxes paid** | Operating, unless specifically tied to an investing or financing transaction | Operating |
| **Bank overdrafts** | May be included in cash and cash equivalents | Treated as financing |

Because of these choices, comparing CFO across firms requires **adjusting for classification**. A company that reports interest paid in financing shows a higher CFO than one that reports it in operating, with no change in true cash generation. Cash paid for interest and taxes must also be disclosed in the notes (supplemental information).

---

## 4. Direct and Indirect Methods for CFO

Both methods give the **same CFO**. They differ only in how it is presented. Investing and financing sections are identical under both.

### Direct method

Lists **major classes of gross cash receipts and payments**:

$$CFO = \text{Cash from customers} - \text{Cash to suppliers} - \text{Cash operating expenses} - \text{Interest paid} - \text{Taxes paid}$$

Useful relationships (derived from the changes in balance sheet accounts):

$$\text{Cash from customers} = \text{Revenue} - \Delta\text{Accounts receivable}$$

$$\text{Purchases} = \text{COGS} + \Delta\text{Inventory}$$

$$\text{Cash paid to suppliers} = \text{Purchases} - \Delta\text{Accounts payable}$$

$$\text{Cash operating expenses} = \text{Operating expenses} + \Delta\text{Prepaid expenses} - \Delta\text{Accrued liabilities}$$

Here $\Delta$ is the change over the period (end minus beginning); an increase in receivables means revenue was recognised but cash not yet collected.

### Indirect method

Starts from **net income** and adjusts to cash:

$$CFO = NI + \text{Non-cash charges} - \text{Non-cash gains} \pm \text{Changes in working capital}$$

| Adjustment | Sign |
| --- | --- |
| Depreciation, amortization, impairment, stock-based compensation, non-cash interest | **Add back** |
| Gains on sale of assets or debt retirement (non-operating) | **Subtract** |
| Losses on sale of assets | **Add back** |
| Increase in an operating **asset** (receivables, inventory) | **Subtract** (cash tied up) |
| Decrease in an operating asset | **Add** |
| Increase in an operating **liability** (payables, accruals) | **Add** (cash retained) |
| Decrease in an operating liability | **Subtract** |
| Increase in deferred tax liability | **Add** |

Most companies use the indirect method. Standard setters prefer the direct method because it shows the actual cash flows, but it is rarely used. Under U.S. GAAP a firm using the direct method must also provide a reconciliation of net income to CFO.

---

## 5. Deriving Investing and Financing Flows from the Balance Sheet

Capital expenditure and debt flows can be recovered from balance sheet changes.

$$\text{Ending net PP\&E} = \text{Beginning net PP\&E} + \text{Capex} - \text{Depreciation} - \text{Book value of assets sold}$$

$$\text{Proceeds from sale} = \text{Book value sold} + \text{Gain} \;(\text{or} - \text{Loss})$$

$$RE_{\text{end}} = RE_{\text{begin}} + NI - \text{Dividends declared}$$

$$\text{Net borrowing} = \text{Ending debt} - \text{Beginning debt}$$

## 6. Worked Example: A Full Cash Flow Statement

All figures are in millions. A company reports the following for the year.

**Income statement:** Revenue 1,600; COGS 900; operating expenses 230; depreciation 80; gain on sale of equipment 30; interest expense 20; income tax 150.

$$\text{Pre-tax income} = 1{,}600 - 900 - 230 - 80 + 30 - 20 = 400, \qquad NI = 400 - 150 = 250$$

**Balance sheet changes:** accounts receivable +40; inventory −20; accounts payable +30; accrued liabilities −10 (accrued operating expenses). Interest and taxes paid equal the expense.

### Step 1: CFO, indirect method

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Net income | 250 |
| Add: depreciation | 80 |
| Less: gain on sale of equipment | (30) |
| Increase in accounts receivable | (40) |
| Decrease in inventory | 20 |
| Increase in accounts payable | 30 |
| Decrease in accrued liabilities | (10) |
| **CFO** | **300** |

Check: $250 + 80 - 30 - 40 + 20 + 30 - 10 = 300$.

### Step 2: CFO, direct method (same answer)

1. Cash from customers $= 1{,}600 - 40 = 1{,}560$
2. Purchases $= 900 + (-20) = 880$; cash paid to suppliers $= 880 - 30 = 850$
3. Cash operating expenses $= 230 + 10 = 240$ (accruals fell, so more cash was paid than expensed)
4. Interest paid $= 20$; taxes paid $= 150$

$$CFO = 1{,}560 - 850 - 240 - 20 - 150 = 300$$

Both methods agree.

### Step 3: Investing

- Net PP&E rose from 800 to 870, depreciation was 80, and the equipment sold had a book value of 50. Capex: $870 = 800 + \text{Capex} - 80 - 50$, so $\text{Capex} = 200$.
- Proceeds from the sale $= 50 + 30 = 80$.
- Purchase of investment securities (non-trading): 50.

$$CFI = +80 - 200 - 50 = -170$$

### Step 4: Financing

Long-term debt issued 100; debt repaid 60; dividends paid 90; shares repurchased 40.

$$CFF = +100 - 60 - 90 - 40 = -90$$

### Step 5: Reconcile cash

$$\Delta\text{Cash} = 300 - 170 - 90 = +40$$

With beginning cash of 120, ending cash is 160. A company with these figures funds all its investing and financing needs from operations, and cash still rises.

### Effect of an IFRS choice

If the company reported under IFRS and classified the 20 of interest paid in **financing**, CFO would be 320 and CFF would be −110. Total cash change is still +40; only the classification moves.

---

## Key takeaways

- The statement reconciles cash and equivalents through CFO, CFI and CFF; non-cash transactions are disclosed separately, not reported.
- **U.S. GAAP**: interest paid and received, dividends received and taxes are operating; dividends paid are financing. **IFRS** allows interest and dividends to be classified in more than one section.
- Direct and indirect methods give **identical CFO**; the indirect method starts at net income, adds back non-cash charges, removes gains (and adds back losses), and adjusts for working capital changes.
- Rising receivables or inventory **reduce** CFO; rising payables or accruals **increase** it.
- A gain on sale is deducted from CFO because the **full proceeds** appear in investing.
- Capex can be derived: $\text{Capex} = \Delta\text{Net PP\&E} + \text{Depreciation} + \text{Book value sold}$.
- When comparing companies, adjust for different classification choices.
