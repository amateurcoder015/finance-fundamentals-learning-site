# International Trade

International trade is the exchange of goods and services across national borders. It shapes a country's output, prices, employment and returns to different factors of production, and it drives exchange rates, corporate earnings and sector valuations. This chapter explains why countries trade, who gains and loses, how governments restrict trade, and how countries integrate into trading blocs.

---

## 1. Autarky, Free Trade and the Gains from Trade

- **Autarky** is a state of no international trade: a country consumes only what it produces, and prices are set entirely by domestic supply and demand.
- **Free trade** is trade with no restrictions. Domestic prices converge to the **world price**.

A country that opens to trade becomes an **exporter** of goods whose world price is above its autarky price, and an **importer** of goods whose world price is below it.

| | Exporting industry | Importing industry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domestic price | Rises to world price | Falls to world price |
| Domestic producers | Gain (producer surplus rises) | Lose |
| Domestic consumers | Lose | Gain (consumer surplus rises) |
| Net national welfare | **Gain** (winners' gain exceeds losers' loss) | **Gain** |

Trade produces a net gain because the winners could in principle compensate the losers and still be better off. In practice, compensation is not automatic, which is why trade creates political pressure for protection. Gains also come from specialisation, economies of scale, greater competition, a wider variety of goods and a larger market for firms.

## 2. Absolute and Comparative Advantage

- A country has an **absolute advantage** in a good if it can produce it using fewer resources (or more output per unit of resources) than another country.
- A country has a **comparative advantage** in a good if its **opportunity cost** of producing it is lower than that of another country.

Opportunity cost is what must be given up of another good. If a country needs $h_1$ hours to produce one unit of good 1 and $h_2$ hours for one unit of good 2:

$$\text{Opp. cost of good 1 (in units of good 2)} = \frac{h_1}{h_2}$$

<Note>
Trade is driven by **comparative**, not absolute, advantage. A country with an absolute advantage in every good still gains by specialising where its opportunity cost is lowest. Two countries cannot both have the lower opportunity cost in the same good.
</Note>

### Worked example: gains from specialisation

Each country has 120 labour hours. Hours needed per unit:

| | Wheat (tonne) | Cloth (bolt) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Country A | 2 | 6 |
| Country B | 4 | 8 |

1. **Absolute advantage:** A needs fewer hours for both goods, so A has an absolute advantage in both.
2. **Opportunity costs:**
   - A: 1 wheat $= 2/6 = 0.333$ cloth; 1 cloth $= 6/2 = 3$ wheat.
   - B: 1 wheat $= 4/8 = 0.5$ cloth; 1 cloth $= 8/4 = 2$ wheat.
3. **Comparative advantage:** A has the lower opportunity cost of wheat (0.333 < 0.5), so A specialises in wheat. B has the lower opportunity cost of cloth (2 < 3), so B specialises in cloth.
4. **Autarky output** (each splits hours 60/60): A makes $60/2 = 30$ wheat and $60/6 = 10$ cloth. B makes $60/4 = 15$ wheat and $60/8 = 7.5$ cloth.
5. **Specialised output:** A makes $120/2 = 60$ wheat. B makes $120/8 = 15$ cloth.
6. **Terms of trade** must lie between the two opportunity costs: 1 cloth is worth between 2 and 3 wheat. Suppose 1 cloth $=$ 2.5 wheat. A sells 25 wheat for 10 cloth.

| | Wheat | Cloth | Value in wheat at own opportunity cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A, autarky | 30 | 10 | $30 + 10\times3 = 60$ |
| A, after trade | $60-25 = 35$ | 10 | $35 + 10\times3 = 65$ |
| B, autarky | 15 | 7.5 | $15 + 7.5\times2 = 30$ |
| B, after trade | 25 | $15-10 = 5$ | $25 + 5\times2 = 35$ |

Both countries end up with consumption worth more (A: +5 wheat-equivalents, B: +5) than they could achieve alone. World output rises from 45 wheat and 17.5 cloth to 60 wheat and 15 cloth: 15 more wheat for 2.5 less cloth, and since a bolt of cloth is worth at most 3 wheat, the extra wheat more than covers the lost cloth.

## 3. Models of Comparative Advantage

| Model | Source of comparative advantage | Key idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ricardian** | Differences in **technology** or labour productivity | One factor of production (labour); countries export goods where their relative productivity is highest |
| **Heckscher-Ohlin** | Differences in **factor endowments** | A country exports goods that intensively use its **relatively abundant** factor and imports goods that use its scarce factor |

- Under Heckscher-Ohlin, a capital-abundant country exports capital-intensive goods, and a labour-abundant country exports labour-intensive goods.
- Trade tends to raise the return to the **abundant** factor and lower the return to the **scarce** factor in each country. This is why owners of scarce factors often lobby for protection.
- Trade is also driven by **economies of scale** and **product differentiation**, which explain trade between similar countries in similar industries.

### Production possibility frontier

A country's **production possibility frontier (PPF)** shows the maximum combinations of two goods it can produce with its resources. Under autarky it consumes on or inside its PPF. With trade, it specialises along its PPF and then exchanges at the terms of trade, so consumption can lie **outside** the PPF.

## 4. Terms of Trade

The **terms of trade** are the ratio of a country's export prices to its import prices:

$$\text{Terms of trade} = \frac{\text{Index of export prices}}{\text{Index of import prices}}\times100$$

- A rise means the country gets more imports for each unit of exports (an **improvement**).
- A fall is a **deterioration**.

Example: export price index 110 and import price index 125 give $110/125\times100 = \mathbf{88.0}$. The country must now sell more exports to buy the same imports.

## 5. Trade Restrictions

Governments restrict trade through:

- **Tariffs**: taxes on imports (or, less often, exports).
- **Quotas**: limits on the quantity that may be imported.
- **Voluntary export restraints (VERs)**: the exporting country agrees to limit its own exports.
- **Export subsidies**: payments to domestic exporters, which lower their price abroad.
- **Other barriers**: local-content rules, licensing, health and safety standards and other non-tariff measures.

### Common justifications

- Protecting **infant industries** until they can compete.
- **National security** and strategic industries.
- Protecting domestic **employment** and income.
- Countering **dumping** (selling abroad below cost or below the home price) and subsidised competitors.
- Raising government **revenue** (tariffs).
- Retaliation and bargaining leverage.

Most economists argue that protection is a costly way to achieve these goals, because it creates deadweight loss and invites retaliation.

### Effects of a tariff for a small country

A small country is a **price taker** in the world market. A tariff raises the domestic price by the amount of the tariff.

Worked example: the world price is 50 per unit, and the country imposes a 20% tariff, so the domestic price rises to 60.

| | At price 50 | At price 60 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domestic quantity demanded | 100 | 80 |
| Domestic quantity supplied | 40 | 55 |
| Imports | 60 | 25 |

1. **Loss in consumer surplus** $=$ rectangle $+$ triangle $= (60-50)\times80 + \tfrac12(60-50)(100-80) = 800 + 100 = \mathbf{900}$.
2. **Gain in producer surplus** $= (60-50)\times40 + \tfrac12(60-50)(55-40) = 400 + 75 = \mathbf{475}$.
3. **Government tariff revenue** $= (60-50)\times25 = \mathbf{250}$.
4. **Deadweight loss** $= \tfrac12(10)(55-40) + \tfrac12(10)(100-80) = 75 + 100 = \mathbf{175}$.
5. **Check:** $475 + 250 - 900 = -175$.

The deadweight loss has two parts: inefficient extra domestic production (75) and lost consumption (100). Consumers lose, producers and government gain, and the country loses overall.

<Note>
A **large** country that imports a lot can lower the world price by restricting imports, which improves its terms of trade. This can offset part of the deadweight loss, but it is a beggar-thy-neighbour gain and invites retaliation.
</Note>

### Quotas and VERs

An import quota that holds imports to 25 units gives the same domestic price (60) and the same changes in consumer and producer surplus as the tariff. The difference is the 250 area, called the **quota rent**:

- If the government **auctions** the licences, it captures the rent, as with a tariff.
- If licences are given to domestic importers, they capture it.
- If licences go to foreign exporters, or under a **VER**, the rent goes abroad. The country's welfare loss is then $175 + 250 = \mathbf{425}$, worse than the tariff.

### Export subsidies

An export subsidy raises the price received by domestic exporters above the world price. Domestic producers gain, domestic consumers lose (the domestic price rises), and the government bears the cost. The country suffers a net welfare loss. If the country is a large exporter, it also pushes the world price down, worsening its terms of trade and benefiting foreign consumers.

## 6. Trading Blocs and Economic Integration

Countries form regional agreements that lower trade barriers among members. The levels of integration, from least to most:

| Form | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Free trade area (FTA)** | No tariffs or quotas on trade among members; each member sets its own external tariffs |
| **Customs union** | FTA plus a **common external tariff** |
| **Common market** | Customs union plus free movement of **labour and capital** |
| **Economic union** | Common market plus harmonised economic policies and institutions |
| **Monetary union** | Economic union plus a **common currency** and a single monetary authority |

### Trade creation and trade diversion

- **Trade creation:** members replace higher-cost domestic production with lower-cost imports from other members. This raises welfare.
- **Trade diversion:** members replace lower-cost imports from non-members with higher-cost imports from members, because non-members face the external tariff. This reduces welfare.

The net effect of a bloc depends on which dominates. Blocs also bring benefits beyond trade, such as political cooperation and larger markets, and costs such as loss of national policy independence. The **World Trade Organization (WTO)** promotes multilateral liberalisation through non-discrimination, transparency and dispute settlement.

---

## Key takeaways

- Free trade makes a country better off overall, but creates winners and losers: exporters and consumers of imports gain; import-competing producers and owners of scarce factors lose.
- Trade follows **comparative advantage** (lowest opportunity cost), not absolute advantage. Terms of trade lie between the two countries' opportunity costs.
- **Ricardian** model: differences in technology. **Heckscher-Ohlin**: differences in factor endowments.
- Terms of trade $=$ export price index $/$ import price index $\times100$.
- A tariff in a small country raises domestic price, lowers consumer surplus, raises producer surplus and government revenue, and causes deadweight loss. Quotas give the same price effect but transfer the rent to licence holders (foreigners under a VER).
- Integration runs from FTA, to customs union, common market, economic union and monetary union. Blocs can create or divert trade.
