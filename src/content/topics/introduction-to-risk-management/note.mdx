# Introduction to Risk Management

**Risk** is exposure to uncertainty about outcomes. **Risk management** is the process of identifying the level of risk an organization wants, measuring the level it actually has, and taking actions to close the gap. The goal is **not** to minimize risk: it is to take the *right* amount of risk, the amount that is consistent with the organization's objectives and ability to bear losses.

This applies to any entity: a bank, an asset manager, a corporation, a pension fund or an individual household.

---

## 1. The Risk Management Process

Risk management is a continuous, repeating process rather than a one-time exercise:

1. **Set the risk governance and strategy**: decide who is responsible for risk, and what the organization's goals and constraints are.
2. **Identify risks**: list every exposure, including financial and non-financial ones.
3. **Measure risks**: quantify the size and likelihood of potential losses.
4. **Decide on the desired level** of each risk: this is set by **risk tolerance** and **risk budgeting**.
5. **Modify risks** to move the actual exposure toward the desired exposure (prevent, accept, transfer, shift).
6. **Monitor and communicate**: track results, report to the board and stakeholders, and adjust as conditions change.

<Note>Risk management is a **process**, not a department. Its job is to give decision makers the information and tools to take risk deliberately, not to remove it.</Note>

---

## 2. Risk Governance and the Risk Management Framework

**Risk governance** is the top-level structure that sets risk-taking and risk-oversight policy for the whole organization. Good governance:

- Aligns risk-taking with the organization's **strategic objectives**.
- Defines **roles and accountability**: the board approves the risk appetite; senior management implements it; a **chief risk officer (CRO)** typically leads the risk function.
- Creates **independent risk oversight** so that those who take risk are not the only ones who measure it.
- Promotes a **risk culture** in which employees understand the risks they run.

A **risk management framework** is the infrastructure that puts governance into practice. It includes:

| Element | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Risk governance structure | Who sets policy and who is accountable |
| Risk identification and measurement | Systems and models to find and quantify exposures |
| Risk infrastructure | People, data, technology and processes to monitor risk |
| Policies and processes | Written limits, escalation rules and reporting lines |
| Risk mitigation and management | Tools to modify risk (Section 6) |
| Communication | Regular reporting to the board and management |
| Strategic risk analysis | Integrating risk into planning, capital allocation and performance review |

### Enterprise risk management (ERM)

**Enterprise risk management** takes a **firm-wide (holistic)** view. Instead of each business unit managing its own risks in a silo, ERM aggregates risks across the organization, recognizes how they interact (they may offset or reinforce each other), and manages the total. The result is a clearer view of the organization's overall risk exposure.

## 3. Risk Tolerance and Risk Budgeting

### Risk tolerance

**Risk tolerance** is the level of risk an organization is **willing and able** to take. It is set by weighing two things:

- **Willingness** to take risk (a function of objectives, culture and preferences).
- **Ability** to take risk (a function of capital, income, liquidity and time horizon).

When willingness and ability conflict, the **lower** of the two should generally govern. A firm that is eager to take risk but has thin capital cannot responsibly do so.

Setting risk tolerance requires looking at the whole organization: its **financial position**, the **cash-flow variability** of the business, the cost of **financial distress**, and the expectations of its stakeholders.

### Risk budgeting

**Risk budgeting** is the process of **allocating the total risk the organization is willing to take** among its business units, portfolios or strategies, for example by assigning each a limit on tracking error, VaR or standard deviation.

- It forces trade-offs: risk is a scarce resource to be spent where the expected return per unit of risk is highest.
- The budget can be expressed in different **risk measures** (standard deviation, VaR, beta, etc.) and may be allocated top-down (from total risk to units) or built bottom-up.
- It helps align risk-taking with return expectations and prevents any single unit from consuming a disproportionate share of risk capacity.

## 4. Financial and Non-Financial Risks

Risks fall into two broad groups.

### Financial risks

Losses arising from **exposure to financial markets or counterparties**:

| Risk | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Market risk** | Losses from changes in interest rates, exchange rates, equity prices or commodity prices |
| **Credit risk** | Loss from a counterparty failing to meet its obligations (default) or from deterioration in its credit quality |
| **Liquidity risk** | Loss from being unable to buy or sell an asset quickly without significantly moving its price, or from being unable to meet cash needs |

### Non-financial risks

Risks arising from **operations, people, the legal setting or the external environment**:

| Risk | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Operational risk** | Loss from failed internal processes, people, systems or external events (fraud, error, outages) |
| **Settlement (Herstatt) risk** | One side of a trade is delivered but the counterparty fails to deliver its side |
| **Model risk** | Loss from using a flawed or misapplied valuation or risk model |
| **Legal and compliance risk** | Loss from breaching laws, regulations or contracts |
| **Regulatory risk** | Loss from changes in law or regulation |
| **Tax risk** | Loss from changes in tax rules or their interpretation |
| **Accounting risk** | Loss from misapplication or misinterpretation of accounting rules |
| **Sovereign risk** | Risk that a government fails to honor its obligations |
| **Political / geopolitical risk** | Risk from political events, conflict or policy change |
| **ESG risk** | Risk from environmental, social and governance factors |
| **Solvency risk** | Risk that an organization cannot meet its obligations because it lacks enough capital |
| **Strategic and reputational risk** | Risk from poor strategy or from damage to the organization's standing |

<Note>Risks **interact**. A market shock can raise liquidity risk and credit risk at the same time, and an operational failure can become a reputational crisis. This is why a firm-wide view (ERM) matters.</Note>

## 5. Measuring Risk

No single number captures risk. Different instruments need different measures.

| Measure | Applies to | What it captures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Standard deviation** (and variance) | Any asset or portfolio | Total dispersion of returns |
| **Beta** | Equities | Sensitivity to market returns (systematic risk) |
| **Duration** | Fixed income | Sensitivity of price to a change in yield |
| **Delta, gamma, vega** | Derivatives | Sensitivity to price, to the change in delta, and to volatility ("the Greeks") |
| **Value at risk (VaR)** | Portfolios, firms | Minimum loss over a horizon at a given probability |
| **Conditional VaR (CVaR)** / expected shortfall | Portfolios, firms | Average loss *given* that the VaR threshold has been breached |
| **Tracking error** | Active portfolios | Standard deviation of returns relative to a benchmark |

### Sensitivity measures: quick examples

- **Beta**: a portfolio with $\beta = 1.2$ is expected to fall about $1.2 \times 10\% = 12\%$ if the market falls 10%.
- **Duration**: the approximate price change is $\Delta P/P \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \times \Delta y$. A \$100 million bond portfolio with modified duration $5.0$ and a $+0.50\%$ yield rise loses about $5.0 \times 0.50\% = 2.5\%$, or \$2.5 million.
- **Delta**: an option with delta $0.50$ changes in value by about \$0.50 for each \$1 move in the underlying.

These measures describe **local** sensitivity to one factor. They do not give a probability-based loss estimate. VaR does.

### Value at risk (VaR)

**VaR** is the **minimum loss that would be expected to occur with a given probability over a given time horizon**. A 5% one-month VaR of \$8.7 million means: in 5% of months, losses are expected to be **at least** \$8.7 million. VaR has three inputs: the **probability** (or confidence level, 95% or 99%), the **horizon**, and the **method**.

**Parametric (variance-covariance) method.** Assuming returns are normally distributed:

$$\text{VaR} = \left[\,E(R_P) - z\,\sigma_P\,\right] \times (-1) \times \text{Portfolio value}$$

where $z = 1.65$ for 5% (95% confidence) and $z = 2.33$ for 1% (99% confidence), using the one-tailed normal values.

**Worked example.** A \$200 million portfolio has an expected monthly return of $0.60\%$ and a monthly standard deviation of $3.0\%$.

1. 5% VaR as a return: $0.60\% - 1.65 \times 3.0\% = 0.60\% - 4.95\% = -4.35\%$.
2. 5% VaR in dollars: $4.35\% \times \mathdollar 200\text{m} = \mathdollar 8.70\text{m}$.
3. 1% VaR as a return: $0.60\% - 2.33 \times 3.0\% = 0.60\% - 6.99\% = -6.39\%$.
4. 1% VaR in dollars: $6.39\% \times \mathdollar 200\text{m} = \mathdollar 12.78\text{m}$.

Interpretation: in 1 month out of 20 we expect to lose at least \$8.70 million; in 1 month out of 100, at least \$12.78 million. A higher confidence level gives a larger VaR.

**Changing the horizon.** If the mean is small relative to volatility, VaR scales with the **square root of time**: a 1-day VaR of \$1 million becomes about $\mathdollar 1\text{m} \times \sqrt{4} = \mathdollar 2$ million over 4 days.

**The three methods:**

| Method | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Parametric** | Assumes a distribution (usually normal); uses mean, standard deviation and correlations | Fast, simple, easy to decompose | Poor for options and for fat-tailed returns |
| **Historical** | Re-prices today's portfolio with actual past returns and reads off the loss percentile | No distribution assumption; captures fat tails seen in the sample | Assumes the past repeats; sample-dependent |
| **Monte Carlo** | Simulates many random scenarios from a specified model | Flexible; handles options and non-normal returns | Computationally heavy; depends on model assumptions (model risk) |

For the historical method with 100 daily observations, the 5% VaR is the **5th-worst** outcome.

### Limitations of VaR

- It gives a **minimum** loss in the tail, not the **maximum**: it says nothing about how bad losses can be *beyond* the threshold. CVaR addresses this.
- Results depend on the **method, horizon and confidence level** chosen.
- It is exposed to **model risk** and to data limitations; estimates can be unreliable in a crisis when correlations spike.
- It does not capture **liquidity risk** or the risk of rare events well, so it should be paired with **stress tests** and **scenario analysis**.

**Stress testing** asks "what if a specific severe event happens?" (for example a 300 bp rate rise). **Scenario analysis** examines the effect of a defined set of changes in several risk factors together. Both complement VaR.

## 6. Methods of Risk Modification

Once risks are measured and compared with the desired level, the organization can modify them. There are four basic responses:

| Method | Idea | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Risk prevention and avoidance** | Do not take the risk at all, or stop the activity | Declining to enter a market; choosing not to underwrite a risky client |
| **Risk acceptance** | Retain the risk deliberately | *Self-insure* by setting aside reserves; *diversify* so that only systematic risk is retained |
| **Risk transfer** | Pass the risk to another party for a payment | Buy **insurance**; obtain a **surety bond** or guarantee |
| **Risk shifting** | Change the *distribution* of outcomes, usually with derivatives | Use futures, forwards, options or swaps to hedge price, rate or currency exposure |

Two important distinctions:

- **Transfer** moves risk of loss to a third party (usually through a contract with fixed terms), while **shifting** reshapes the probability distribution of outcomes: it reduces the probability of large losses but may give up some upside.
- **Diversification** is a form of risk *acceptance* that exploits imperfect correlation: it reduces non-systematic risk at no cost in expected return.

<Note>Hedging is not free and not always desirable. A derivative hedge removes variability, but it also gives up gains, adds counterparty and basis risk, and may be a poor use of the risk budget if the organization is in fact paid for bearing that risk.</Note>

### Evaluating the result: risk-adjusted performance

Risk-taking should be judged against return. Common **risk-adjusted return** measures (Sharpe ratio, Treynor ratio, return on VaR) divide return by a measure of risk. A business unit that earns a high return only because it consumes a large share of the risk budget has not necessarily added value. Risk-adjusted measures help allocate the budget to where it earns the most per unit of risk.

## Key takeaways

- Risk management is a **continuous process** that aligns the risk actually taken with the risk the organization is **willing and able** to take. It does not aim to eliminate risk.
- **Risk governance** sets policy and accountability; the **framework** is the infrastructure; **ERM** manages risk on a firm-wide basis, including interactions among risks.
- **Risk tolerance** combines willingness and ability to bear risk; **risk budgeting** allocates the total risk among units and strategies.
- **Financial risks**: market, credit, liquidity. **Non-financial risks**: operational, model, legal, regulatory, tax, sovereign, ESG, solvency and others. They interact.
- Use the right measure: **standard deviation and beta** (equity), **duration** (bonds), **delta, gamma and vega** (derivatives), **VaR and CVaR** (portfolio loss).
- **Parametric VaR** $= [E(R_P) - z\sigma_P] \times (-1) \times$ value, with $z = 1.65$ (5%) and $2.33$ (1%). VaR is a **minimum** loss in the tail; supplement it with CVaR, stress tests and scenario analysis.
- The four modification methods are **avoid, accept (self-insure or diversify), transfer (insurance, surety)** and **shift (derivatives)**.
