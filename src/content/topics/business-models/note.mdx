# Business Models

A **business model** describes how a company creates, delivers and captures value: who its customers are, what it sells them, how it charges, and what it costs to operate. Two companies in the same industry can have very different business models, and therefore very different margins, capital needs, growth paths and risks. Analysts use the business model as the starting point for forecasting and valuation, because it links strategy to the numbers in the financial statements.

---

## 1. What a Business Model Is

A business model answers a handful of questions:

| Question | Element | Example prompts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What do we offer, and why does it matter? | **Value proposition** | Lower price, better quality, convenience, status, customisation |
| Who do we serve? | **Customer segments** | Which customers, how many, how loyal, how price-sensitive |
| How do we reach them? | **Channels** | Direct sales, own stores, online, distributors, franchisees |
| How do we earn money? | **Revenue model and pricing** | One-off sale, subscription, usage fee, commission, advertising |
| What does it cost to deliver? | **Cost structure and operating model** | Fixed versus variable costs, capital intensity, make versus buy |
| What do we need in place? | **Key resources and partners** | Brands, patents, data, people, suppliers, regulatory licences |

A model is not the same as a **strategy**. Strategy is the set of choices a firm makes to compete and win (cost leadership, differentiation, focus). The business model is how those choices turn into a working, money-making system. A sound model is one where customers value the proposition enough to pay a price that exceeds the cost of delivering it, by a margin large enough to earn the investors' required return.

<Note>When you meet a new company, write its model in one sentence: "It sells X to Y through Z and earns money by W." If you cannot, you cannot yet forecast it.</Note>

## 2. Key Features of a Business Model

### 2.1 Customers and markets

- **B2C (business to consumer)**: sells to individuals. Typically many small transactions, brand and marketing matter, demand is sensitive to household income and sentiment.
- **B2B (business to business)**: sells to other firms. Fewer, larger customers, longer sales cycles, contracts, and often a concentration risk if one customer is a large share of revenue.
- **B2G (business to government)**: sells to public bodies. Procurement rules, long contracts and political risk, but often stable payments.
- **Customer segmentation** divides customers by needs or behaviour (for example mass market versus premium). A firm can serve one segment well or several segments with different offerings.

### 2.2 Revenue model and pricing

How and when revenue is earned drives revenue visibility and working capital.

| Revenue model | How the firm earns | Typical feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Transactional / one-off sale** | Sells a product once | Lumpy, cyclical, depends on new sales every period |
| **Subscription** | Recurring fee for access over time | Predictable, recurring revenue, often paid in advance |
| **Usage / pay-per-use** | Charge scales with consumption | Revenue grows with customer activity |
| **Razor-and-blade (captive product)** | Sells a base product cheaply, earns on consumables | Low or negative margin on the base item, high margin on repeat purchases |
| **Commission / take rate** | Percentage of transaction value passing through | Asset-light, scales with volume |
| **Advertising** | Free service paid for by advertisers | Needs a large, engaged audience |
| **Freemium** | Free basic tier, paid premium tier | Low acquisition cost, but only a fraction convert to paid |
| **Franchise / licence** | Receives upfront fees and royalties from operators who run outlets | Fast expansion with little capital, royalty income |

**Pricing approaches** include cost-plus, value-based, penetration (low entry price to win share), skimming (high initial price that falls over time), tiered pricing and **dynamic pricing**, where the price changes with demand, time or inventory (airlines, ride-hailing).

### 2.3 Channels and distribution

A firm may sell **directly** (own stores, own website, direct sales force) or **indirectly** through distributors, wholesalers, retailers or franchisees. Direct channels keep more margin and customer data but require more capital and fixed cost. Indirect channels reach wider markets quickly but share margin and give up control of the customer relationship. Many firms are **omnichannel**, combining stores and online.

### 2.4 Cost structure and operating model

- **Fixed costs** do not change with volume in the short run (rent, depreciation, salaried staff, R&D).
- **Variable costs** change in proportion to volume (materials, sales commissions, shipping).
- A **capital-intensive** model needs heavy investment in plant and equipment (utilities, airlines, steel). An **asset-light** model leans on partners, licences, franchisees or software (franchisors, software firms).
- **Vertical integration** means the firm owns more of the value chain (suppliers, distribution). It can improve control and margin but raises fixed cost and capital need. **Outsourcing** does the reverse.

## 3. Common Business Model Types

### 3.1 Pure-play versus diversified

A **pure-play** company focuses on one business or sector, so its results and valuation reflect that business directly. A **conglomerate** or diversified group holds several unrelated businesses. Diversification can smooth earnings, but it can also hide weak units, make capital allocation less transparent and lead to a "conglomerate discount" if the market believes the parts are worth more separately. Analysts often value diversified groups using a **sum-of-the-parts** approach.

### 3.2 Platform (marketplace) models

A **platform** connects two or more groups of users (for example buyers and sellers, riders and drivers, advertisers and audiences) and earns money from the interaction, usually through a take rate, listing fees or advertising.

- **Network effects**: the platform becomes more valuable to each user as more users join. A **direct (same-side) network effect** arises among one type of user; a **cross-side (indirect) effect** arises when more users on one side attract more on the other.
- Platforms are typically **asset-light**, with high gross margins and low marginal cost per extra transaction, but they face high spending to reach critical mass (the "chicken-and-egg" problem) and may attract regulatory attention if they become dominant.
- Once established, network effects can create a **winner-take-most** market with high barriers to entry.

### 3.3 Subscription and recurring-revenue models

Subscription revenue is easier to forecast because customers renew automatically. Key measures are:

- **Churn rate**: share of customers lost per period. Expected customer lifetime is roughly $1 / \text{churn}$.
- **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)**: sales and marketing spend per new customer.
- **Customer lifetime value (LTV)**: gross profit a customer is expected to generate over their life with the firm.
- **LTV / CAC** and the **payback period** (CAC divided by monthly gross profit per customer) show whether growth creates value.

Subscriptions are often billed **in advance**, which creates **deferred (unearned) revenue** on the balance sheet and can make working capital negative, a source of cash.

### 3.4 Franchise and licensing models

A franchisor sells the right to use its brand and system. It earns an upfront fee plus a royalty on franchisee sales. Because franchisees fund the outlets and bear most operating costs, the franchisor needs little capital, has a high return on capital and a stable royalty stream, but depends on brand consistency and franchisee health.

## 4. Business Model and Financial Performance

The model shapes the financial statements in predictable ways.

| Model feature | Income statement | Balance sheet / cash flow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asset-light, platform or software | High gross margin, low depreciation, margins can expand with scale | Low capital expenditure, high free cash flow conversion, high return on capital |
| Capital-intensive | Heavy depreciation, high fixed costs, lower margin | Large fixed assets and debt, high capex needs |
| Subscription / prepaid | Smooth, recurring revenue | Deferred revenue, favourable working capital |
| Razor-and-blade | Low margin on base product, high on consumables | Installed base drives future cash flow |
| Direct distribution | Higher gross margin but higher selling expense | More inventory and stores on the balance sheet |
| Franchisor | Revenue is fee income, high operating margin | Very little capital employed |

### 4.1 Operating leverage

**Operating leverage** is the degree to which a firm's costs are fixed. A model with high fixed costs and low variable costs has high operating leverage: profit rises quickly when sales rise and falls quickly when sales fall.

$$\text{Contribution margin per unit} = \text{Price} - \text{Variable cost per unit}$$

$$\text{Break-even quantity} = \frac{\text{Fixed costs}}{\text{Contribution margin per unit}}$$

$$\text{Degree of operating leverage (DOL)} = \frac{\%\Delta\,\text{EBIT}}{\%\Delta\,\text{Sales}} = \frac{Q\,(P - V)}{Q\,(P - V) - F}$$

**Worked example.** Two firms sell the same product at a price of \$50 per unit and each sells 40,000 units, so revenue is \$2,000,000.

- **Firm A** (outsourced, asset-light): variable cost \$30 per unit, fixed costs \$200,000.
- **Firm B** (vertically integrated, capital-intensive): variable cost \$10 per unit, fixed costs \$800,000.

*Firm A*

1. Contribution margin $= 50 - 30 = 20$ per unit. Total contribution $= 40{,}000 \times 20 = 800{,}000$.
2. EBIT $= 800{,}000 - 200{,}000 = 600{,}000$, an operating margin of $600{,}000 / 2{,}000{,}000 = 30\%$.
3. $\text{DOL} = 800{,}000 / 600{,}000 = 1.33$.
4. Break-even $= 200{,}000 / 20 = 10{,}000$ units.

*Firm B*

1. Contribution margin $= 50 - 10 = 40$ per unit. Total contribution $= 40{,}000 \times 40 = 1{,}600{,}000$.
2. EBIT $= 1{,}600{,}000 - 800{,}000 = 800{,}000$, an operating margin of $800{,}000 / 2{,}000{,}000 = 40\%$.
3. $\text{DOL} = 1{,}600{,}000 / 800{,}000 = 2.00$.
4. Break-even $= 800{,}000 / 40 = 20{,}000$ units.

*What happens if sales move by 10%?*

| | Sales volume | Firm A EBIT | Change | Firm B EBIT | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base | 40,000 | 600,000 | | 800,000 | |
| Sales +10% | 44,000 | $44{,}000 \times 20 - 200{,}000 = 680{,}000$ | $+13.3\%$ | $44{,}000 \times 40 - 800{,}000 = 960{,}000$ | $+20.0\%$ |
| Sales $-10\%$ | 36,000 | $36{,}000 \times 20 - 200{,}000 = 520{,}000$ | $-13.3\%$ | $36{,}000 \times 40 - 800{,}000 = 640{,}000$ | $-20.0\%$ |

Firm B earns the higher margin in normal times and gains more when sales grow, but it needs twice the volume to break even and its profit swings more in a downturn. This is why cyclical, capital-intensive models are riskier for the same level of sales volatility.

<Note>High operating leverage is not good or bad by itself. It magnifies results in both directions. Combined with financial leverage (debt), it magnifies them further.</Note>

### 4.2 Unit economics of a subscription model

**Worked example.** A software firm charges \$12 per customer per month. Gross margin is 75%, monthly churn is 2%, and it spends \$180 to acquire each customer.

1. Gross profit per customer per month $= 12 \times 0.75 = 9$.
2. Expected lifetime $= 1 / 0.02 = 50$ months.
3. Lifetime gross profit (LTV, simple and undiscounted) $= 9 \times 50 = 450$.
4. $\text{LTV} / \text{CAC} = 450 / 180 = 2.5$.
5. Payback period $= 180 / 9 = 20$ months.

Each customer returns 2.5 times its acquisition cost over its life, and the firm recovers the outlay after 20 months. If churn rose to 4%, lifetime would halve to 25 months, LTV would fall to \$225 and LTV/CAC to 1.25, so the model would barely cover its acquisition cost once discounting and operating overhead are considered.

### 4.3 Growth, risk and working capital

- **Growth** comes from more customers, higher price, more purchases per customer or new products and markets. Platform and subscription models can grow quickly at low marginal cost; capital-intensive models need capex to grow.
- **Risk** depends on customer concentration, cyclicality of demand, dependence on key suppliers, regulatory exposure and the strength of competitive barriers (brand, scale, switching costs, network effects, patents).
- **Working capital** differs by model: retailers carry inventory; B2B sellers often have large receivables; subscription and prepaid models collect cash before delivering service.

## 5. Comparing Business Models

When comparing firms, check:

1. **Value proposition and customers**: is the advantage durable, and who really pays?
2. **Revenue quality**: recurring versus one-off, concentration, pricing power.
3. **Cost structure**: fixed versus variable, operating leverage, scale benefits.
4. **Capital intensity**: capex and working capital needs relative to sales, and return on invested capital.
5. **Scalability**: can revenue grow much faster than costs (a platform) or must costs rise in step (a labour-intensive service)?
6. **Barriers to entry and sustainability**: what stops a rival copying the model?
7. **Reporting consequences**: how revenue is recognised (up front versus over time), deferred revenue, capitalised costs, and segment disclosure, because the same economics can look different on paper.

## Key takeaways

- A business model explains how a firm creates, delivers and captures value: value proposition, customers, channels, revenue model, cost structure and key resources.
- Model types differ in customer base (B2C, B2B, B2G), revenue source (sale, subscription, usage, take rate, advertising, royalties) and scope (pure-play versus diversified).
- Platforms rely on network effects and are asset-light; subscriptions give recurring, often prepaid revenue; franchisors earn fees and royalties with little capital.
- Fixed-cost-heavy models have high operating leverage: $\text{DOL} = \dfrac{Q(P-V)}{Q(P-V)-F}$ and higher break-even volume.
- Subscription economics: expected lifetime $\approx 1/\text{churn}$, $\text{LTV} = $ monthly gross profit $\times$ lifetime, and LTV/CAC and payback show whether growth creates value.
- The model determines margins, capital intensity, working capital, growth potential and risk, so analyse it before forecasting.
