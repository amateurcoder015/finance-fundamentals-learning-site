# Firms and Market Structures

A firm does not choose its price and output in a vacuum. How much **pricing power** it has depends on its **market structure**: how many competitors there are, how similar their products are, how easily new firms can enter, and how much firms influence one another. For an analyst, market structure explains why some industries earn lasting excess profits and others earn only a normal return.

---

## 1. The Four Market Structures

| Feature | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Number of sellers** | Very many | Many | Few | One |
| **Product** | Identical (homogeneous) | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated | Unique, no close substitutes |
| **Barriers to entry** | None | Low | High | Very high |
| **Pricing power** | None (price taker) | Some | Considerable, but depends on rivals | Substantial (price searcher), may be regulated |
| **Non-price competition** | None | Heavy (branding, quality, advertising) | Often heavy | Limited (advertising to build demand) |
| **Examples** | Wheat, commodity farming | Restaurants, clothing, hair salons | Aircraft, autos, telecom | Local utility, patented drug |

**Monopoly** can arise from control of a key resource, patents and legal protection, **economies of scale** (a **natural monopoly**, where one firm can serve the market at lower average cost than two or more) or government franchises.

## 2. Demand, Revenue and Cost Concepts

### Revenue

- **Total revenue**: $TR = P \times Q$
- **Average revenue**: $AR = TR/Q = P$
- **Marginal revenue**: $MR = \Delta TR / \Delta Q$, the extra revenue from selling one more unit

For a **price taker**, $MR = P$ at every quantity (the demand curve facing the firm is horizontal). For a firm with a **downward-sloping demand curve**, selling more requires a lower price on *all* units, so $MR < P$.

For a straight-line demand curve $P = a - bQ$:

$$TR = aQ - bQ^2, \qquad MR = a - 2bQ$$

The MR curve has the same intercept as demand but **twice the slope**.

### Marginal revenue and elasticity

$$MR = P\left(1 - \frac{1}{|E_P|}\right)$$

where $E_P$ is the price elasticity of demand. Consequences:

- $MR > 0$ only where demand is **elastic** ($|E_P| > 1$);
- $MR = 0$ where demand is unit elastic;
- A profit-maximizing firm with pricing power (and positive marginal cost) therefore always operates on the **elastic** portion of its demand curve.

### Costs

| Measure | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Total fixed cost (TFC)** | Does not change with output in the short run |
| **Total variable cost (TVC)** | Changes with output |
| **Total cost (TC)** | $TFC + TVC$ |
| **Average total cost (ATC)** | $TC/Q$ |
| **Average variable cost (AVC)** | $TVC/Q$ |
| **Marginal cost (MC)** | $\Delta TC / \Delta Q$, the extra cost of one more unit |

MC cuts both ATC and AVC at their minimum points. In the long run all costs are variable; the **long-run average cost (LRAC)** curve shows the lowest cost for each output once the plant size can be chosen. Falling LRAC means **economies of scale**; rising LRAC means **diseconomies of scale**. The output where LRAC is lowest is the **minimum efficient scale**.

## 3. Profit Maximization: MR = MC

Economic profit equals total revenue minus **all** costs, including the opportunity cost of the owners' capital. A firm maximizes profit by producing the quantity at which

$$MR = MC \quad \text{(with MC rising through MR)}$$

- If $MR > MC$, one more unit adds more revenue than cost, so raise output.
- If $MR < MC$, the last unit loses money, so cut output.

Having found $Q^*$, the price is read from the **demand curve** (for a firm with pricing power) and profit is $(P - ATC) \times Q^*$.

### Breakeven and shutdown (short run)

| Condition | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| $P > ATC$ | Economic profit; produce where $MR = MC$ |
| $AVC < P < ATC$ | Loss, but revenue covers all variable cost and part of fixed cost, so **keep operating** in the short run |
| $P < AVC$ | Revenue does not even cover variable cost; **shut down** (the loss is then just the fixed cost) |

In the long run a firm exits if revenue does not cover **total** cost ($P < ATC$ at best output).

### Worked example 1: should the firm keep operating?

A price-taking firm faces a market price of \$12. At its best output of 1,000 units, average variable cost is \$10 and average total cost is \$14.

1. Operate: $\text{Profit} = (P - ATC) \times Q = (12 - 14) \times 1000 = -2000$ dollars, a loss of \$2,000.
2. Fixed cost per unit $= ATC - AVC = 14 - 10 = 4$, so total fixed cost $= 4 \times 1000 = 4000$ dollars.
3. Shut down: loss equals fixed cost, \$4,000.

Operating loses \$2,000 versus \$4,000 if shut: because $P = 12 > AVC = 10$, the firm covers variable cost and contributes \$2 per unit (\$2,000 in total) toward fixed cost. **Keep operating** in the short run; exit in the long run unless prices rise.

## 4. Perfect Competition

Every firm is a price taker, so its demand curve is horizontal at the market price, and $MR = AR = P$. Profit maximization becomes:

$$P = MR = MC$$

- **Short run**: firms may earn profit or loss depending on whether $P$ is above or below ATC.
- **Long run**: profit attracts entry (supply shifts right, price falls); losses cause exit (price rises). The process stops when economic profit is zero, so

$$P = MC = \min ATC = \min LRAC$$

The industry is **allocatively efficient** ($P = MC$, so the value consumers place on the last unit equals its cost) and **productively efficient** (output is produced at minimum average cost). In the long run firms earn only a **normal profit** (zero economic profit).

---

## 5. Monopoly

A monopolist is the only seller, so it faces the *market* demand curve, which slopes down. It maximizes profit where $MR = MC$ and charges the price **on the demand curve** at that quantity, so $P > MR = MC$.

Compared with perfect competition, a monopoly produces **less** output at a **higher** price, creating a **deadweight loss**: output that consumers value above its cost is never produced. Barriers to entry let the monopolist keep economic profit in the long run. Spending to gain or defend the monopoly (**rent seeking**) can dissipate some of those profits.

A **price-discriminating** monopolist charges different customers different prices for the same product according to their willingness to pay. Perfect discrimination removes the deadweight loss but transfers all consumer surplus to the seller.

### Regulation of monopolies

| Approach | How it works | Issue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Marginal-cost pricing** | Price set where $P = MC$ | Efficient, but for a natural monopoly $P = MC$ is below ATC, so the firm runs at a loss and needs a subsidy |
| **Average-cost pricing** | Price set where $P = ATC$ | Firm earns normal profit; some deadweight loss remains |
| **Cost-plus / rate-of-return** | Costs plus an allowed return | Weak incentive to control cost |
| **Price caps / incentive regulation** | Cap on price changes | Better incentive for efficiency; risk of quality cuts |

### Worked example 2: monopolist versus perfect competition

Market demand is $P = 100 - 2Q$. A firm has constant marginal cost $MC = 20$ and fixed cost \$200, so $TC = 200 + 20Q$.

**Monopoly**

1. $TR = 100Q - 2Q^2$, so $MR = 100 - 4Q$.
2. Set $MR = MC$: $100 - 4Q = 20 \Rightarrow Q = 20$.
3. Price from demand: $P = 100 - 2(20) = 60$.
4. Profit: $TR = 60 \times 20 = 1,200$; $TC = 200 + 20(20) = 600$; profit $= 600$.

Check with elasticity: demand is $Q = 50 - 0.5P$, so $|E_P| = 0.5 \times 60 / 20 = 1.5$ (elastic, as required), and $MR = 60(1 - 1/1.5) = 60 \times \tfrac13 = 20 = MC$. ✓

**Perfect competition (same cost, same demand)**

1. Set $P = MC$: $100 - 2Q = 20 \Rightarrow Q = 40$ and $P = 20$.
2. Profit: $TR = 20 \times 40 = 800$; $TC = 200 + 20(40) = 1,000$; profit $= -200$. (The loss is the fixed cost; with a single plant the competitive price only covers marginal cost.)

**Deadweight loss of the monopoly** is the triangle between demand and MC from $Q = 20$ to $Q = 40$:

$$DWL = \tfrac12 \times (60 - 20) \times (40 - 20) = \tfrac12 \times 40 \times 20 = 400$$

## 6. Monopolistic Competition

Many firms sell **differentiated** products (by quality, branding, location, features) with easy entry and exit. Each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so it behaves like a small monopolist: $MR = MC$ fixes output and price is above MC.

- **Short run**: economic profit or loss is possible.
- **Long run**: entry (or exit) shifts each firm's demand until demand is just tangent to ATC. Economic profit is **zero**, but $P > MC$ and the firm produces at an output **below** the minimum-ATC point, so there is **excess capacity**.
- Firms compete on **product differentiation, advertising and branding**. Differentiation raises cost but gives customers variety, so the inefficiency is partly offset by choice.

---

## 7. Oligopoly

A few firms dominate, barriers to entry are high, and **each firm's best decision depends on what rivals do** (interdependence). There is no single oligopoly model; the common ones are:

### Kinked demand curve

If rivals **match price cuts but ignore price increases**, demand is **elastic above** the current price (a rise loses many customers) and **inelastic below** it (a cut gains few). This creates a kink and a **gap in the MR curve**, so price tends to stay stable when marginal cost moves inside the gap.

### Game theory and Nash equilibrium

Firms' decisions are modeled as a game with strategies and payoffs. A **Nash equilibrium** is a set of strategies in which no firm can improve its payoff by changing its strategy alone, given the others' strategies. In a **prisoner's dilemma**, each firm's best response is to cheat on the cooperative outcome, so both end worse off than if they had cooperated. Repeated interaction, the ability to detect cheating and punish it, and few firms make **collusion** more stable.

### Cournot (quantity competition)

Each firm chooses its output assuming rivals' output stays fixed. The Nash equilibrium lies between the monopoly and perfectly competitive outcomes, and total output rises (and price falls) as the number of firms increases.

### Other models

- **Dominant firm model**: one large firm sets the price, and a fringe of small price-taking firms supplies whatever they will at that price. The dominant firm serves the remaining demand.
- **Stackelberg (sequential)**: a leader commits to output first, and followers respond; the leader earns more than in Cournot.
- **Bertrand (price competition)**: with identical products and equal constant cost, firms undercut each other until $P = MC$, the competitive result, even with only two firms.

### Worked example 3: Cournot duopoly

Market demand is $P = 100 - Q$, where $Q = q_1 + q_2$, and each firm has constant $MC = 10$ and no fixed cost.

1. Firm 1's revenue is $TR_1 = (100 - q_1 - q_2)q_1$, so, holding $q_2$ fixed, $MR_1 = 100 - q_2 - 2q_1$.
2. Set $MR_1 = MC = 10$: $q_1 = (90 - q_2)/2$. This is firm 1's reaction function; by symmetry $q_2 = (90 - q_1)/2$.
3. In equilibrium $q_1 = q_2 = q$: $q = (90 - q)/2 \Rightarrow 3q = 90 \Rightarrow q = 30$.
4. Total $Q = 60$, so $P = 100 - 60 = 40$, and each firm earns $(40 - 10) \times 30 = 900$ (industry profit \$1,800).

Compare outcomes:

| Structure | Total Q | Price | Industry profit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect competition ($P = MC$) | 90 | \$10 | \$0 |
| Cournot duopoly | 60 | \$40 | \$1,800 |
| Monopoly / perfect collusion ($MR = 100 - 2Q = 10$) | 45 | \$55 | $(55 - 10) \times 45 = 2,025$ |

Each duopolist would get $2,025/2 = 1,012.50$ by colluding, but each is tempted to produce more, which is the prisoner's dilemma.

## 8. Identifying Market Structure: Concentration Measures

Analysts look at both the **demand side** (elasticity, availability of substitutes, cross-price elasticity) and the **supply side** (concentration, barriers to entry), and watch for signs of pricing power such as persistently high returns and stable market shares.

- **N-firm concentration ratio**: the sum of the market shares of the $N$ largest firms (e.g., the four-firm ratio). Easy to compute but ignores how shares are split inside the top $N$ and ignores the small firms, and it may not reflect the effect of mergers.
- **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)**: the sum of squared market shares (shares in percent, so HHI ranges from near 0 to 10,000):

$$HHI = \sum_{i=1}^{N} (s_i)^2$$

HHI gives more weight to large firms and is more sensitive to mergers. Regulators treat markets with higher HHI as more concentrated and review mergers that raise it materially. Low HHI suggests competitive structures; very high HHI suggests monopoly or tight oligopoly.

### Worked example 4: concentration

Four firms hold market shares of 40%, 30%, 20% and 10%.

- Four-firm concentration ratio $= 40 + 30 + 20 + 10 = 100\%$.
- $HHI = 40^2 + 30^2 + 20^2 + 10^2 = 1{,}600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3{,}000$.

If the two smallest firms merge (to a 30% firm), $HHI = 1{,}600 + 900 + 900 = 3{,}400$, an increase of 400. A monopoly has $HHI = 100^2 = 10{,}000$.

Limitations: both measures depend on how the market is defined, and they say little about **barriers to entry** or import competition, which also limit pricing power.

## Key takeaways

- Market structure (number of sellers, product differentiation, entry barriers, pricing power) determines how a firm sets price and output.
- All firms maximize profit where $MR = MC$. A price taker has $MR = P$; a firm with pricing power has $MR < P$ and sets price from the demand curve above $MR = MC$.
- Short run: operate if $P \geq AVC$, shut down if $P < AVC$. Long run: exit if $P < ATC$.
- Perfect competition ends with $P = MC = \min ATC$ and zero economic profit; monopolistic competition ends with zero economic profit, $P > MC$ and excess capacity.
- A monopoly restricts output, charges $P > MC$ and creates deadweight loss; natural monopolies are regulated by average-cost pricing, marginal-cost pricing with a subsidy, or price caps.
- Oligopolies are interdependent: study them with kinked demand, Nash equilibrium, Cournot, dominant firm, Stackelberg and Bertrand models; collusion is unstable because each firm gains by cheating.
- $HHI = \sum s_i^2$ (shares in percent); it is more informative than the N-firm ratio but neither captures barriers to entry.
