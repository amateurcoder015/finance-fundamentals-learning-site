# Portfolio Management: An Overview

**Portfolio management** is the process of combining investments to meet an investor's objectives while respecting the investor's constraints. Instead of judging each security on its own, the manager asks how it changes the **risk and return of the whole portfolio**. This chapter introduces the portfolio perspective, the steps of the investment process, who the clients and managers are, and the vehicles through which investors gain exposure.

---

## 1. The Portfolio Approach

Evaluating a security **in isolation** looks only at its own expected return and risk. The **portfolio perspective** looks at how the security behaves **relative to the other holdings**. Two securities that are each risky can, when combined, produce a portfolio that is less risky than either, provided their returns are not perfectly correlated.

- **Diversification** means spreading money across assets whose returns do not move in lockstep, so that poor outcomes in some holdings are offset by better outcomes in others.
- Diversification reduces **portfolio risk without necessarily giving up expected return**. It is often called the closest thing to a "free lunch" in investing.
- Diversification cannot remove **systematic (market) risk**; it mainly removes risk specific to an individual security or issuer.
- Diversification does not guarantee against loss, and in a severe market crisis correlations between assets often rise, which weakens the benefit.

### Portfolio return

The expected return of a portfolio is the weighted average of the expected returns of its holdings:

$$E(R_p) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, E(R_i), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i = 1$$

Risk is **not** a simple weighted average. For two assets:

$$\sigma_p = \sqrt{w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2\,w_1 w_2\,\rho_{12}\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}$$

where $\rho_{12}$ is the correlation of the two returns. When $\rho_{12} = +1$, $\sigma_p$ equals the weighted average of the two standard deviations; whenever $\rho_{12} < 1$, $\sigma_p$ is **lower** than that average. (Part I of Portfolio Risk and Return develops this fully.)

<Note>
The key idea is that a portfolio's expected return is a weighted average, but its risk is not. The gap between the two is the benefit of diversification.
</Note>

## 2. The Investment Process

Portfolio management is a continuing process with three broad steps.

| Step | What happens | Main outputs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Planning** | Understand the client's needs, then write down objectives and constraints | **Investment policy statement (IPS)**, capital market expectations, **strategic asset allocation (SAA)** |
| **2. Execution** | Turn the plan into a portfolio | Tactical adjustments, security selection, **portfolio construction**, trading |
| **3. Feedback** | Monitor and evaluate | Monitoring, **rebalancing**, performance measurement and reporting |

### Step 1: Planning

The **investment policy statement** is the written plan that guides all decisions. It typically records:

- **Objectives**: the return desired and the **risk tolerance** (a combination of willingness and ability to take risk).
- **Constraints**: **liquidity** needs, **time horizon**, **tax** considerations, **legal and regulatory** limits, and **unique circumstances** (for example, ethical exclusions).

From the IPS and expectations for the capital markets, the manager sets the **strategic asset allocation**: the long-term target weights to each **asset class**. SAA is usually the single most important driver of a portfolio's long-run returns and risk.

### Step 2: Execution

- **Asset allocation** may be adjusted tactically around the strategic targets.
- **Security selection** chooses the individual holdings within each asset class.
- **Portfolio construction** combines these decisions into a portfolio that meets the IPS, taking into account costs and trading.

### Step 3: Feedback

- **Monitoring** checks that the portfolio still fits the client's circumstances and that markets and expectations have not changed enough to alter the plan.
- **Rebalancing** restores the target weights when market moves cause drift.
- **Performance evaluation** compares results with a benchmark and with the objectives, and the results feed back into planning.

## 3. Asset Classes

An **asset class** is a group of investments with similar characteristics, subject to similar laws and regulations, and that tend to behave similarly. Typical classes are:

- **Traditional**: cash and cash equivalents, fixed income (government and corporate bonds), and public equity (domestic and international, large and small cap).
- **Alternative**: real assets such as real estate, infrastructure and commodities; **hedge funds**; **private capital** (private equity and private debt); and digital assets.

Classes differ in expected return, risk, liquidity and correlation with each other, which is why mixing them helps. A manager may define classes more finely (for example, splitting equity by region) when the investor's needs justify it.

## 4. Asset Owners and Asset Managers

**Asset owners** supply the capital; **asset managers** (or investment managers) run the portfolios on their behalf.

### Retail investors

Individuals investing for goals such as retirement, education or a home. Their circumstances differ widely, so their objectives and constraints are individual. Many invest through pooled products because of small account sizes.

### Institutional investors

| Type | Typical objective and features |
| --- | --- |
| **Defined-benefit (DB) pension plan** | Funds promised retirement benefits; long horizon, but liabilities are fixed, so the plan aims to meet them. The sponsor bears the funding risk |
| **Defined-contribution (DC) plan** | Contributions go into individual accounts; the **employee bears investment risk** and chooses from the offered options |
| **Endowments and foundations** | Support a mission (education, research, charity) from investment income, often in perpetuity, with spending policies and long horizons |
| **Sovereign wealth funds** | Government-owned pools funded by commodity revenue or reserves; long horizons, often high risk tolerance |
| **Insurance companies** | Invest premiums to pay future claims; liabilities drive a conservative approach, especially for life insurers |
| **Banks** | Invest deposits in loans and securities; liquidity and regulatory capital are key constraints |

### Active and passive management

- **Passive management** tracks a benchmark index at low cost, aiming to match its return.
- **Active management** seeks to **outperform** a benchmark through security selection and timing, at higher fees and trading costs.
- Many investors combine the two (a **core-satellite** approach: a passive core with active satellites).

## 5. Pooled Investment Vehicles

### Mutual funds

A **mutual fund** pools money from many investors in a portfolio managed by professionals. Investors own **shares or units**, priced at the fund's **net asset value (NAV)**:

$$NAV = \frac{\text{Total assets} - \text{Total liabilities}}{\text{Shares outstanding}}$$

- **Open-end funds** issue and redeem shares with investors at NAV, usually once a day after markets close, so the number of shares varies.
- **Closed-end funds** issue a fixed number of shares that then trade on an exchange; the market price can sit at a **premium or discount to NAV**.
- Costs include an **expense ratio** (management and administrative fees as a percentage of assets) and sometimes sales charges (**loads**).

### Exchange-traded funds (ETFs)

An **ETF** is a pooled fund whose shares trade on an exchange throughout the day at market prices, usually tracking an index.

- **Creation and redemption**: large institutions called **authorized participants (APs)** deliver the underlying securities to the ETF in exchange for ETF shares (**creation**), or hand back ETF shares for the securities (**redemption**), often **in kind**.
- Arbitrage by APs keeps the ETF's price close to its NAV: if the ETF trades above NAV, APs create shares and sell them; if below, they buy shares and redeem them.
- In-kind transfers can also make ETFs **tax-efficient** in some jurisdictions.
- Typical features: low expense ratios, intraday liquidity, transparent holdings; investors also pay brokerage commissions and the bid-ask spread.

### Separately managed accounts (SMAs)

An **SMA** is a portfolio of securities owned directly by one client and managed on that client's behalf. It can be **customized** (for tax, ethical or concentration preferences), the client holds the actual securities, and fees are negotiated, but minimum sizes are usually higher than for pooled funds.

### Hedge funds and private capital

- **Hedge funds** are privately offered pools using flexible strategies, such as short selling, leverage and derivatives, often with a performance fee and redemption restrictions (see the Hedge Funds chapter).
- **Private equity** and **private debt** invest in companies and loans not traded on public markets; they are illiquid, have long lock-ups, and are typically open only to institutional or qualified investors (see Investments in Private Capital).

| Feature | Mutual fund | ETF | SMA | Hedge fund |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ownership | Fund shares | Fund shares | Securities held directly | Partnership interests |
| Pricing | NAV (open-end) | Market price during the day | Direct holdings | NAV at valuation dates |
| Customization | None | None | High | Limited, strategy-specific |
| Liquidity | Daily | Intraday | Depends on holdings | Restricted, with lock-ups |

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example A: Portfolio return and the benefit of diversification

A portfolio holds 60% in Asset A (expected return 10%, standard deviation 20%) and 40% in Asset B (expected return 5%, standard deviation 10%). The correlation is 0.3.

1. Expected return $= 0.6 \times 10\% + 0.4 \times 5\% = 6\% + 2\% = 8\%$
2. Weighted average standard deviation $= 0.6 \times 20\% + 0.4 \times 10\% = 16\%$ (the risk if $\rho = +1$)
3. Variance:
   - $w_1^2\sigma_1^2 = 0.36 \times 0.04 = 0.0144$
   - $w_2^2\sigma_2^2 = 0.16 \times 0.01 = 0.0016$
   - $2\,w_1w_2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 \times 0.3 \times 0.20 \times 0.10 = 0.00288$
   - Total $= 0.01888$
4. $\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.01888} = 13.74\%$

Portfolio risk of 13.74% is below the 16% weighted average. Expected return is unchanged at 8%, so the benefit of diversification is about 2.26 percentage points of risk.

### Example B: Rebalancing to the strategic allocation

An investor's SAA is 60% equities and 40% bonds on a 1,000,000 portfolio. Over a year, equities gain 20% and bonds lose 10%.

1. Equities $= 600{,}000 \times 1.20 = 720{,}000$; bonds $= 400{,}000 \times 0.90 = 360{,}000$
2. Total $= 1{,}080{,}000$; equity weight $= 720{,}000 / 1{,}080{,}000 = 66.7\%$
3. Target equities $= 0.60 \times 1{,}080{,}000 = 648{,}000$; target bonds $= 432{,}000$
4. Sell $720{,}000 - 648{,}000 = 72{,}000$ of equities and buy 72,000 of bonds.

Rebalancing is a disciplined "sell what has risen, buy what has fallen" that keeps risk at the level in the IPS.

### Example C: The cost of fees

An investor puts 100,000 in a fund earning 7% a year before fees, for 10 years. Compare the result with no fees against a fund charging a 1.4% expense ratio (net return 5.6%).

- Without fees: $100{,}000 \times 1.07^{10} = 196{,}715.14$
- With a 1.4% expense ratio: $100{,}000 \times 1.056^{10} = 172{,}440.46$
- Difference: $196{,}715.14 - 172{,}440.46 = 24{,}274.68$

A seemingly small annual fee compounds into a large difference in terminal wealth.

### Example D: Net asset value

A mutual fund has assets of 520 million, liabilities of 20 million and 10 million shares outstanding.

$$NAV = \frac{520 - 20}{10} = 50.00 \text{ per share}$$

An investor who buys 200 shares from an open-end fund at NAV pays $200 \times 50 = 10{,}000$ (ignoring loads).

## Key takeaways

- The **portfolio perspective** judges holdings by their contribution to total risk and return. Expected return is a weighted average; risk is **lower** than the weighted average when correlation is below +1.
- Diversification reduces non-systematic risk but not market risk, and it may fail to protect in a crisis.
- The investment process has three steps: **planning** (IPS, capital market expectations, SAA), **execution** (asset allocation, security selection, portfolio construction) and **feedback** (monitoring, rebalancing, performance evaluation).
- Asset owners include retail investors and institutions (DB and DC pensions, endowments, sovereign wealth funds, insurers, banks); managers can be active or passive.
- **Open-end** mutual funds transact at NAV; **closed-end** funds and **ETFs** trade on exchanges, and ETF arbitrage by authorized participants keeps the price near NAV.
- SMAs hold securities directly for one client and can be customized; hedge funds and private capital are less liquid and offer flexible or specialized strategies at higher fees.
