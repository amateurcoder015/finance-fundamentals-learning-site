# Capital Structure

A company funds its assets with a mix of **debt** and **equity**. That mix is its **capital structure**. Financing choices change the firm's risk, the return shareholders demand and the taxes it pays. This chapter asks two questions: *does the mix affect firm value*, and if so, *what is the best mix*?

---

## 1. Capital Structure and the Cost of Capital

The firm's **weighted average cost of capital (WACC)** is the average cost of its financing sources, weighted by their market-value proportions:

$$\text{WACC} = \frac{E}{V}\,r_e + \frac{D}{V}\,r_d\,(1 - t)$$

where $V = D + E$ (market values), $r_e$ is the cost of equity, $r_d$ the pre-tax cost of debt and $t$ the marginal tax rate. WACC is the discount rate for the firm's free cash flows, so:

$$V = \frac{\text{FCFF}}{\text{WACC}} \quad \text{(for a no-growth perpetuity)}$$

**Lower WACC means higher firm value** (for the same operating cash flows). The **optimal capital structure** is the mix of debt and equity that **minimizes WACC** and therefore **maximizes firm value**. The **target capital structure** is the mix management aims for in the long run.

Two reasons debt looks cheaper than equity:

1. Debt holders have a prior claim and a contractual payment, so they demand a lower return than shareholders ($r_d < r_e$).
2. Interest is **tax-deductible**, so the after-tax cost is $r_d(1 - t)$.

But adding debt raises the risk of equity, so $r_e$ rises. The theories below explain how these effects net out.

## 2. Business Risk, Financial Risk and Leverage

| Risk | Meaning | Driven by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sales risk** | Uncertainty of revenue | Demand, competition, pricing |
| **Operating risk** | Uncertainty of operating income (EBIT) given sales | Cost structure: the share of **fixed operating costs** |
| **Business risk** | Sales risk plus operating risk | Industry and the firm's operations |
| **Financial risk** | Additional variability of net income and EPS from using **fixed financing costs** (interest) | Capital structure |

Total risk to shareholders $=$ business risk $+$ financial risk. A firm with high business risk usually should carry **less** debt, because it cannot also afford high financial risk.

### Degrees of leverage

$$\text{DOL} = \frac{\%\Delta \text{EBIT}}{\%\Delta \text{Sales}} = \frac{Q(P - V)}{Q(P - V) - F}$$

$$\text{DFL} = \frac{\%\Delta \text{EPS}}{\%\Delta \text{EBIT}} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{EBIT} - \text{Interest}}$$

$$\text{DTL} = \text{DOL} \times \text{DFL} = \frac{\%\Delta \text{EPS}}{\%\Delta \text{Sales}} = \frac{Q(P - V)}{Q(P - V) - F - \text{Interest}}$$

where $Q(P-V)$ is total contribution margin and $F$ is fixed operating cost.

**Worked example.** A firm has sales of \$1,000, variable costs of \$600, fixed operating costs of \$200 and interest expense of \$50.

1. Contribution margin $= 1000 - 600 = \mathdollar 400$.
2. EBIT $= 400 - 200 = \mathdollar 200$.
3. $\text{DOL} = 400 / 200 = 2.00$.
4. $\text{EBT} = 200 - 50 = \mathdollar 150$, so $\text{DFL} = 200 / 150 = 1.33$.
5. $\text{DTL} = 2.00 \times 1.33 = 2.67$ (check: $400 / 150 = 2.67$).

A 10% rise in sales raises EBIT by $2.00 \times 10\% = 20\%$ and EPS by $2.67 \times 10\% = 26.7\%$. The same leverage amplifies a 10% *fall* in sales to a 26.7% fall in EPS.

<Note>Operating leverage comes from fixed **operating** costs; financial leverage comes from fixed **financing** costs. Both magnify the effect of changes in sales on the earnings that shareholders receive.</Note>

## 3. Modigliani-Miller Propositions

Modigliani and Miller (MM) analyzed capital structure under idealized assumptions: no taxes, no transaction or bankruptcy costs, no agency costs, symmetric information, and the ability of investors to borrow at the same rate as the firm. They then relaxed the tax assumption.

### MM without taxes

- **Proposition I**: the **value of the firm is independent of its capital structure**. Value is determined by the firm's operating cash flows and business risk, not by how those flows are split between debt and equity.

$$V_L = V_U$$

Investors can create or undo the firm's leverage themselves ("homemade leverage"), so the firm cannot add value by doing it.

- **Proposition II**: the **cost of equity rises linearly with leverage**:

$$r_e = r_0 + (r_0 - r_d)\frac{D}{E}$$

where $r_0$ is the cost of capital of the **unlevered** firm. The higher cost of equity exactly offsets the use of cheaper debt, so **WACC is constant** and equals $r_0$ at every capital structure.

**Example (no taxes).** $r_0 = 10\%$, $r_d = 6\%$, $V = \mathdollar 700$m, $D = \mathdollar 300$m, so $E = \mathdollar 400$m.

1. $r_e = 10\% + (10\% - 6\%) \times 300/400 = 10\% + 3\% = 13\%$.
2. $\text{WACC} = (400/700)(13\%) + (300/700)(6\%) = 7.43\% + 2.57\% = 10.0\% = r_0$.

### MM with taxes

When interest is tax-deductible, debt creates a **tax shield** worth $t \times D$ (assuming permanent debt):

- **Proposition I (with taxes)**:

$$V_L = V_U + t\,D$$

Value rises with debt. Taken to the extreme, the firm would use almost 100% debt.

- **Proposition II (with taxes)**:

$$r_e = r_0 + (r_0 - r_d)(1 - t)\frac{D}{E}$$

The cost of equity still rises with leverage, but **more slowly** than without taxes, and WACC **falls** as debt rises:

$$\text{WACC} = r_0\left(1 - t\,\frac{D}{V}\right)$$

**Worked example (with taxes).** An all-equity firm has perpetual EBIT of \$100m, a tax rate $t = 30\%$ and an unlevered cost of equity $r_0 = 10\%$. It issues $D = \mathdollar 300$m of perpetual debt at $r_d = 6\%$ and uses the proceeds to repurchase shares.

1. Unlevered value: $V_U = \dfrac{100 \times (1 - 0.30)}{0.10} = \dfrac{70}{0.10} = \mathdollar 700\text{m}$.
2. Tax shield: $t \times D = 0.30 \times 300 = \mathdollar 90\text{m}$.
3. Levered value: $V_L = 700 + 90 = \mathdollar 790\text{m}$, so $E = 790 - 300 = \mathdollar 490\text{m}$.
4. Cost of equity: $r_e = 10\% + (10\% - 6\%)(1 - 0.30)\dfrac{300}{490} = 10\% + 2.8\% \times 0.6122 = 11.71\%$.
5. WACC: $\dfrac{490}{790}(11.71\%) + \dfrac{300}{790}(6\%)(0.70) = 7.27\% + 1.59\% = 8.86\%$.
6. Check with the shortcut: $10\% \times (1 - 0.30 \times 300/790) = 10\% \times 0.8861 = 8.86\%$. 
7. Check the value: $70 / 0.0886 = \mathdollar 790\text{m}$. 

Debt lowered WACC from $10.0\%$ to $8.86\%$ and added \$90m of value, all from the tax shield.

## 4. Costs of Financial Distress

MM with taxes predicts that firms should use almost all debt. They do not, because **debt also has costs**. **Financial distress** is the situation in which a firm struggles to meet its debt obligations.

| Cost | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct costs** | Legal, accounting and administrative fees of bankruptcy or restructuring |
| **Indirect costs** | Lost customers who doubt the firm will honor warranties, suppliers demanding cash terms, key employees leaving, forced asset sales, management distracted from operations |

The **expected cost of financial distress** depends on:

- the **probability** of distress, which rises with leverage and with business risk (high operating leverage, volatile cash flows, cyclical sales); and
- the **magnitude** of the costs if distress occurs, which is greater for firms with specialized or intangible assets (little liquidation value) than for firms with tangible assets that can be sold.

### Agency costs

Conflicts between managers, shareholders and creditors create **agency costs** that also depend on leverage:

- **Free cash flow problem**: managers with large free cash flow may waste it on empire building and perks. **More debt reduces this** by committing cash to interest and principal, which disciplines management.
- **Asset substitution**: once debt is in place, shareholders may favor riskier projects (they keep the upside, creditors bear the downside). Creditors anticipate this and demand higher yields or covenants.
- **Underinvestment (debt overhang)**: a heavily indebted firm may pass up positive-NPV projects because the benefits would mainly go to creditors.
- **Monitoring and bonding costs**: covenants, audits and reporting needed to control these conflicts.

## 5. Static Trade-Off Theory

The **static trade-off theory** combines MM's tax benefit with the costs of distress. Value of a levered firm:

$$V_L = V_U + PV(\text{tax shield}) - PV(\text{costs of financial distress})$$

- At low debt, the **tax shield dominates** and each extra unit of debt adds value.
- As debt rises, the **probability and cost of distress** (and agency costs of debt) rise at an increasing rate.
- The **optimal capital structure** is at the debt level where the **marginal benefit** of the tax shield equals the **marginal cost** of financial distress. This is also the point of **minimum WACC**.

Beyond the optimum, more debt reduces firm value. The optimum is a **range** rather than a single exact point, and it is lower for firms with high business risk or few tangible assets.

<Note>The theory is "static" because it treats the firm as choosing one target ratio and weighing costs and benefits at a point in time. It explains why firms in stable industries with tangible assets (utilities, real estate) use more debt than high-growth, intangible-heavy firms (software, biotech).</Note>

## 6. Pecking Order Theory

The **pecking order theory** (Myers and Majluf) says firms do not aim at a target ratio at all. Because managers know more about the firm than outside investors (**asymmetric information**), the choice of financing sends a signal and has a cost:

1. **Internal funds (retained earnings)** first: no signal, no issue cost.
2. **Debt** second: cheaper to issue than equity and a weaker negative signal.
3. **New equity last**: investors suspect managers issue equity when shares are **overvalued**, so the share price tends to fall on announcement.

Predictions: profitable firms borrow less because they have retained earnings; leverage rises when internal funds are insufficient to cover investment needs; and the observed debt ratio is the **cumulative result** of financing needs, not a target.

| | **Static trade-off** | **Pecking order** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is there an optimal D/E? | Yes: minimizes WACC | No: a by-product of financing needs |
| Main forces | Tax shield vs. distress and agency costs | Asymmetric information and issue costs |
| Most profitable firms | Borrow **more** (more tax to shield, lower distress risk) | Borrow **less** (use retained earnings) |

## 7. Target Capital Structure and Practical Considerations

A firm's **target capital structure** is set by management and the board with attention to:

- **Business risk** and operating leverage: higher business risk supports less debt.
- **Industry norms**: peers' debt ratios are a benchmark. Large departures need justification.
- **Asset tangibility and cash-flow stability**: tangible, predictable assets support more debt.
- **Taxes**: a high marginal tax rate raises the value of the interest tax shield.
- **Growth opportunities and need for flexibility**: firms want unused borrowing capacity ("financial flexibility") to exploit future opportunities.
- **Credit rating goals**: many firms aim to hold an investment-grade rating; this limits leverage (debt/EBITDA, interest coverage).
- **Market conditions**: relative cost and availability of debt versus equity at the time.
- **Management and shareholder preferences**: e.g., reluctance to dilute ownership, or the signaling effect of an equity issue.

Firms **evaluate** capital structure policy using market-based and accounting ratios (debt-to-equity, debt/EBITDA, interest coverage), by comparing with peers, and by monitoring credit rating and market-cost indicators. The firm's actual capital structure may deviate from the target at times (as share prices move or when it pursues a large acquisition), and management gradually rebalances back toward it.

**Differences across countries.** Capital structures vary internationally because of differences in legal systems and creditor protection, the **tax treatment** of interest and dividends, the development of **bond and equity markets**, the role of **banks** (bank-centered systems tend to see more debt financing), and the quality of accounting disclosure.

## Key takeaways

- **Optimal capital structure** minimizes WACC and maximizes firm value. $\text{WACC} = \frac{E}{V}r_e + \frac{D}{V}r_d(1-t)$.
- **Business risk** (sales plus operating risk) is separate from **financial risk** (fixed interest costs). $\text{DOL} = \frac{Q(P-V)}{Q(P-V)-F}$, $\text{DFL} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{EBIT}-I}$, $\text{DTL} = \text{DOL}\times\text{DFL}$.
- **MM without taxes**: $V_L = V_U$; $r_e = r_0 + (r_0 - r_d)\frac{D}{E}$; WACC is constant at $r_0$.
- **MM with taxes**: $V_L = V_U + tD$; $r_e = r_0 + (r_0 - r_d)(1-t)\frac{D}{E}$; WACC falls as debt rises, so taxes alone imply 100% debt.
- Real firms stop short because of **costs of financial distress** (direct and indirect) and **agency costs**. **Static trade-off**: $V_L = V_U + PV(\text{tax shield}) - PV(\text{distress costs})$, with the optimum where marginal benefit equals marginal cost.
- **Pecking order**: internal funds, then debt, then equity, because of asymmetric information. There is no target ratio.
- Practical targets reflect business risk, peers, tangibility, taxes, flexibility, credit rating goals and country-specific institutions.
