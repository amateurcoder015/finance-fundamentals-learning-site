# Asset-Backed Security Instrument and Market Features

The chapter on securitization explained the general structure: a seller transfers a pool of assets to a bankruptcy-remote **special purpose entity (SPE)**, which issues securities repaid from the pool's cash flows. This chapter looks at the instruments themselves. An **asset-backed security (ABS)** is a security backed by a pool of **non-mortgage** financial assets (auto loans, credit card receivables, student loans, equipment leases, corporate loans). Pools of mortgage loans create **mortgage-backed securities (MBS)**, which are covered separately.

The key question for each type of ABS is: **how do the collateral's cash flows behave** (amortizing or revolving, how it prepays, how it defaults) **and what protects the investor**?

---

## 1. The ABS Market: Collateral Types

| Collateral | Typical cash flow | Main risks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Auto loans and leases** | Fully amortizing, level monthly payments, short maturity (roughly 3–6 years) | Defaults and loss severity (car depreciates), some prepayment |
| **Credit card receivables** | Non-amortizing, revolving balances | Charge-offs, falling payment rates, yield compression |
| **Student loans** | Amortizing after a deferment period; government-guaranteed or private | Default (private loans), deferral, extension |
| **Equipment loans and leases** | Amortizing, fixed payments | Obligor default, residual value of leased equipment |
| **Corporate loans (CLOs)** | Floating-rate bullet loans, managed pool | Credit losses, manager skill, loan reinvestment |
| **Trade receivables, small business loans, others** | Short, self-liquidating or amortizing | Concentration, obligor credit |

ABS are usually issued in **tranches** with different seniority and are typically **rated**. Unlike mortgage pools, many ABS collateral pools have **little prepayment sensitivity**, so credit risk (defaults and loss severity) is the dominant concern.

### 1.1 Two ways to hold the collateral

- **Pool of homogeneous loans**: thousands of similar small loans (auto loans, cards). Performance is predicted statistically from historical pools.
- **Loan-by-loan / managed portfolio**: fewer, larger and heterogeneous loans (CLOs, some equipment deals). The manager's credit selection matters.

### 1.2 Amortizing versus non-amortizing (revolving) structures

- **Amortizing structure**: borrowers make scheduled interest and principal payments, plus any prepayments. All principal collected is passed through to investors. The pool balance declines to zero. Example: auto loan ABS.
- **Revolving structure**: principal collected during the **revolving (lockout) period** is used to buy **new receivables**, so the pool balance and the securities' principal stay constant. After this period, an **amortization (accumulation) period** begins and principal is returned to investors. Example: credit card ABS, and CLOs during the reinvestment period.

## 2. Auto Loan ABS

An auto loan pool consists of fixed-rate, level-payment, fully amortizing loans. Because each loan is small and the pool is large, the pool's payments are predictable apart from prepayments and defaults.

### 2.1 Prepayments and the ABS speed

Auto loan prepayments are small and driven by trade-ins, sales, insurance payoffs after accidents, and refinancing at lower rates. They are measured by the **absolute prepayment speed (ABS)**, which expresses prepayments as a percentage of the **original number of loans** each month. This is different from the conditional prepayment rate (CPR) used for mortgages, which is relative to the *current* balance.

The **single monthly mortality rate (SMM)**, the fraction of the *remaining* pool prepaying in month $t$, is

$$SMM_t = \frac{ABS}{1 - ABS\,(t - 1)}$$

**Worked example.** An auto pool prepays at **ABS = 1.5%** per month (that is, 1.5% of the original loans prepay each month).

- Month 1: $SMM_1 = 0.015 / (1 - 0.015 \times 0) = 1.50\%$.
- After 12 months, $12 \times 1.5\% = 18\%$ of the original loans have prepaid, leaving $82\%$ outstanding.
- Month 13: $SMM_{13} = 0.015 / (1 - 0.015 \times 12) = 0.015/0.82 = 1.829\%$.

Check: 1.5% of the *original* loans is $1.5/82 = 1.829\%$ of the *remaining* 82%. The SMM rises over time even though ABS is constant.

### 2.2 Credit protection

Losses depend on defaults and **loss severity** (the car's resale value is less than the loan balance). Investors are protected by **subordination**, **overcollateralization**, **excess spread** (the loans' coupons are above the securities' coupons) and **reserve accounts**.

## 3. Credit Card Receivable ABS

Credit card balances have no fixed maturity or amortization schedule: cardholders pay down and re-borrow. The securities therefore have a **revolving period** followed by an **amortization period**.

### 3.1 Cash flow components

- **Finance charge collections**: interest, fees. These pay interest on the notes, the servicing fee, and absorb charge-offs.
- **Principal collections**: reinvested in new receivables during the revolving period; passed to investors during amortization.
- **Charge-offs**: receivables written off as uncollectible.

The **monthly payment rate (MPR)** is principal collected divided by beginning pool balance. Higher MPR means faster repayment and shorter average life.

### 3.2 Excess spread and early amortization

**Excess spread (annualized, % of pool)**:

$$\text{Excess spread} = \text{portfolio yield} - \text{charge-offs} - \text{coupon on notes} - \text{servicing fee}$$

**Worked example.** A credit card trust has a portfolio yield of 16.0%, annualized charge-offs of 5.0%, a weighted average coupon on the notes of 4.5%, and a servicing fee of 1.5%.

$$\text{Excess spread} = 16.0 - 5.0 - 4.5 - 1.5 = 5.0\%$$

If charge-offs rise to 11.0%, excess spread becomes $16.0 - 11.0 - 4.5 - 1.5 = -1.0\%$ and the deal is under stress.

An **early amortization (pay-out) trigger** ends the revolving period early to protect investors. Typical triggers:

- the **three-month average excess spread falls below zero** (or a threshold);
- the **payment rate** falls below a minimum;
- credit enhancement drops below a required level;
- servicer or seller bankruptcy, or breach of covenants.

When triggered, all principal collections are used to repay the notes, normally **sequentially from senior to junior**. A pay-out shortens the notes' expected maturity, but it also reduces the risk that collateral deterioration reaches the notes.

<Note>
Credit card ABS carry little prepayment risk in the mortgage sense, because there is nothing to refinance. The relevant "timing" risks are early amortization (shorter life) and a slow payment rate (longer life).
</Note>

## 4. Collateralized Loan Obligations (CLOs)

A **collateralized loan obligation (CLO)** holds a managed portfolio of **senior secured leveraged loans** (typically floating rate) financed with tranches of notes and an **equity** tranche.

| Feature | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Collateral manager** | Actively buys and sells loans within covenant limits; a CLO is a *managed* vehicle |
| **Reinvestment period** | Several years in which repayments and sale proceeds are reinvested in new loans |
| **Amortization period** | After reinvestment, principal collected repays the notes in order of seniority |
| **Tranches** | Senior (AAA), mezzanine (AA to BB) notes, then **equity** (residual, first loss, receives the leftover cash) |
| **Coverage tests** | Overcollateralization (par value) and interest coverage tests that protect senior notes |

### 4.1 Coverage tests

- **Overcollateralization (OC / par value) test** for a tranche = collateral par value divided by the par value of that tranche *plus all tranches senior to it*.
- **Interest coverage (IC) test** = interest collected on the collateral divided by interest due on that tranche and all tranches senior to it.

If a test is **breached**, cash that would go to equity and junior notes is **diverted** to repay the senior notes until the test is cured.

**Worked example.** A CLO holds 500 million of loans, financed by 300 million Class A notes (4.0% coupon), 100 million Class B notes (6.0%) and 100 million equity. Loans earn 6.0%.

*OC ratios:*

- Class A: $500/300 = 166.7\%$.
- Class B: $500/(300+100) = 125.0\%$.

Suppose the Class B OC trigger is 120%. If 40 million of loans default with zero recovery, collateral par falls to 460 million:

$$\text{Class B OC} = \frac{460}{400} = 115.0\% < 120\%$$

The test fails, so cash flows that would go to equity are redirected to repay Class A (and then B) principal until the ratio is restored.

*Interest coverage (before the defaults):*

- Interest collected: $500 \times 6\% = 30$ million.
- Class B IC: $30 / (300 \times 4\% + 100 \times 6\%) = 30/(12 + 6) = 1.67\times$.

## 5. Credit Enhancement and Waterfalls in ABS

ABS rely on the same toolkit of credit enhancement as other securitizations, but different collateral emphasizes different tools:

| Tool | Auto loan ABS | Credit card ABS | CLO |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Subordination | Yes, senior/subordinate classes | Yes, class A/B/C notes | Yes, strongest feature |
| Overcollateralization | Common | Seller's interest, minimum | Par value tests |
| Excess spread | Yes (loan coupons exceed note coupons) | Central, drives trigger | Spread between loan yield and note cost; diverted when tests fail |
| Reserve accounts | Yes | Yes (spread account) | Sometimes |
| Triggers | Cumulative net loss, delinquency triggers | Early amortization | OC and IC tests |

A **waterfall** specifies the order: fees and expenses, then interest on the senior class, then junior classes, then principal, then excess to the residual. Under **sequential pay** principal goes to the most senior class first; under **pro rata** pay all classes receive principal in proportion until a trigger switches the deal to sequential.

## 6. Comparing ABS Types

| Aspect | Auto loan ABS | Credit card ABS | CLO |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Amortization | Amortizing | Revolving, then amortizing | Reinvestment, then amortizing |
| Prepayment risk | Small, measured by ABS | None of mortgage type | Loans can be repaid or refinanced; managed |
| Main risk | Defaults / loss severity | Charge-offs, payment rate | Credit losses and manager decisions |
| Coupon | Mostly fixed | Fixed or floating | Floating |
| Key protection | Subordination, excess spread | Excess spread, early amortization | OC and IC tests, subordination |

## Key takeaways

- **ABS** are backed by non-mortgage collateral; credit risk is usually more important than prepayment risk.
- **Amortizing** pools (auto loans) pass through principal and prepayments; **revolving** pools (credit cards, CLOs) reinvest principal during a lockout period, then amortize.
- Auto loan prepayments are measured by the **ABS speed**, relative to the *original* number of loans: $SMM_t = ABS/[1 - ABS(t-1)]$. With ABS = 1.5%, $SMM_{13} = 1.829\%$.
- Credit card **excess spread** = yield − charge-offs − note coupon − servicing fee (here 16.0 − 5.0 − 4.5 − 1.5 = 5.0%). **Early amortization triggers** (such as negative excess spread) protect investors.
- **CLOs** are managed loan portfolios financed by senior and mezzanine notes and equity; **OC and IC tests** divert cash from junior holders to senior notes when breached (in the example, Class B OC falls from 125% to 115%).
- Credit enhancement: **subordination**, **overcollateralization**, **excess spread**, **reserve accounts**, and **triggers**, applied differently by collateral type.
