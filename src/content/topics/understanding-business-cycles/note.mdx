# Understanding Business Cycles

Economies do not grow in a straight line. Output, employment, spending and inflation rise and fall in recurring but irregular **business cycles**. For an analyst, the position in the cycle drives earnings forecasts, credit quality, interest rates and the relative attractiveness of asset classes. This chapter covers the phases of the cycle, its main drivers, the labour-market and price measures used to describe it, and the indicators used to anticipate it.

---

## 1. Phases of the Business Cycle

A **business cycle** is a fluctuation in economic activity around its long-term growth trend. Cycles recur but are **not periodic**: their length and depth vary widely.

| Phase | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **Expansion** | Real GDP rises, employment grows, spending and investment increase. Inflation is usually low at first and rises as the expansion matures |
| **Peak** | Activity reaches its high point. Capacity is strained, and inflation and interest rates are often elevated. Growth is about to slow |
| **Contraction (recession)** | Real GDP falls, unemployment rises, spending and investment decline. Inflation tends to fall |
| **Trough** | Activity reaches its low point. The economy is about to recover |

A common rule of thumb defines a **recession** as two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. Official dating bodies (for example the NBER in the United States) use a broader judgement: a significant decline in activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months, looking at output, income, employment and sales. A very severe, prolonged contraction is a **depression**.

### Characteristics at each phase

| Variable | Early expansion | Late expansion / peak | Contraction | Trough |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Real GDP** | Accelerating | Growth slowing | Falling | Bottoming |
| **Employment** | Starts to recover (lags output) | Strong, labour tight | Falling | Still weak |
| **Inflation** | Low, may still be falling | Rising | Slowing (lags the cycle) | Low |
| **Interest rates** | Low | Rising | Falling | Low |
| **Consumer and business confidence** | Recovering | High | Falling | Low |
| **Inventories** | Run down, then rebuilt | Building up | Excess stock, cut production | Low |

Employment and inflation are **lagging** variables: they usually reach their turning points *after* output does. Firms are slow to hire early in a recovery (they first raise hours of existing staff) and slow to fire at the start of a slowdown.

## 2. What Drives the Cycle

Several sectors are especially cyclical.

- **Consumer spending**: the largest part of GDP in most economies. Consumers spend more when employment, income, wealth and confidence rise. Spending on **durable goods** (cars, appliances) is far more volatile than on non-durables and services, because it can be postponed.
- **Business investment (fixed capital)**: firms add capacity when they expect rising demand and when financing is cheap. Investment is very volatile and moves with expected profitability and capacity utilisation.
- **Housing**: housing activity responds strongly to **interest rates**, income growth, demographics and credit availability, and tends to **lead** the cycle. Large price swings affect household wealth and bank balance sheets.
- **Government**: fiscal policy (spending and taxes) and monetary policy can smooth or amplify the cycle.
- **External trade**: exports depend on foreign demand and on the exchange rate; a rising currency makes exports less competitive.

### The inventory cycle

Firms hold **inventories** to meet expected sales. The **inventory-to-sales ratio** drives short cycles of about 2 to 4 years:

1. Sales slow unexpectedly, so inventories build up and the ratio rises.
2. Firms cut orders and production to work stock down, which deepens the slowdown.
3. When the ratio falls below the desired level, firms raise production faster than sales just to restock, which helps launch the recovery.

Because production swings by more than final sales, inventory adjustment explains a large part of the change in GDP growth at turning points.

### The credit cycle

Credit expands in good times (easy lending standards, high collateral values) and contracts in bad times as lenders tighten. Credit booms can fuel asset-price bubbles and over-investment, and a credit crunch can turn a normal slowdown into a deep recession.

### Competing explanations of cycles

| School | Main explanation |
| --- | --- |
| **Neoclassical** | The economy is self-correcting: flexible wages and prices restore full employment, so cycles are caused by temporary shocks |
| **Keynesian** | Fluctuations in **aggregate demand** (driven by sentiment, "animal spirits") cause cycles; wages and prices adjust slowly, so recessions can persist and justify government stimulus |
| **Monetarist** | Variation in the **growth of the money supply** drives cycles; steady money growth would stabilise the economy |
| **Austrian** | Artificially low interest rates from central-bank credit expansion cause over-investment in unsustainable projects that must later be liquidated |
| **Real business cycle (RBC)** | Real shocks, especially to **technology** and productivity, cause cycles; the changes are efficient responses by rational agents |

## 3. Unemployment

### Definitions

- **Working-age population**: people old enough to work (for example 16 and over), excluding those in institutions.
- **Labour force**: people **employed** plus people **unemployed** (without work, available to work and actively seeking it).
- **Out of the labour force**: everyone else, such as students, retirees, and **discouraged workers** who have stopped looking.

$$\text{Unemployment rate} = \frac{\text{Unemployed}}{\text{Labour force}}$$

$$\text{Labour force participation rate} = \frac{\text{Labour force}}{\text{Working-age population}}$$

### Types of unemployment

| Type | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| **Frictional** | Normal job search and turnover (new graduates, people moving between jobs) |
| **Structural** | Mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs available, often caused by technological change or industry decline |
| **Cyclical** | Weak aggregate demand during a contraction |
| **Seasonal** | Predictable seasonal patterns (e.g., farm or holiday retail work) |

The **natural rate of unemployment** is the rate at "full employment": frictional plus structural unemployment, with **no cyclical unemployment**.

### Limits of the headline rate

The unemployment rate **understates** weakness when discouraged workers leave the labour force (they stop being counted as unemployed), and when people work part time but want full-time jobs (**underemployment**). It can also **overstate** weakness if some of those counted do not search seriously or work in the informal economy. Because of these effects, analysts also look at the participation rate, the employment-to-population ratio, hours worked and broader "underutilisation" measures.

### Worked example 1: unemployment and participation

An economy has a working-age population of 250 million, of whom 152 million are employed and 8 million are unemployed.

1. Labour force $= 152 + 8 = 160$ million.
2. Unemployment rate $= 8 / 160 = 5.0\%$.
3. Participation rate $= 160 / 250 = 64.0\%$.

Now suppose 2 million of the unemployed become discouraged and stop searching (employment unchanged).

1. Unemployed $= 8 - 2 = 6$ million; labour force $= 152 + 6 = 158$ million.
2. Unemployment rate $= 6 / 158 = 3.80\%$.
3. Participation rate $= 158 / 250 = 63.2\%$.

The unemployment rate **fell** from 5.0% to 3.8% although nobody found a job. The decline reflects exit from the labour force, so a falling unemployment rate alongside a falling participation rate should be read with caution.

## 4. Inflation, Deflation and Disinflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level, which reduces the purchasing power of money. The inflation rate is the percentage change in a price index:

$$\text{Inflation rate} = \frac{\text{Index}_t}{\text{Index}_{t-1}} - 1$$

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Inflation** | Price level is rising |
| **Disinflation** | Inflation is positive but **falling** (prices still rise, more slowly) |
| **Deflation** | Price level is falling (negative inflation), often with weak demand; it raises the real burden of debt and can trigger a deflationary spiral |
| **Hyperinflation** | Extremely rapid, accelerating inflation (a common rule of thumb is more than 50% per month) that destroys the value of money |

### Price indices

- **Consumer price index (CPI)**: cost of a fixed basket of goods and services bought by a typical household, relative to a base period. It is a **Laspeyres-type** index because the basket (quantities) is fixed:
$$\text{CPI} = \frac{\text{Cost of basket at current prices}}{\text{Cost of basket at base-period prices}} \times 100$$
- **Producer price index (PPI)**: prices received by producers at the factory gate; often a leading signal for consumer prices.
- **GDP deflator**: nominal GDP ÷ real GDP; covers all domestic output and uses a changing basket.
- **Headline inflation** includes all items; **core inflation** excludes volatile components, usually **food and energy**, and gives a cleaner view of the underlying trend.

A fixed basket suffers from three biases that tend to **overstate** measured inflation: **substitution bias** (consumers switch away from items whose prices rose), **new-product bias** (new goods enter the basket slowly), and **quality bias** (price rises that reflect better quality are counted as inflation).

### Causes of inflation

- **Demand-pull**: aggregate demand exceeds the economy's capacity, so too much spending chases too few goods (strong growth, rapid money growth, near full employment).
- **Cost-push**: higher input costs (wages, energy, raw materials) squeeze margins and are passed on to consumers; supply shocks can cause **stagflation**, with weak growth and high inflation together.

Expectations matter: if workers and firms expect high inflation, they build it into wage and price decisions, which makes inflation self-sustaining.

### Worked example 2: headline and core inflation

A simplified CPI basket contains food, fuel and services:

| Item | Quantity | Base price | Current price | Base cost | Current cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Food | 50 | \$4.00 | \$4.40 | \$200 | \$220 |
| Fuel | 10 | \$20.00 | \$23.00 | \$200 | \$230 |
| Services | 5 | \$40.00 | \$42.00 | \$200 | \$210 |
| **Total** | | | | **\$600** | **\$660** |

1. Headline CPI $= 660 / 600 \times 100 = 110.0$, i.e., headline inflation of **10.0%** versus the base period.
2. Core basket (excluding food and fuel) is services only: base \$200, current \$210, so core inflation is $210 / 200 - 1 = 5.0\%$.

Headline inflation is double core inflation because fuel (+15%) and food (+10%) rose faster than services (+5%). An analyst would judge the *underlying* trend closer to 5% if the food and fuel jumps look temporary.

## 5. Economic Indicators

An **economic indicator** is a statistic that shows the current or future state of the economy. Indicators are classified by timing relative to the cycle.

| Type | Timing | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Leading** | Turn **before** the economy | Stock prices, new orders for goods, building permits, the slope of the yield curve (long minus short rates), consumer expectations, average weekly manufacturing hours, initial jobless claims |
| **Coincident** | Turn **with** the economy | Employment (non-farm payrolls), industrial production, personal income, manufacturing and trade sales |
| **Lagging** | Turn **after** the economy | Average duration of unemployment, inventory-to-sales ratio, change in unit labour costs, average prime lending rate, commercial and industrial loans outstanding, consumer instalment credit relative to income, inflation (CPI services) |

- **Leading indicators** help forecast turning points but give **false signals**, and the lead time varies.
- **Coincident indicators** confirm the current phase and are used to date peaks and troughs.
- **Lagging indicators** confirm that a turn has happened.

Analysts combine many indicators into **composite indexes**, and also use **diffusion indexes**: the percentage of components that are rising. A diffusion index above 50% means more than half the components are improving.

A positively sloped yield curve (long rates well above short rates) generally signals expected growth; an **inverted** curve (short rates above long rates) has often preceded recessions.

### Worked example 3: tracking a composite

A composite leading index has ten components. Last month, 7 rose, 1 was unchanged and 2 fell. The diffusion index counts rising components (a common convention gives unchanged components half weight):

$$\text{Diffusion index} = \frac{7 + 0.5 \times 1}{10} = 75\%$$

A reading above 50% indicates broad improvement. If the diffusion index had been 30% for several months while the composite itself was still rising, that would warn that growth is becoming narrow and may be near a peak.

## 6. Business Cycles and Investing

- **Equities** are forward looking and tend to **lead** the cycle: they often bottom before the trough and peak before the economy does. Cyclical sectors (consumer discretionary, industrials, materials) are more sensitive than defensive sectors (utilities, staples, healthcare).
- **Interest rates and bonds**: central banks cut rates in contractions and raise them near peaks, so bond yields typically fall into recessions. Credit spreads **widen** when defaults rise in a contraction.
- **Corporate earnings and credit** are more volatile for firms with high **operating and financial leverage**.
- **Policy** responds to the cycle: expansionary monetary and fiscal policy in downturns and tightening near peaks, which shapes asset prices.

---

## Key takeaways

- The business cycle has four phases: **expansion, peak, contraction, trough**. Cycles are recurrent but not periodic.
- Consumer spending, business investment, housing, government and trade drive cycles; **inventory adjustment** and **credit conditions** amplify them.
- Unemployment rate $=$ unemployed ÷ labour force; participation rate $=$ labour force ÷ working-age population. A falling unemployment rate can reflect discouraged workers leaving the labour force.
- Unemployment is frictional, structural, cyclical or seasonal; the **natural rate** has no cyclical component.
- **Disinflation** is falling positive inflation; **deflation** is a falling price level. Headline inflation includes all items, core excludes food and energy.
- CPI $=$ basket cost now ÷ basket cost in the base period $\times$ 100; fixed baskets tend to overstate inflation (substitution, new-product and quality biases).
- **Leading** indicators turn before the economy, **coincident** with it and **lagging** after it; employment and inflation lag the cycle.
