# Fixed-Income Markets for Corporate Issuers

A company that needs cash can sell ownership (equity) or borrow (debt). Borrowing happens in the **fixed-income market**, where a borrower promises to pay interest and repay principal on set dates. This chapter looks at that market from the **corporate issuer's** side: which funding sources exist, how short-term and long-term needs are matched with different instruments, and how the bonds end up trading among investors.

---

## 1. The Global Fixed-Income Market

The market is large and is usually classified along several lines:

| Dimension | Categories |
| --- | --- |
| **Type of issuer** | Households (e.g., mortgages), non-financial corporations, financial corporations, sovereign and other government bodies (central governments, quasi-government agencies, local governments), and supranational institutions |
| **Credit quality** | **Investment grade** (Baa3/BBB− or higher) versus **high yield** or non-investment grade (below that) |
| **Maturity** | **Money market** (original maturity of one year or less) versus **capital market** (longer than one year) |
| **Currency** | Domestic currency, foreign currency, or a global market for the same issuer |
| **Coupon type** | Fixed-rate, floating-rate, zero-coupon |
| **Where issued** | Domestic market, foreign market, or the offshore (Eurobond) market |

Corporate issuers are split into **financial** issuers (banks, insurers, broker-dealers) and **non-financial** issuers (manufacturers, retailers, utilities). Banks are both the biggest corporate bond issuers and the lenders that provide credit to the rest.

A **benchmark** government yield curve underlies pricing. A corporate bond's yield is built up as

$$\text{Corporate yield} = \text{Benchmark yield} + \text{Spread}$$

where the **spread** compensates for credit risk, liquidity risk, and (in some cases) tax or other features. A wider spread means the market demands more compensation for risk.

---

## 2. Matching Funding to Needs

Companies need cash for two broad purposes, and good practice is to match the **tenor** of the funding with the **life of the need**:

- **Working capital and liquidity**: short-term, recurring needs (payroll, inventory) funded by short-term sources.
- **Investment and expansion**: long-lived assets, acquisitions and refinancing funded by long-term debt or equity.

Financing a long-lived asset with short-term debt creates **rollover (refinancing) risk**: the debt must be renewed in the future, possibly at higher rates or not at all.

## 3. Short-Term Funding for Corporate Issuers

### Bank lines of credit

A **line of credit** lets the company borrow from a bank up to a limit. Types differ in how strong the bank's promise is:

| Type | Bank's obligation | Cost | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Uncommitted line** | None: the bank may refuse or withdraw | Cheapest; usually no commitment fee | Occasional, small needs |
| **Committed line** | Legally binding up to the limit for a stated term | **Commitment fee** on the undrawn amount, plus interest on the drawn amount | Reliable liquidity |
| **Revolving credit facility (revolver)** | Committed, multi-year; borrowings can be repaid and **re-borrowed** | Commitment fee on the undrawn portion plus interest on the drawn portion at a floating **reference rate + spread** | Core liquidity backstop for large corporates |

Revolvers usually carry **covenants** (limits on leverage or required coverage ratios), which gives lenders early warning and a seat at the table if credit quality worsens.

### Commercial paper (CP)

**Commercial paper** is short-term, **unsecured** debt sold by large, high-quality companies directly (or through dealers) to money market investors.

- Sold on a **discount** basis: the investor pays less than face value and receives face value at maturity.
- In the United States, CP with a maturity of **270 days or less** is exempt from SEC registration, so most CP is issued for shorter periods (often under 90 days). Eurocommercial paper (ECP) is issued outside the home market and can have somewhat longer maturities (up to about a year).
- **Asset-backed commercial paper (ABCP)** is issued by a special vehicle and secured by a pool of financial assets.
- Issuers normally keep a **backstop line** from banks equal to the CP outstanding. If investors will not roll over the CP (a "run"), the issuer draws the line. For this reason CP is strongly tied to the issuer's credit rating and market confidence.

CP is cheaper than a bank loan for strong issuers because it removes the bank's intermediation margin, but the issuer must bear rollover risk.

### Repurchase agreements

A **repo** is a short-term collateralized loan: one party sells a security and agrees to buy it back at a higher price later. The price difference is the interest. Financial firms use repos heavily to fund bond inventory; non-financial corporates use them less.

## 4. Long-Term Funding for Corporate Issuers

### Bank term loans and syndicated loans

A **term loan** has a fixed amount and maturity and is repaid over time or at maturity. A **syndicated loan** is arranged by one or more **lead (arranger) banks** and sold to a **group of lenders**, so no single bank carries the whole exposure. Features:

- Usually **floating-rate**: reference rate + a credit spread that is stated in basis points. The reference rate resets periodically (for example every 3 months), so interest cost moves with market rates.
- Can be **secured** (liens on assets) and carry **covenants**.
- Lenders can trade syndicated loans in a **secondary loan market**, although it is generally less liquid and less standardized than bonds.

**Leveraged loans** are loans to borrowers with below-investment-grade ratings or high leverage. They are typically senior secured and floating-rate, and their spreads are much wider than for investment grade loans. **Private credit** (direct lending by non-bank funds) has grown as an alternative to banks and public markets, offering speed and customized terms at a higher cost.

### Corporate bonds

A **corporate bond** is a security in which the issuer borrows from many investors. Compared with a bank loan:

| Feature | Bank/syndicated loan | Public bond |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of lenders | One or a few | Many investors |
| Rate | Usually floating | Usually fixed |
| Flexibility to renegotiate | High (few parties) | Low (many holders, trustee) |
| Tradability | Limited | Tradable in the secondary market |
| Covenants | Usually tighter, with maintenance tests | Typically looser (incurrence-based) |
| Cost of issuing | Lower fixed cost | Higher fees, ratings, documentation |

Bonds are issued with a **maturity** (commonly 2 to 30 years), a **coupon**, and often **seniority** and security features. **Investment grade** bonds are rated **Baa3/BBB− or above** and are mostly unsecured; **high-yield** (speculative or "junk") bonds are rated below that, pay higher coupons and often have more restrictive covenants and security.

A bond that drops from investment grade to high yield is a **fallen angel**; one that rises into investment grade is a **rising star**. Because many investors can hold only investment-grade debt, a downgrade across that line can cause forced selling.

### Credit ratings

Rating agencies assess the issuer's ability and willingness to repay.

| | Moody's | S&P and Fitch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Highest quality | Aaa | AAA |
| Investment grade (lowest) | Baa3 | BBB− |
| High yield (highest) | Ba1 | BB+ |

Ratings drive both access to the market and the spread: lower-rated issuers pay a wider spread over the benchmark.

## 5. Issuing and Trading Corporate Bonds

**Primary market.** Most large corporate bonds are sold through an **underwritten public offering**: an investment bank buys the entire issue and resells it, bearing the placement risk. Smaller or lower-quality deals may use **best efforts** or **private placement**. A frequent issuer may use **shelf registration** to register once and issue in tranches later.

**Secondary market.** Corporate bonds trade mainly **over the counter** with dealers quoting bid and ask prices, often through electronic platforms. Compared with government bonds:

- There are far **more distinct issues**, and many trade rarely.
- **Liquidity is lower** and **bid-ask spreads are wider**, especially for high-yield and small issues, and liquidity falls sharply in stress.
- Without a recent trade, dealers use **matrix pricing**: estimating a price from similar, more actively traded bonds.
- Post-trade reporting systems (for example TRACE in the United States) publish trade prices and improve transparency.

Part of the corporate spread is therefore a **liquidity premium**, in addition to compensation for default risk.

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example A: Cost of a revolving credit facility

A company has a 100 million revolver priced at SOFR + 1.50%. SOFR is 4.00%. The bank charges a **0.25% commitment fee** on the undrawn amount. During the year the company has 40 million drawn throughout.

1. Rate on drawn amount $= 4.00\% + 1.50\% = 5.50\%$
2. Interest on drawn $= 40 \times 5.50\% = 2.20$ million
3. Undrawn amount $= 100 - 40 = 60$ million
4. Commitment fee $= 60 \times 0.25\% = 0.15$ million
5. Total cost $= 2.20 + 0.15 = 2.35$ million
6. Effective cost of the borrowed funds $= 2.35 / 40 = 5.875\%$

The effective cost exceeds the stated 5.50% because the company pays a fee to keep the unused capacity available.

### Example B: Pricing commercial paper

A company issues 50 million face value of 90-day CP at a **discount rate** of 5.20% (360-day year).

$$P = F \times \left(1 - d \times \frac{t}{360}\right)$$

1. Discount $= 0.0520 \times 90 / 360 = 0.0130$
2. Price $= 50 \times (1 - 0.0130) = 49.35$ million
3. Interest earned by the investor $= 50 - 49.35 = 0.65$ million

The investor's **money market yield** is measured on the amount invested, not on face value:

$$\text{MMY} = \frac{F - P}{P} \times \frac{360}{t} = \frac{0.65}{49.35} \times \frac{360}{90} = 0.013171 \times 4 = 5.27\%$$

The yield (5.27%) is higher than the 5.20% discount rate because the discount rate divides by the face value, which is larger than the amount actually paid.

### Example C: Building a corporate yield

A 10-year corporate bond is rated BBB. The 10-year government benchmark yields 4.20% and the market spread for BBB issuers is 135 basis points.

$$\text{Yield} = 4.20\% + 1.35\% = 5.55\%$$

If the bond is downgraded and the spread widens to 220 bp, the yield rises to 6.40% and the bond's price falls.

### Example D: Interest on a floating-rate syndicated loan

A 200 million syndicated term loan pays reference rate + 275 bp. The reference rate resets to 4.30%.

1. All-in rate $= 4.30\% + 2.75\% = 7.05\%$
2. Annual interest $= 200 \times 7.05\% = 14.10$ million

If the reference rate rises by 100 bp, interest cost rises by $200 \times 1.00\% = 2.00$ million per year. The borrower bears the interest-rate risk on floating-rate debt, while a fixed-rate bond transfers it to investors.

## Key takeaways

- The fixed-income market is classified by issuer, credit quality, maturity (money versus capital market), currency and coupon type. Investment grade means **Baa3/BBB− or higher**.
- Corporate yield $=$ benchmark yield $+$ spread; the spread compensates for credit and liquidity risk.
- Short-term funding: **uncommitted** lines (no obligation), **committed** lines and **revolvers** (commitment fee on the undrawn amount), **commercial paper** (unsecured, discount basis, backed by bank lines) and **repos** (collateralized).
- Long-term funding: bank term loans, **syndicated** and **leveraged** loans (usually floating-rate, secured, with covenants), **private credit** and **corporate bonds** (usually fixed-rate, many holders, tradable).
- Match funding tenor to the asset life to limit **rollover risk**.
- Corporate bonds trade over the counter with wider bid-ask spreads and less liquidity than government bonds; **matrix pricing** is used when there is no recent trade.
- Fallen angels (downgraded to high yield) can face forced selling by investors limited to investment grade.
