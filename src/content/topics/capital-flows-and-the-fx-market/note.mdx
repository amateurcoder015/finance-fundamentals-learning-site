# Capital Flows and the FX Market

Money moves across borders to pay for goods and services, to buy assets, and to fund investment. The **foreign exchange (FX) market** is where one currency is exchanged for another, and **capital flows** are the cross-border movements of money for investment. Together they determine exchange rates, shape trade and growth, and affect the returns of every investor with foreign assets or liabilities. This chapter covers the structure of the FX market, how exchange rates are quoted and measured, the regimes under which they are set, and the balance of payments that records the flows.

---

## 1. The Foreign Exchange Market

### 1.1 Functions

The FX market is the largest financial market by turnover, trading several trillion US dollars a day, and it operates around the clock across dealing centres in different time zones. Its main functions are to:

- **Facilitate international trade** by converting currency to pay for imports and receive export proceeds.
- **Facilitate cross-border investment** by letting investors buy foreign securities, firms invest abroad and fund managers repatriate returns.
- **Allow hedging** of currency risk from foreign-currency receivables, payables and assets.
- **Allow speculation** and **price discovery**: trading on views about currencies, which also helps set the exchange rate.

### 1.2 Participants

| Group | Who | Typical motive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sell side** | Large banks acting as dealers, making two-way prices | Earn the bid-offer spread, manage inventory |
| **Buy side: corporations** | Importers, exporters, multinationals | Pay for trade, hedge receivables and payables |
| **Buy side: real money** | Mutual funds, pension funds, insurers, sovereign wealth funds | Buy foreign assets, hedge portfolio currency risk |
| **Buy side: leveraged accounts** | Hedge funds, proprietary traders | Speculation, carry trades, arbitrage |
| **Retail** | Individuals, tourists, remitters | Travel, remittances, small speculation |
| **Governments and central banks** | Treasuries, central banks | Manage reserves, intervene to influence the rate |

### 1.3 Instruments

- **Spot**: exchange of currencies at the current rate, settling typically two business days later (some pairs settle the next day).
- **Forward**: an agreement to exchange currencies at a fixed rate on a future date.
- **FX swap**: a spot trade combined with an offsetting forward trade (buy now, sell later, or the reverse). It is widely used for funding and rolling hedges, and is the largest segment by volume.
- **Currency swap**: exchange of principal and interest payments in two currencies over several years.
- **Futures and options**: exchange-traded or OTC contracts; options give the right but not the obligation to exchange at a set rate.
- **Non-deliverable forward (NDF)**: a forward on a restricted currency, settled in cash in a convertible currency (usually USD) based on the difference between the agreed and the prevailing rate.

## 2. Quoting Exchange Rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. Using the notation **price currency / base currency (P/B)**, a quote of $\text{USD/EUR} = 1.10$ means **1 euro (the base currency) costs 1.10 US dollars (the price currency)**. The base currency is always one unit; the price currency is the number of units paid.

- A **direct quote** states the price of one unit of *foreign* currency in *domestic* currency. For a US investor, USD/EUR is a direct quote.
- An **indirect quote** states the price of one unit of *domestic* currency in foreign currency (EUR/USD for a US investor).
- The two are reciprocals: $\text{EUR/USD} = 1 / \text{USD/EUR}$.

A dealer quotes a **bid** (the price at which the dealer buys the base currency) and an **offer** or ask (the price at which the dealer sells the base currency). The difference is the **bid-offer spread**, measured in **pips** (usually 0.0001 for a major pair).

**Worked example.** A dealer quotes USD/EUR 1.1048 / 1.1052.

1. The dealer buys 1 EUR at 1.1048 USD and sells 1 EUR at 1.1052 USD.
2. Spread $= 1.1052 - 1.1048 = 0.0004$, which is 4 pips.
3. A client who buys EUR 1,000,000 pays $1{,}000{,}000 \times 1.1052 = 1{,}105{,}200$ USD. A client who sells EUR 1,000,000 receives $1{,}000{,}000 \times 1.1048 = 1{,}104{,}800$ USD. The round-trip cost of buying and immediately selling is \$400, the dealer's spread revenue.

Spreads are narrow for liquid pairs and larger for exotic currencies, large sizes, or volatile times.

### 2.1 Percentage change in a currency

A currency **appreciates** when it buys more units of the other currency, and **depreciates** when it buys fewer. The rate is always the price of the **base** currency, so the percentage change in the base currency is:

$$\%\Delta\,\text{base currency} = \frac{S_1}{S_0} - 1$$

where $S$ is the price (P/B) quote. The change in the *price* currency is the reverse, computed from the reciprocal quote.

**Worked example.** USD/EUR rises from 1.1000 to 1.1550.

1. The euro (base) appreciates by $1.1550 / 1.1000 - 1 = 0.05 = \mathbf{5.0\%}$ against the dollar.
2. The dollar value of one euro rose, so the dollar now buys fewer euros. In EUR/USD terms: initially $1 / 1.1000 = 0.9091$; now $1 / 1.1550 = 0.8658$.
3. The dollar depreciates by $0.8658 / 0.9091 - 1 = -4.76\%$ against the euro.

<Note>A 5% appreciation of one currency is not a 5% depreciation of the other. Appreciation is $+5.0\%$ for the euro but the dollar falls by $1 - 1/1.05 = 4.76\%$. Always take the percentage change of the *base* currency from the quote as given.</Note>

## 3. Nominal and Real Exchange Rates

The **nominal exchange rate** is the rate quoted in the market. The **real exchange rate** adjusts it for price levels in the two countries and indicates the relative purchasing power of the currencies.

With domestic currency as the price currency, $S_{d/f}$ the nominal rate (units of domestic currency per unit of foreign), and price indices $P_d$ and $P_f$:

$$\text{Real exchange rate}_{d/f} = S_{d/f} \times \frac{P_f}{P_d}$$

An increase in the real rate means foreign goods have become more expensive relative to domestic goods (a **real depreciation of the domestic currency**), and it makes the home country more competitive.

**Worked example.** In the base year both price indices are 100 and USD/EUR is 1.00. Now USD/EUR is 1.10, the US price index is 110 and the euro-area price index is 104.

1. Real rate $= 1.10 \times 104 / 110 = 114.4 / 110 = 1.04$.
2. The base-year real rate was 1.00, so the euro has appreciated by about 4% in **real** terms (10% nominally, less the 5.77% higher US inflation relative to euro-area inflation: $110/104 = 1.0577$).
3. Euro-area goods are about 4% more expensive for US buyers than in the base year, which tends to hurt euro-area competitiveness.

A **nominal depreciation of the domestic currency** (higher $S_{d/f}$) or **lower domestic inflation relative to abroad** (lower $P_d/P_f$ growth) raises the real exchange rate; domestic inflation with a constant nominal rate lowers it. In the long run, relative price levels and the nominal rate tend to adjust (the idea behind purchasing power parity), but large deviations can persist for years.

## 4. Exchange Rate Regimes

A country chooses how much its exchange rate is set by the market and how much by policy. From the least to the most flexible, the regimes are:

| Regime | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Formal dollarization / no separate legal tender** | The country uses another country's currency (or a currency union shares one currency, such as the euro). No independent monetary policy. |
| **Currency board** | The domestic currency is issued only against a fixed amount of a foreign currency at a fixed rate; the central bank has no discretion to create base money beyond reserves. |
| **Conventional fixed peg** | The rate is fixed to another currency or basket, within a narrow band (usually $\pm 1\%$). The central bank defends it using reserves and interest rates. |
| **Target zone** | A wider band around a central rate; the central bank intervenes only near the edges. |
| **Crawling peg** | The rate is adjusted periodically in small steps, in a pre-announced path or in response to inflation differentials. |
| **Crawling band** | A band around a central rate that is itself adjusted gradually. |
| **Managed float** | The market sets the rate, but the central bank intervenes without a precise target path to smooth or steer it. |
| **Independent float** | The rate is determined by the market; intervention is rare and aimed at extreme volatility. |

**Central bank intervention** means buying or selling foreign exchange. Buying foreign currency weakens the domestic currency and adds to foreign reserves; selling foreign currency supports the domestic currency and draws reserves down. If the central bank does not want to change the domestic money supply, it can **sterilise** the intervention with offsetting open-market operations.

### The impossible trinity

A country cannot have all three at once:

1. A **fixed exchange rate**,
2. **Free capital mobility**, and
3. An **independent monetary policy**.

It must give up one. A currency union or a hard peg with open capital gives up monetary independence; a floating currency with open capital keeps independent policy; a country that fixes its rate and wants its own monetary policy must use **capital controls**.

## 5. The Balance of Payments

The **balance of payments (BOP)** records all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world over a period. It has three main accounts:

| Account | What it records |
| --- | --- |
| **Current account** | Trade in goods and services, primary income (investment income and compensation of employees) and secondary income (transfers such as remittances and aid) |
| **Capital account** | Capital transfers and the purchase or sale of non-produced, non-financial assets (a small account) |
| **Financial account** | Cross-border investment flows: **direct investment** (lasting control), **portfolio investment** (stocks and bonds without control), other investment (loans, deposits) and **reserve assets** |

Because every transaction has two sides, the three accounts together sum to zero (apart from statistical errors and omissions):

$$\text{Current account} + \text{Capital account} + \text{Financial account} = 0$$

A country with a **current account deficit** (it imports more than it earns from exports and net income) must have a net surplus in the capital and financial accounts, that is, a net **capital inflow**: foreigners lend to it or buy its assets, or it draws down reserves.

**Worked example.** A country runs a current account balance of $-80$ billion and a capital account balance of $+5$ billion. Private net capital inflows (direct and portfolio investment and loans) are $+60$ billion.

1. The financial account must offset the others: $-80 + 5 + \text{FA} = 0$, so $\text{FA} = +75$ billion.
2. Private inflows supply only 60, so the remaining $75 - 60 = 15$ billion is met by the **official reserves**: the central bank sells 15 billion of foreign-currency reserves (recorded as a positive financial account item).
3. Selling reserves supports the domestic currency; if reserves run low the country may have to let the currency depreciate or tighten policy.

### 5.1 The saving-investment identity

The current account also equals the gap between national saving and domestic investment:

$$\text{CA} = (S_{private} - I) + (T - G)$$

where $T - G$ is the government budget balance (taxes minus spending). A country that invests more than it saves, or that runs a budget deficit, tends to run a current account deficit.

**Example (as % of GDP).** Private saving is 18%, investment is 22% and the government deficit is 3% ($T - G = -3\%$). Then $\text{CA} = (18 - 22) + (-3) = -7\%$ of GDP: the country must borrow from abroad 7% of GDP.

## 6. Capital Flows and Their Effects

**Foreign direct investment (FDI)** is a lasting investment in a foreign firm that gives control or significant influence (commonly 10% or more of voting shares). It is long-term and relatively stable. **Portfolio flows** (foreign purchases of equities and bonds) are more liquid and can reverse quickly.

**What drives capital flows**

- Higher expected **returns**: faster growth, higher interest rates or valuations.
- **Interest rate differentials** and expected currency moves (carry trades).
- **Risk appetite**: capital leaves emerging markets in global risk-off periods.
- Institutional factors: property rights, openness, capital controls and political stability.

**Effects of large capital inflows on the recipient country**

- Appreciation of the currency, which can erode export competitiveness.
- Lower domestic interest rates and faster credit growth, which can inflate asset prices.
- Higher investment and growth when inflows fund productive capital.
- **Sudden stops or reversals**: when inflows end abruptly, the currency falls, interest rates spike and banks and firms with foreign-currency debt are hit. This is the typical pattern in emerging-market crises.

Governments may respond with **capital controls** (taxes or limits on inflows or outflows), intervention in the FX market, macro-prudential rules, or by building up reserves.

## 7. Exchange Rates, Trade and the Economy

A depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, which tends to improve the trade balance. Whether it does depends on how responsive demand is.

**Marshall-Lerner condition.** Starting from balanced trade, a depreciation improves the trade balance if the sum of the absolute values of the price elasticities of export demand and import demand is greater than 1:

$$|\varepsilon_X| + |\varepsilon_M| > 1$$

In the short run, contracts and habits make demand inelastic, so the balance can worsen first and improve later, tracing a **J-curve**.

Other effects of exchange rates:

- **Inflation**: a depreciation raises import prices and can add to domestic inflation; an appreciation lowers it.
- **Monetary policy**: central banks watch exchange rates because a strong or weak currency changes inflation and growth outcomes.
- **Corporate and investor returns**: foreign earnings are translated into the home currency, so currency moves change reported revenue and portfolio returns, and firms with foreign-currency debt face balance-sheet risk.

## Key takeaways

- The FX market facilitates trade and investment, hedging and speculation; dealers make two-way prices and the spot, forward and swap markets are linked.
- In the P/B convention, USD/EUR $= 1.10$ means 1 EUR costs 1.10 USD. The percentage change in the base currency is $S_1/S_0 - 1$, and the other currency's change is computed from the reciprocal.
- Real exchange rate $= S_{d/f} \times P_f / P_d$: it adjusts the nominal rate for relative price levels.
- Regimes range from dollarization and currency boards through pegs, bands and managed floats to independent floats. The impossible trinity allows only two of: a fixed rate, free capital flows and independent monetary policy.
- BOP: current account + capital account + financial account $= 0$. A current account deficit is financed by net capital inflows or by drawing down reserves, and $\text{CA} = (S_{private} - I) + (T - G)$.
- Capital inflows can strengthen the currency and fuel credit booms, and sudden stops can trigger crises; FDI is more stable than portfolio flows.
- A depreciation improves the trade balance only if elasticities are high enough (the Marshall-Lerner condition), and the J-curve describes the usual short-run lag.
