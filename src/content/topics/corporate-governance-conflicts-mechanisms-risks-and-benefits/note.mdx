# Corporate Governance: Conflicts, Mechanisms, Risks, and Benefits

**Corporate governance** is the system of internal controls and procedures by which a company is managed and overseen. It defines the **rights, roles and responsibilities** of the groups with a stake in the firm (shareholders, the board, managers, creditors, employees and others) and the rules for how decisions are made and how those making them are held to account. The conflicts that governance must manage are described in *Investors and Other Stakeholders*; this chapter covers the structures built to manage them, and what is gained or lost depending on how well they work.

---

## 1. Objectives and Principles of Good Governance

Governance aims to:

- **Align the interests** of managers, directors and shareholders, and protect other stakeholders;
- **Reduce agency costs** by making managerial behaviour observable and accountable;
- Ensure **reliable information** reaches investors; and
- Support **long-term value creation** within the law and ethical norms.

The widely used principles are:

| Principle | Meaning in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Fairness** | All shareholders, including minority holders, are treated equitably; related-party dealings are on arm's-length terms |
| **Transparency** | Timely, accurate disclosure of financial results, ownership, pay and risks |
| **Accountability** | Managers answer to the board, and the board answers to shareholders; clear consequences for poor performance |
| **Responsibility** | Directors and managers act in the long-term interest of the company, comply with the law and respect stakeholders |

Governance frameworks differ across countries. Some rely on detailed **legal rules**, others on **comply-or-explain** codes (a company must follow the code or disclose and justify any departure). Ownership also differs: dispersed ownership (common in the US and UK) puts the main conflict between managers and shareholders; concentrated or family ownership (common in much of Asia and continental Europe) puts it between controlling and minority shareholders.

## 2. The Board of Directors

The **board** is elected by shareholders to oversee management, approve strategy, appoint and dismiss the CEO, set (or approve) executive pay, and ensure the integrity of financial reporting and risk management. Directors owe **fiduciary duties**: the **duty of care** (act with the diligence of a prudent person) and the **duty of loyalty** (put the company's interests above their own).

### Board structures

| | **One-tier (unitary) board** | **Two-tier board** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Structure** | A single board containing both executive and non-executive directors | A **supervisory board** (oversight, often includes employee and creditor representatives) above a **management board** (day-to-day running) |
| **Typical countries** | US, UK, Canada, India | Germany, the Netherlands, many Nordic and Asian firms |
| **Strength** | Close contact between directors and management; speed | Clear separation of oversight from management; wider stakeholder voice |
| **Weakness** | Risk of management dominance, especially when the CEO is also chair | Information may flow slowly to the supervisory board |

### Independence and composition

**Independent directors** have no material business, family or employment ties to the company or its management, so they can challenge management objectively. Good practice includes:

- A **majority** (or at least a substantial proportion) of independent directors;
- **Separating the roles of chair and CEO**, or appointing a lead independent director;
- Directors with relevant skills, diversity of background and enough time (limits on the number of other boards);
- Avoiding **staggered (classified) boards**, where only part of the board stands for election each year; they reduce accountability and make a hostile takeover or proxy contest harder.

**Worked example: independence ratio.** A 12-member board has 5 executives and 7 non-executives. Of the non-executives, 1 is a former executive who left two years ago and 1 is a major supplier's director. Only the remaining 5 are independent.

- Independent share $= 5/12 = 41.7\%$ (not $7/12 = 58.3\%$).

Judged on independence rather than on title, the board has no independent majority, which weakens its ability to challenge management.

### Board committees

Committees concentrate specialist work, and the key ones should be made up **entirely or mainly of independent directors**:

| Committee | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| **Audit** | Integrity of financial statements, internal controls, relationship with the external auditor, whistleblowing |
| **Remuneration (compensation)** | Executive pay design and its link to long-term performance |
| **Nomination (governance)** | Selecting and evaluating directors and succession planning |
| **Risk** | Oversight of risk appetite, risk limits and enterprise risk management |
| **Investment / strategy** | Review of major capital allocation and acquisitions (where used) |

## 3. Shareholder Rights and Voting

Shareholders exercise control mainly through **voting**: electing directors, approving mergers and major transactions, appointing auditors, approving certain pay arrangements and changing the charter. Rights usually include voting, pre-emptive rights, information rights, and the right to sue directors.

### Voting methods

- **Statutory (majority) voting:** each share gets one vote **per seat**. Each seat is decided separately, so a holder with more than 50% of the shares can elect **every** director.
- **Cumulative voting:** a shareholder may pool all their votes (shares $\times$ number of seats) and put them on **one or a few candidates**. This helps a minority obtain board representation.
- **Proxy voting:** shareholders who do not attend the meeting authorize someone else (a **proxy**) to vote. A **proxy contest** is a campaign by a dissident to collect proxies and win seats or a vote against management.
- **Say-on-pay:** a (usually advisory) shareholder vote on executive compensation.

**Worked example: cumulative voting.** A company has 10,000,000 shares and elects 4 directors. A minority investor holds 2,500,000 shares (25%); the remaining 7,500,000 shares act together.

*Statutory voting.* On each of the 4 seats the majority bloc casts $7.5\text{m}$ votes against $2.5\text{m}$, so the minority wins **no seats**.

*Cumulative voting.*

1. Total votes $= 10\text{m} \times 4 = 40\text{m}$. Minority votes $= 2.5\text{m} \times 4 = 10\text{m}$; majority votes $= 7.5\text{m} \times 4 = 30\text{m}$.
2. The minority puts all 10m on one candidate.
3. To push the minority candidate out of the top 4, the majority would need four candidates each with more than 10m votes, which takes more than 40m votes. It has only 30m.
4. The minority therefore **wins one seat**.

The minimum shares needed to guarantee one seat: $\dfrac{\text{shares outstanding}}{\text{seats} + 1} + 1 = \dfrac{10\text{m}}{5} + 1 = 2{,}000{,}001$ shares. The 25% holder clears that bar; a holder of exactly 20% (2,000,000 shares) could be tied out by a majority that spreads its votes over four candidates.

### Shareholder activism and the market for control

Dissatisfied shareholders can sell the shares ("vote with their feet"), but can also act: engage privately with the board, submit **shareholder proposals**, withhold votes, run a **proxy contest**, or in extreme cases pursue litigation. **Activist investors** buy a stake to push for change in strategy, capital structure or management.

The **market for corporate control** is a governance mechanism too: a poorly run company with a depressed share price attracts takeover bids that replace management. Companies sometimes adopt **anti-takeover devices** (staggered boards, poison pills, supermajority requirements, dual-class shares). These protect managers and so can weaken governance, although they are also defended as a means to resist low-ball offers.

## 4. Governance Mechanisms Against Conflicts

| Conflict | Governance response |
| --- | --- |
| **Managers pursue their own interests** (empire building, perquisites, short-termism) | Independent board and audit committee; pay tied to long-term results (restricted stock, multi-year targets, clawbacks, ownership requirements); say-on-pay |
| **Controlling versus minority shareholders** | Independent directors and committees to approve **related-party transactions**; cumulative voting; minority approval requirements; full disclosure |
| **Shareholders versus creditors** | Debt covenants, seniority and collateral; board duties that extend to creditors near insolvency |
| **Misleading information** | Independent external auditors, audit committee, internal controls over reporting, whistleblower channels, regulator oversight |
| **Excess risk or poor risk oversight** | Risk committee and a chief risk officer; risk-sensitive pay design |

**Worked example: a related-party transaction.** A controlling shareholder owns **60%** of Company A and **100%** of Supplier S. A buys 1,000,000 units a year from S at **120** per unit, when the market price is **100**.

1. Excess cost to A $= 1\text{m} \times (120 - 100) = 20\text{m}$, which cuts A's pre-tax profit by 20m.
2. The controlling shareholder bears $60\% \times 20\text{m} = 12\text{m}$ of that loss through A, but earns the full $20\text{m}$ extra profit at S: **net gain $= 20 - 12 = 8\text{m}$**.
3. Minority holders own $40\%$ of A and bear $40\% \times 20\text{m} = 8\text{m}$ of the loss, with no share of S's gain.

Value of 8m moves from minority shareholders to the controlling shareholder. Hence the standard safeguards: approval by independent directors or a minority vote, an independent fairness opinion, and disclosure of the terms.

## 5. Risks of Weak Governance and Benefits of Good Governance

### Risks of poor governance

- **Weak control systems:** poor internal controls and oversight allow errors, fraud and misreporting to continue undetected.
- **Ineffective decision-making:** a dominant CEO or inattentive board approves poor investments or acquisitions, wastes capital and misses risks.
- **Legal, regulatory and reputational risk:** fines, litigation, restatements, loss of customers and loss of investor trust.
- **Excess risk-taking and default risk:** weak risk oversight and short-term pay encourage risky positions and high leverage, increasing the chance of financial distress and raising the cost of debt.
- **Value transfer:** related-party dealings, excessive pay and entrenchment divert value from minority shareholders.

### Benefits of effective governance

- **Better control and reliable reporting:** investors can trust the numbers, lowering the information risk they must price.
- **Better decisions and operating efficiency:** independent challenge and aligned pay lead to better capital allocation and lower agency costs.
- **Lower cost of capital:** lower perceived risk reduces the required return on equity and the interest cost of debt, and the firm typically has a **higher valuation** and lower default risk.
- **Stronger stakeholder relations and resilience:** fewer scandals, better access to financing and an easier path through downturns.

### Governance in practice: an analyst's checklist

When evaluating a company's governance, an analyst reviews:

1. **Board:** independence, chair/CEO split, skills, tenure, committee composition and attendance.
2. **Ownership and voting:** dual-class or controlling holders, anti-takeover provisions, voting rights.
3. **Pay:** structure, link to long-term performance, clawbacks, ownership requirements.
4. **Audit and reporting:** auditor independence and tenure, restatements, quality of disclosure, qualified opinions.
5. **Related-party transactions:** size, terms and approval process.
6. **Track record:** past regulatory actions, litigation, and treatment of minority holders.

Governance is one pillar of **ESG** (environmental, social and governance) analysis, and ESG factors increasingly feed into valuation and risk assessment. Poor governance is a reason to demand a higher required return or to apply a valuation discount.

## Key takeaways

- Corporate governance is the system of controls and procedures that defines the rights, roles and responsibilities of the firm's stakeholders; its principles are fairness, transparency, accountability and responsibility.
- Boards can be **one-tier** or **two-tier**. Independent directors, a separate chair and CEO and independent audit, pay and nomination committees strengthen oversight. Judge independence by actual ties, not job titles.
- **Cumulative voting** helps minorities win seats; guaranteeing one seat takes more than shares $\div$ (seats $+ 1$). Statutory voting lets a majority elect every director.
- Governance manages conflicts through boards, pay design, covenants, audit and disclosure, shareholder voting and activism, and the market for corporate control.
- A related-party deal at an above-market price can transfer value to a controlling shareholder; in the example, 8m moved from minority holders.
- **Weak governance** raises control, decision-making, legal, reputational and default risk. **Good governance** brings better control, better decisions and operating results, lower cost of capital and lower default risk.
