Options pricing is not arbitrary. Two foundational principles govern modern options market making and pricing integrity: **Put-Call Parity** (which maintains static price equilibrium across options and spot markets) and **Delta Hedging** (which enables market makers to manage directional risk dynamically).

## 1. The Put-Call Parity Principle

**Put-Call Parity** defines a mandatory mathematical relationship linking European Call prices, European Put prices, the spot price of the underlying asset, and the present value of the strike price.

### The Put-Call Parity Formula

For European options with the same strike price ($K$) and expiration date ($T$):

$$
C + K \cdot e^{-rT} = P + S
$$

*Or in discrete compounding format:*

$$
C + \frac{K}{(1+r)^T} = P + S
$$

*Where:*
- $C$ = European Call Option Premium
- $P$ = European Put Option Premium
- $S$ = Current Spot Price of Underlying Asset
- $K$ = Strike Price (Exercise Price)
- $r$ = Annualized Risk-Free Interest Rate
- $T$ = Time to Expiration (in years)
- $K \cdot e^{-rT}$ = Present Value of Strike Price ($PV(K)$)

*(Note: This basic formula assumes zero cash dividends paid during the contract period).*

---

## 2. Conceptual Intuition: Portfolio Equivalence

To understand why Put-Call Parity must hold, consider two separate investment portfolios created today:

1. **Portfolio 1 (Fiduciary Call)**:
   - Long 1 European Call Option (Strike $K$)
   - Long 1 Zero-Coupon Risk-Free Bond with face value $K$ maturing at expiry ($PV = K \cdot e^{-rT}$)
2. **Portfolio 2 (Protective Put)**:
   - Long 1 European Put Option (Strike $K$)
   - Long 1 Share of Underlying Stock (Current Spot $S$)

### Payoff Verification at Expiration ($T$)

| Expiration Outcome | Portfolio 1 Payoff (Call + Bond) | Portfolio 2 Payoff (Put + Stock) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stock Rises ($S_T > K$)** | Call pays $(S_T - K)$ + Bond pays $K$ = **$S_T$** | Put pays $\$0$ + Stock is worth $S_T$ = **$S_T$** |
| **Stock Falls ($S_T \le K$)** | Call pays $\$0$ + Bond pays $K$ = **$K$** | Put pays $(K - S_T)$ + Stock is worth $S_T$ = **$K$** |

### The No-Arbitrage Conclusion
Because Portfolio 1 and Portfolio 2 generate **identical economic payoffs under every possible market outcome**, they **must cost the exact same amount to construct today**. If their costs differed, an immediate risk-free arbitrage opportunity would exist.

---

## 3. No-Arbitrage Enforcement & Worked Example

If market prices dislocate such that $C + K \cdot e^{-rT} \neq P + S$, arbitrageurs execute simultaneous trades (buying the cheaper portfolio and selling the overpriced portfolio) until supply-demand pressure restores exact parity.

### Worked Numerical Example
Suppose an asset has the following parameters:
- **Current Spot Price ($S$)**: \$100
- **Strike Price ($K$)**: \$100
- **Risk-Free Rate ($r$)**: $5\%$ ($0.05$)
- **Time to Expiry ($T$)**: $1.0$ year ($e^{-0.05} \approx 0.9512$)
- **European Call Premium ($C$)**: \$8.00

#### Step 1: Calculate Present Value of Strike Price
$$
PV(K) = \$100 \times e^{-0.05} = \$100 \times 0.9512 = \$95.12
$$

#### Step 2: Solve for Theoretical Put Price ($P$)
Using Put-Call Parity ($C + PV(K) = P + S$):

$$
\$8.00 + \$95.12 = P + \$100
$$

$$
\$103.12 = P + \$100 \implies P = \$103.12 - \$100 = \mathbf{\$3.12}
$$

The theoretical fair value of the Put option is **\$3.12**.

## 4. Delta Hedging & Delta-Neutrality

While Put-Call Parity ensures static market pricing alignment, **Delta Hedging** allows option sellers and market makers to manage ongoing directional price risk.

### What is Delta-Neutrality?
Recall that **Delta ($\Delta$)** measures an option's price change for a \$1 move in the spot price. 

A **Delta-Neutral Position** is a combined portfolio of options and underlying assets structured so that the net sum of all Deltas equals **zero** ($\sum \Delta = 0$). Small fluctuations in the underlying stock price produce zero net change in portfolio value.

### Worked Sizing Example: Hedging Short Calls
A market maker sells (shorts) **10 Call Option contracts** ($L = 100$ shares per contract, total 1,000 option shares). The call option currently has a **Delta of $+0.60$**.

#### Step 1: Calculate Short Option Delta
$$
\text{Short Option Delta} = -10 \text{ contracts} \times 100 \text{ shares} \times (+0.60) = \mathbf{-600 \Delta}
$$

#### Step 2: Determine Offsetting Stock Position
To offset $-600 \Delta$ to zero, the market maker must acquire positive Delta by buying underlying stock (each share has a Delta of $+1.0$):

$$
\text{Required Shares} = \frac{+600 \Delta}{+1.0} = \mathbf{600 \text{ shares}}
$$

By purchasing **600 shares of underlying stock**, the market maker achieves a net Delta of zero ($-600 + 600 = 0$).

---

## 5. Dynamic Hedging & The Role of Gamma

Delta hedging is not a static "one-and-done" transaction. A Delta hedge is only effective for small, immediate spot price movements because **Delta changes continuously**.

### The Role of Gamma ($\Gamma$)
Recall that **Gamma** measures the rate of change of Delta per \$1 move in spot price ($\Gamma = \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial S}$).

- If the stock price rises, the call option's Delta increases from $+0.60$ to $+0.65$. The short option position becomes $-650 \Delta$.
- To maintain Delta-neutrality, the market maker must buy **50 additional shares** of stock.
- If the stock price falls, the call option's Delta decreases to $+0.55$. The short option position becomes $-550 \Delta$, requiring the market maker to sell **50 shares**.

### Dynamic Rebalancing
This process of continuously adjusting the underlying stock position as spot prices move is called **Dynamic Delta Hedging**. It is how institutional options desks manage multi-million dollar derivatives portfolios while remaining insulated from directional market swings.
