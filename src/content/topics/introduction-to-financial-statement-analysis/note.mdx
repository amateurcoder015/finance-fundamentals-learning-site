# Introduction to Financial Statement Analysis

**Financial statement analysis** is the use of a company's reported financial information to evaluate its past performance and current condition, and to form a view about its future. Investors use it to value securities, creditors use it to judge repayment ability, and managers use it to guide decisions. This chapter introduces the statements, where else to find information, and a disciplined process for turning raw reports into a conclusion.

---

## 1. The Roles of Financial Reporting and Analysis

- **Financial reporting** is the preparation and presentation of a company's financial position, performance and cash flows. Management is responsible for it, following a body of accounting standards.
- **Financial statement analysis** is the job of the outside (or inside) analyst: to evaluate that information and turn it into decisions, such as whether to buy a stock, extend credit or approve an acquisition.

Standards come mainly from two sets:

- **IFRS** (International Financial Reporting Standards), issued by the IASB and used in more than 140 jurisdictions.
- **US GAAP**, issued by the FASB and used by US-listed domestic companies.

The two sets share the same objective of providing useful information, but they differ in many specific recognition and measurement rules, so analysts must know which one a company uses before comparing it with peers.

<Note>Reported numbers reflect management's accounting choices and estimates (useful lives, provisions, revenue timing). A good analyst reads the statements critically rather than treating every line as a plain fact.</Note>

## 2. The Four Primary Financial Statements

### The balance sheet (statement of financial position)

A snapshot **at a point in time** of what the company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities) and the residual claim of its owners (equity). It follows the **accounting equation**:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owners' equity}$$

### The income statement (statement of profit or loss)

A report **over a period** of revenues earned, expenses incurred and the resulting profit:

$$\text{Net income} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses}$$

Expenses here include cost of goods sold, operating expenses, interest and taxes. Profit is measured on an **accrual basis**: revenue is recognized when earned and expenses when incurred, not when cash moves.

### The statement of cash flows

Reports the cash received and paid **during a period**, classified into three activities:

| Activity | Typical items |
| --- | --- |
| **Operating** | Cash received from customers, cash paid to suppliers and employees, taxes |
| **Investing** | Purchase and sale of long-term assets and investments |
| **Financing** | Issuing or repaying debt, issuing or buying back shares, dividends |

Cash flow statements reveal whether profit is turning into cash, which accrual earnings alone cannot show.

### The statement of changes in equity

Reconciles the opening and closing balance of owners' equity. Changes come from net income, dividends, share issuance or repurchases and other comprehensive income.

### How the statements link

The statements articulate with each other:

$$\text{Ending retained earnings} = \text{Beginning retained earnings} + \text{Net income} - \text{Dividends}$$

Net income flows from the income statement into equity on the balance sheet; the ending cash balance on the cash flow statement equals cash on the balance sheet.

## 3. Other Sources of Information

The statements do not stand alone. Analysts also rely on:

- **Notes (footnotes) to the financial statements**: accounting policies, estimates, segment data, debt terms, commitments, contingencies and related-party transactions. They are an integral part of the statements.
- **Management's discussion and analysis (MD&A)** (a management commentary under IFRS): management's explanation of results, liquidity, capital resources, trends and risks. It is not audited in the same way as the statements, and it reflects management's own perspective.
- **The auditor's report**: an independent opinion on whether the statements fairly present the company's position.
- **Proxy statements**: information on executive compensation, board composition and shareholder voting items.
- **Other sources**: press releases, earnings calls, regulatory filings, industry data, and competitor and economic information.

### Types of audit opinion

| Opinion | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Unqualified (clean)** | The statements are fairly presented in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable standards |
| **Qualified** | Fairly presented except for a specific matter |
| **Adverse** | The statements are materially misstated and do not present fairly |
| **Disclaimer of opinion** | The auditor could not obtain enough evidence to form an opinion |

An audit gives **reasonable assurance**, not a guarantee, that the statements are free of material misstatement. Auditors also report **key audit matters** (areas of greatest significance in the audit). A company's management and, ultimately, its board and audit committee remain responsible for the statements.

## 4. The Financial Statement Analysis Framework

The framework breaks an analysis into six phases:

1. **Articulate the purpose and context**: What question is being asked, who is the audience, what is the time frame and budget?
2. **Collect input data**: Obtain the statements, notes, industry and economic data, and speak to management where possible.
3. **Process the data**: Adjust the statements for comparability, compute ratios and growth rates, and prepare common-size statements and charts.
4. **Analyze and interpret the processed data**: Compare against history, peers and expectations, and understand the reasons behind the changes.
5. **Develop and communicate conclusions**: Present findings and recommendations following the required standards, noting the limits of the analysis.
6. **Follow up**: Revisit the conclusions as new information arrives and update the recommendation.

## 5. Worked Example

**Setting.** A company has the following figures (in millions):

| Item | Beginning of year | End of year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total assets | 600 | 700 |
| Total liabilities | 400 | 455 |
| Owners' equity | 200 | ? |

For the year: revenue 800, net income 60, dividends declared and paid 15, no share issuance or repurchase.

**Step 1: Ending equity.** Equity rises by retained profit:

$$200 + 60 - 15 = 245$$

**Step 2: Check the accounting equation.** Assets = Liabilities + Equity: $455 + 245 = 700$. This matches total assets of 700.

**Step 3: Key ratios.**

- Net profit margin $= \dfrac{60}{800} = 7.5\%$
- Average total assets $= \dfrac{600 + 700}{2} = 650$, so asset turnover $= \dfrac{800}{650} = 1.23$
- Average equity $= \dfrac{200 + 245}{2} = 222.5$
- Return on equity $= \dfrac{60}{222.5} = 26.97\% \approx 27.0\%$
- Debt-to-equity (end of year) $= \dfrac{455}{245} = 1.86$

**Step 4: Cross-check with the DuPont identity.**

$$\text{ROE} = \text{Net margin} \times \text{Asset turnover} \times \text{Equity multiplier}$$

The equity multiplier is average assets over average equity: $650 / 222.5 = 2.92$. So

$$7.5\% \times 1.2308 \times 2.9213 = 26.97\%$$

which equals the direct calculation. The return on equity of about 27% is driven by moderate margins, decent asset use and fairly heavy leverage, an observation that leads naturally to the follow-up question of how sustainable that leverage is.

## Key takeaways

- Financial reporting is **management's** presentation of results; financial statement analysis is the **user's** evaluation of them.
- The four primary statements are the **balance sheet, income statement, statement of cash flows** and **statement of changes in equity**.
- The accounting equation is **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**; ending retained earnings = beginning + net income - dividends.
- Notes, **MD&A** and the **auditor's report** are essential complements to the statements. A clean (unqualified) opinion gives reasonable, not absolute, assurance.
- The analysis framework has six phases, from stating the purpose to following up.
- Always check which accounting standards (IFRS or US GAAP) a company uses before comparing it with peers.
