# Overview of Equity Securities

An **equity security** represents an ownership claim on a company. Unlike a bond, it has no maturity and no promised payments: shareholders are the **residual claimants**, paid only after creditors and preferred holders. This chapter surveys the types of equity securities, their features and risks, and how equity connects to a company's financial statements and cost of capital.

---

## 1. Types of Equity Securities

### Common shares

**Common shares** are the basic form of ownership. They give holders:

- a residual claim on assets and earnings (after debt and preferred shares are satisfied);
- **dividends**, if declared by the board (dividends are discretionary, not contractual);
- **voting rights** on matters such as electing directors, approving mergers and auditor appointments;
- limited liability: losses are limited to the amount invested.

**Voting systems.** Under **statutory voting** each share gets one vote per director seat. Under **cumulative voting**, a shareholder may pool all votes on a single candidate, which helps minority holders win board representation. For example, a holder with 100 shares voting for 3 open board seats has $100 \times 3 = 300$ votes under cumulative voting (all can go to one candidate) but only 100 votes per seat under statutory voting.

**Other features and share classes**

- **Callable common shares**: the issuer may repurchase shares at a set price. The call price caps the upside, so these shares are usually priced lower than otherwise identical non-callable shares.
- **Putable common shares**: the holder may sell shares back to the issuer at a set price, which provides downside protection. They are worth more than otherwise identical non-putable shares.
- **Dual-class (multiple-class) structures**: classes carry different voting rights or dividend claims, so founders can keep control with a minority of the economic ownership.
- **Preemptive rights**: some shareholders have the right to buy new shares first, which protects them from dilution.

### Preferred shares

**Preferred shares** rank ahead of common shares for dividends and in liquidation, but behind debt. They typically pay a **fixed dividend** (a percentage of par value) and usually carry **no voting rights**. Because the fixed payment resembles interest, preferred shares behave partly like a bond and partly like equity.

| Type | Feature |
| --- | --- |
| **Cumulative** | Unpaid dividends accumulate as **arrears** and must be paid before any common dividend |
| **Non-cumulative** | Missed dividends are lost; no arrears build up |
| **Participating** | Holders share in extra earnings above the stated dividend (and sometimes liquidation proceeds) |
| **Non-participating** | Holders receive only the stated dividend and liquidation preference |
| **Convertible** | Holder may convert into a stated number of common shares (conversion ratio) |
| **Callable / Putable** | Issuer may call, or holder may put, at a set price |
| **Adjustable-rate (floating)** | Dividend resets with a reference rate |

**Convertible preferred** details:

$$\text{Conversion price} = \frac{\text{Par (issue) value}}{\text{Conversion ratio}}, \qquad \text{Conversion value} = \text{Conversion ratio} \times \text{Share price}$$

### Worked example 1: cumulative preferred and convertible preferred

A company has 100,000 preferred shares with a \$100 par value and a 5% cumulative dividend. It paid nothing on them for the past two years and now wants to resume dividends to common shareholders.

1. Annual preferred dividend = 5% × \$100 = \$5 per share.
2. Arrears = 2 × \$5 = \$10 per share.
3. Amount owed to preferred before any common dividend = (\$10 + \$5) × 100,000 = \$1,500,000.

If the shares were **non-cumulative**, the two missed years would be lost and only the current \$5 per share (\$500,000) would be owed.

Now suppose each preferred share is convertible into 2.5 common shares:

1. Conversion price $= \$100 / 2.5 = \$40$ per common share.
2. If common trades at \$48, conversion value $= 2.5 \times \$48 = \$120$ per preferred share, above par, so conversion is attractive (the preferred will trade at no less than about \$120).
3. If common trades at \$36, conversion value $= 2.5 \times \$36 = \$90$, below par, so holders would keep the preferred.

## 2. Public and Private Equity

| Feature | **Public equity** | **Private equity** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trading** | Listed on exchanges or traded over the counter | Not publicly traded; negotiated, infrequent transfers |
| **Liquidity** | High; transparent prices | Low; valuation is by appraisal |
| **Disclosure** | Extensive regulatory reporting | Limited, mostly to investors |
| **Regulation** | Heavily regulated | Lighter regulation |
| **Investors** | Anyone | Typically institutions, funds and wealthy investors |

**Private equity** includes:

- **Venture capital**: funding for young, high-growth companies, with high risk and potential return.
- **Leveraged buyouts (LBOs)**: acquisition of a mature company using a large amount of debt; the buyer later exits via sale or IPO.
- **Private investment in public equity (PIPE)**: a private placement of shares in an already-listed company, often at a discount.

Private equity is usually illiquid and valued infrequently, so investors typically require a **liquidity premium** and rely on the manager's appraisals.

### Listing and the market for shares

A company becomes public through an **initial public offering (IPO)**. Public shares provide liquidity and access to capital markets, but also disclosure obligations, regulatory costs and short-term market pressure.

## 3. Depository Receipts

A **depository receipt (DR)** is a security issued by a depository bank that represents shares of a foreign company, so investors can hold foreign equity in their home market and currency.

| Type | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **American depository receipt (ADR)** | Trades in the United States, priced in US dollars |
| **Global depository receipt (GDR)** | Issued outside the issuer's home country and outside the US (and often in more than one market) |
| **Sponsored DR** | The issuer participates: it has a deposit agreement, provides information and the DR carries shareholder rights, including voting |
| **Unsponsored DR** | Issued by a bank without the company's involvement; fewer disclosure requirements and often no voting rights |

Benefits: trading in local currency and time zones, local settlement and custody, avoiding foreign-market access barriers. Risks that remain: **currency risk** (the DR's price still reflects the underlying share price in the foreign currency) and country or political risk.

### Worked example 2: pricing an ADR

An ADR represents 4 ordinary shares of a European company. The ordinary shares trade at €30, and the exchange rate is \$1.10 per euro.

1. Value of 4 shares in euro $= 4 \times €30 = €120$.
2. Value in dollars $= €120 \times 1.10 = \$132$.

The ADR should trade at about \$132. If it traded at \$135, an arbitrageur could buy the ordinary shares, convert them into ADRs and sell the ADRs, until the price gap closes (ignoring costs).

## 4. Risk and Return of Equity

### Return components

The **total return** on an equity investment over a period has two parts:

$$R = \frac{P_1 - P_0 + D_1}{P_0} = \underbrace{\frac{P_1 - P_0}{P_0}}_{\text{price return}} + \underbrace{\frac{D_1}{P_0}}_{\text{dividend yield}}$$

Other sources of return include dividends reinvested and, for foreign equity, **currency gains or losses**. Over a multi-period horizon, returns compound, and the **holding period return** is the product of $(1 + R_t)$ minus one.

### Worked example 3: total return

An investor buys a share at \$50, receives dividends of \$2 over the year and sells at \$54.

1. Price return $= (54 - 50) / 50 = 8.0\%$.
2. Dividend yield $= 2 / 50 = 4.0\%$.
3. Total return $= 8.0\% + 4.0\% = 12.0\%$, or $(54 - 50 + 2)/50 = 6/50 = 12.0\%$.

### Relative risk

| Security | Risk and return (from lowest to highest) |
| --- | --- |
| **Debt** | Contractual claims; lowest risk and expected return |
| **Preferred shares** | Fixed dividends but still subordinated to debt |
| **Common shares** | Residual claim; highest risk and highest expected return |

- **Equity risk** is commonly measured by the **standard deviation** of returns. **Putable** shares carry less risk than ordinary shares, **callable** shares more.
- Common shares carry **business, financial and market risk**; their value can fall to zero, but shareholders cannot lose more than they invested.
- Preferred shares are less volatile than common shares, but their value is sensitive to interest rates, like bonds, and to the issuer's credit quality.

## 5. Equity and the Company: Book Value, Market Value and ROE

### Equity in financing

Companies raise equity from the **primary market** (new share issues, IPOs and follow-on offerings) and trade it in the **secondary market**. Equity provides funds without mandatory repayment and acts as a **buffer** for creditors. It is also costly, because shareholders require a return greater than lenders.

### Book value versus market value

- **Book value of equity** $=$ total assets $-$ total liabilities (an accounting figure, reflecting historical costs and retained earnings). **Common equity** excludes the preferred shares' book value.
- **Market value of equity** $=$ share price $\times$ shares outstanding, which reflects investors' expectations of future cash flows and risk.

Market value usually exceeds book value for profitable, growing firms (a **price-to-book** ratio above 1), because balance-sheet accounting omits internally generated intangibles such as brands and know-how.

$$\text{BVPS} = \frac{\text{Common equity}}{\text{Shares outstanding}}$$

### Return on equity

Accounting **return on equity (ROE)** measures profitability relative to shareholders' book investment:

$$\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net income} - \text{Preferred dividends}}{\text{Average book value of common equity}}$$

### Worked example 4: BVPS and ROE

A company reports total assets of \$800 million, total liabilities of \$500 million and preferred equity of \$50 million at year end. Common equity at the start of the year was \$230 million. Net income was \$45 million, preferred dividends \$5 million, and 20 million common shares are outstanding.

1. Year-end common equity $= 800 - 500 - 50 = \$250$ million.
2. $\text{BVPS} = 250 / 20 = \$12.50$.
3. Average common equity $= (230 + 250)/2 = \$240$ million.
4. $\text{ROE} = (45 - 5) / 240 = 40/240 = 16.67\%$.

If the market price is \$30, the market value of equity is $30 \times 20 = \$600$ million and price-to-book is $30 / 12.50 = 2.4$.

### Cost of equity

The **cost of equity** is the minimum return that shareholders require for bearing the firm's equity risk. It is a required return, not an accounting figure. It is higher than the cost of debt because shareholders have a residual claim, and it can be estimated with models such as CAPM:

$$r_e = R_f + \beta\left(E(R_m) - R_f\right)$$

Comparing ROE with the cost of equity helps judge value creation: a company earning ROE **above** its cost of equity (over time) creates value for shareholders, while one earning **below** it destroys value. In the example above, if the cost of equity were 10%, the 16.67% ROE would exceed it.

## Key takeaways

- Common shares are residual claims with voting rights and discretionary dividends. Preferred shares have fixed, senior dividends and usually no votes.
- Preferred features: **cumulative** (arrears must be paid first), **participating**, **convertible** (conversion price $=$ par ÷ conversion ratio), callable and putable. Callable features lower value for holders, putable features raise it.
- Public equity is liquid and regulated; private equity (venture capital, LBOs, PIPEs) is illiquid with limited disclosure.
- Depository receipts (ADRs, GDRs) let investors hold foreign shares locally. Sponsored DRs carry shareholder rights and disclosure; currency risk remains.
- Total return $=$ price return $+$ dividend yield. Equity has the highest risk and expected return among the main security types.
- Book value of equity is an accounting figure; market value is price $\times$ shares. $\text{ROE} = (\text{NI} - \text{preferred dividends}) / \text{average common equity}$.
- The cost of equity is shareholders' required return; ROE above the cost of equity signals value creation.
