# Fixed-Income Markets for Government Issuers

Governments are the largest borrowers in the fixed-income market. Their bonds set the **benchmark** against which almost every other bond is priced. This chapter covers why and how governments borrow, the instruments they use, how new debt is sold and traded, and the other public-sector issuers: quasi-government agencies, supranational institutions, and local or regional governments.

---

## 1. Government Issuers and Why They Borrow

| Issuer type | Examples | Key feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sovereign (national) government** | Central government of a country | Issues in its own currency (domestic) or in a foreign currency; largest and most liquid issuer |
| **Quasi-government (agency)** | Government-sponsored or government-owned agencies and enterprises | Created for a public purpose; support from the government may be explicit (guarantee) or implicit |
| **Supranational** | World Bank, European Investment Bank, Asian Development Bank | Owned by several member countries; lends for development; usually top-rated |
| **Non-sovereign (sub-national)** | States, provinces, cities, municipalities | Borrow to build infrastructure; repayment from taxes or project revenue |

### Funding a government budget

A government finances spending from three sources: **taxes**, **borrowing** and, in some cases, **monetary financing** (the central bank creating money). When spending exceeds tax revenue the result is a **budget deficit**; a **surplus** is the reverse.

$$\text{Deficit} = \text{Government spending} - \text{Tax revenue}$$

Annual deficits are financed by new borrowing and add to the stock of **public debt**. The usual measure of debt burden is

$$\text{Debt-to-GDP} = \frac{\text{Total government debt}}{\text{Nominal GDP}}$$

Heavy reliance on monetary financing risks inflation, so most governments cover deficits by issuing bonds. Governments also borrow to manage the timing gap between receipts and payments, which is why **short-term bills** are issued frequently.

### Domestic versus foreign currency

- **Local (domestic) currency debt**: the government can in principle always raise taxes or create money to repay, so default risk is lower.
- **Foreign currency debt**: the government must acquire foreign currency to repay, and depreciation of its own currency raises the real burden. Emerging market sovereigns often borrow in USD or EUR to attract foreign investors, which creates **currency mismatch risk**.

Rating agencies therefore often assign **separate ratings for local-currency and foreign-currency debt**, and sovereign defaults have historically been more frequent on foreign-currency debt.

## 2. Sovereign Debt Instruments

| Instrument | Maturity | Coupon | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Treasury bill (T-bill)** | One year or less (money market) | None: issued at a **discount** to par | Return is the difference between purchase price and par |
| **Note** | Intermediate (about 2 to 10 years in the U.S.) | Fixed, usually semiannual | Capital-market instrument |
| **Bond** | Long (over 10 years in the U.S.) | Fixed, usually semiannual | Often the longest-dated sovereign security |
| **Floating-rate note (FRN)** | Varies | Reference rate $+$ spread, resetting periodically | Protects investors against rising rates; price stays near par at reset dates |
| **Inflation-linked bond** | Varies | Principal and/or coupon adjusted for inflation | Gives a **real** return; U.S. example is TIPS |
| **Zero-coupon / strips** | Varies | None; sold at a discount | Created by separating coupons and principal |

Terminology (note versus bond) varies by country; the important distinction is bills (money market, discount) versus coupon-bearing securities (capital market).

### Inflation-linked bonds

The principal is multiplied by an **inflation index factor**. Coupons are paid on the **adjusted principal**:

$$\text{Adjusted principal}_t = \text{Par} \times \text{Index ratio}_t, \qquad \text{Coupon}_t = \text{Adjusted principal}_t \times \frac{\text{Real coupon rate}}{\text{Periods per year}}$$

In the United States, TIPS pay at maturity the **greater of** the inflation-adjusted principal or original par, so deflation cannot take the redemption below par. Some other countries do not have this floor. The yield on an inflation-linked bond is a **real yield**; the gap between a nominal bond's yield and an inflation-linked bond's real yield of similar maturity is the **breakeven inflation rate**, the inflation level at which the two investments give the same return.

### Example: coupon on a TIPS-style bond

A bond has par 1,000 and a real coupon rate of 2.00% paid semiannually. Inflation over the first six months raises the index ratio to 1.015.

1. Adjusted principal $= 1{,}000 \times 1.015 = 1{,}015$
2. Coupon $= 1{,}015 \times 0.02 / 2 = 10.15$

A conventional 1,000 par bond with a 2% coupon would pay only 10.00. The extra 0.15 in the first coupon reflects the inflation compensation, and later coupons are also paid on the larger adjusted principal.

## 3. Primary Market: Auctions and Dealers

Most developed-market sovereigns sell debt through a regular, announced calendar of **auctions**. Smaller or less regular deals may use **syndication** (a group of banks places the bond), and a government may **tap** (add to) or **reopen** an existing issue so that it grows to a large, liquid size.

### Primary dealers

Many governments work with a group of **primary dealers**: banks and securities firms that must bid at each auction, make markets in the secondary market and provide information to the debt office. In return they get privileged access to the issuer. Their presence supports demand at each sale and improves liquidity.

### Bidding

- **Competitive bids** state the quantity and the price or yield the bidder will accept. Bids at the best (lowest-yield, highest-price) levels are filled first until the offering is sold.
- **Non-competitive bids** (typically small investors) state only quantity and receive the average or stop-out price.
- The **stop-out yield** (or price) is the least attractive level at which bids are still accepted.

Two main pricing formats:

| Format | What successful bidders pay | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Single-price (Dutch) auction** | All winners get the **stop-out price** (same yield) | Encourages aggressive bidding: a bidder cannot lose by bidding a better price than the stop-out level, because they pay only the stop-out price. U.S. Treasury auctions use this format |
| **Multiple-price (discriminatory/English) auction** | Each winner pays **their own bid** | Revenue for the issuer is higher for a given set of bids, but bidders may shade bids to avoid the "winner's curse" |

The market also trades the security **when-issued**, before the auction settles, which gives an early price signal and helps dealers distribute the issue.

### Example: Comparing auction formats

A government offers 100 (millions) of 10-year bonds in a yield-based auction. The bids are:

| Bidder | Quantity | Yield bid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 40 | 4.10% |
| B | 30 | 4.12% |
| C | 40 | 4.15% |
| D | 30 | 4.20% |

1. Lower yield means higher price, so the best bids are filled first: A (40), then B (30), leaving 30 of the offering.
2. C bids 40 at 4.15%, but only 30 remains, so C is **partially filled** with 30. The **stop-out yield is 4.15%**. D is rejected.
3. **Single-price:** A, B and C all receive their allocation at 4.15%.
4. **Multiple-price:** A gets 40 at 4.10%, B gets 30 at 4.12% and C gets 30 at 4.15%.

In the multiple-price format A and B pay a higher price (lower yield) than in the single-price format, so the issuer raises more cash. In practice bidders adjust their bids to the format, so the real difference can be smaller.

### Pricing a T-bill

T-bills are quoted on a **discount rate** basis, with a 360-day year in the United States:

$$P = F\left(1 - d \times \frac{t}{360}\right)$$

Example: a 91-day bill with face value 100 and a discount rate of 4.80%.

1. $d \times t/360 = 0.048 \times 91/360 = 0.012133$
2. $P = 100 \times (1 - 0.012133) = 98.7867$
3. Money market yield $= \dfrac{100 - 98.7867}{98.7867} \times \dfrac{360}{91} = 0.012282 \times 3.9560 = 4.86\%$

The money market yield (4.86%) exceeds the discount rate (4.80%) because the discount rate is measured against face value, whereas the investor's true return is measured on the lower amount paid.

## 4. Secondary Market and the Benchmark Curve

Sovereign bonds trade mainly **over the counter** among dealers and investors, with high volume and **narrow bid-ask spreads**. Features:

- **On-the-run** securities are the most recently auctioned issues of a given maturity; they are the most liquid and trade at a slight price premium. **Off-the-run** securities are older and trade less.
- Government bonds are used as **collateral** in repos, in derivatives margining and in bank liquidity requirements, which sustains demand.
- The yields of on-the-run securities form the **sovereign (benchmark) yield curve**. Other issuers' bonds are priced as a **spread over the benchmark**:

$$\text{Yield on a non-sovereign bond} = \text{Benchmark yield for same maturity} + \text{Spread}$$

- Central banks buy and sell government securities in **open market operations**, so sovereign bond yields transmit monetary policy to the broader economy.

For emerging markets, the benchmark may be a hard-currency (USD) sovereign curve, and the local government curve may be less liquid or incomplete.

*Local note:* in India, the central government issues dated securities and T-bills (91-, 182- and 364-day) through auctions held by the Reserve Bank of India, and **state governments** raise money through state development loans (SDLs), whose yields are quoted as a spread over central government securities.

## 5. Non-Sovereign Issuers

### Quasi-government agencies

Agencies are set up to carry out a public function such as housing finance, export credit or infrastructure. Their bonds may be:

- **Explicitly guaranteed** by the government: credit risk close to the sovereign.
- **Implicitly supported** but not guaranteed: priced at a modest spread to the sovereign, with more credit risk if support is uncertain.

Agencies issue to fund their own lending, and often securitize pools of loans (see the separate chapters on securitization).

### Supranational issuers

Supranationals are owned by several sovereign member states and lend to developing or member countries (the World Bank and regional development banks, for example). Their strengths:

- **Paid-in capital** from members plus **callable capital** (a commitment from members to supply more capital if needed).
- **Preferred creditor status**: borrowers generally repay them before other creditors.
- Usually **AAA-rated**, and they borrow in many currencies, often swapping the proceeds into the currency they need.

Supranational bonds typically trade at a small spread to the sovereign benchmark, reflecting strong credit quality but lower liquidity than sovereign bonds.

### Local and regional governments: municipal bonds

States, provinces and cities fund roads, schools, utilities and hospitals by selling **municipal bonds**. Two main types:

| Type | Repaid from | Credit analysis focuses on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **General obligation (GO) bond** | The issuer's general taxing power; "full faith and credit" | Tax base, debt burden, budget health, economy |
| **Revenue bond** | Revenue of a specific project (toll road, airport, water system, hospital) | Project cash flows, debt service coverage, legal protections |

Sub-national governments usually cannot print money, so their credit risk can exceed the sovereign's. In some countries (notably the United States) municipal interest is exempt from national income tax, so investors compare yields on a **taxable-equivalent** basis:

$$\text{Taxable-equivalent yield} = \frac{\text{Tax-exempt yield}}{1 - \text{Marginal tax rate}}$$

### Example: Municipal versus taxable bond

A tax-exempt municipal bond yields 3.60%. An investor in the 32% marginal bracket is comparing it with a taxable 4.80% bond of similar risk.

1. Taxable-equivalent yield $= 3.60\% / (1 - 0.32) = 3.60\% / 0.68 = 5.29\%$
2. After-tax yield on the taxable bond $= 4.80\% \times (1 - 0.32) = 3.26\%$

The municipal bond is better after tax (3.60% versus 3.26%), equivalently 5.29% versus 4.80% pre-tax.

## 6. Debt Burden: A Worked Example

A government has debt of 800 and nominal GDP of 1,000, so debt-to-GDP is 80%. This year the deficit will be 50 (5% of GDP), and nominal GDP is expected to grow by 4%.

1. New debt $= 800 + 50 = 850$ (assuming the deficit is fully financed by borrowing)
2. New GDP $= 1{,}000 \times 1.04 = 1{,}040$
3. New debt-to-GDP $= 850 / 1{,}040 = 81.7\%$

The ratio rose by 1.7 percentage points. The ratio stabilises only if the deficit shrinks or nominal GDP grows fast enough. A higher interest rate on the debt raises future deficits, which is why markets watch both the debt level and the **interest burden** (interest payments relative to revenue).

## Key takeaways

- Governments finance deficits through **taxes, borrowing and monetary financing**; most deficits are covered by bond issuance.
- **T-bills** are discount instruments of one year or less; **notes and bonds** pay fixed coupons; **FRNs** reset to a reference rate; **inflation-linked bonds** adjust principal and pay a real yield.
- Local-currency sovereign debt carries lower default risk than foreign-currency debt; ratings are often assigned separately.
- Sovereign bonds are sold by **auction** to **primary dealers** and investors. **Single-price** auctions pay all winners the stop-out price; **multiple-price** auctions pay each bidder their own bid.
- On-the-run sovereign yields form the **benchmark curve**; other bonds are priced as benchmark $+$ spread.
- **Quasi-government** bonds rely on explicit or implicit support; **supranationals** are backed by member capital and preferred creditor status; **municipal** GO bonds rely on taxing power, revenue bonds on project cash flows.
- Compare tax-exempt and taxable bonds with the taxable-equivalent yield $= y / (1 - t)$.
