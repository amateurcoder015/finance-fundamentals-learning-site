# Industry and Competitive Analysis

A company's prospects depend heavily on the industry it competes in. A well-run business in a shrinking, price-competitive industry may earn poor returns, while an average one in a growing, protected industry may earn good ones. **Industry analysis** studies the structure, growth and profitability of the industry; **competitive analysis** studies how a company is positioned within it. Together they form the top-down context for forecasting revenue, margins and returns on capital. This chapter covers industry classification and peer groups, cyclical and defensive industries, the industry life cycle, Porter's five forces, external influences, competitive strategy and industry concentration.

---

## 1. Classifying Companies

### Why classify?

Grouping companies lets analysts compare like with like: build **peer groups** for valuation multiples, benchmark performance, structure portfolios by sector, and judge diversification.

### Classification systems

| Type | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Commercial (data provider)** | GICS (MSCI/S&P), ICB (FTSE Russell), TRBC (Refinitiv/LSEG) | Based mostly on the company's **principal business activity**, usually its largest source of revenue (and earnings). Updated regularly; used for indices |
| **Government** | ISIC (UN), NAICS (North America), NIC (India), NACE (EU) | Classify business *establishments* for economic statistics; used widely but often with less detail on listed companies |

Commercial systems are hierarchical. GICS, for example, has four levels, from broad to narrow: **sector**, **industry group**, **industry**, **sub-industry**. A company is assigned to one sub-industry according to its principal business activity.

Classification has limits: diversified conglomerates fit poorly into one category, different systems place companies differently, and a company's business mix changes over time.

### Peer groups

A **peer group** is a set of companies with similar business activities, demand drivers, cost structure and exposure to economic factors. To build one:

1. Start from a commercial classification (the company's sub-industry or industry).
2. Review the company's filings, competitors named in its annual report, and analysts' coverage lists.
3. Check that revenue sources, geography, size and growth profile are similar.
4. Test statistically: peers' returns should be highly **correlated**, and cluster analysis can group companies by return behaviour.

A good peer group is essential for relative valuation: a multiple applied to the wrong peers misleads.

## 2. Cyclical and Defensive Industries

Companies are often described by how their earnings respond to the business cycle.

| Feature | **Cyclical** | **Defensive (non-cyclical)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand sensitivity | High; swings with GDP and income | Low; demand stays steady |
| Typical goods | Durables, autos, airlines, luxury goods, capital goods, homebuilding | Food, household staples, utilities, healthcare, telecom services |
| Earnings | Volatile; large rise in expansion, sharp fall in recession | Stable and predictable |
| Operating leverage | Often high (large fixed costs magnify profit swings) | Often lower |
| Valuation | Often cheapest on peak earnings (low P/E), dearest on trough earnings | Steady, premium multiples |

Related terms: **growth industries** expand faster than the economy because of structural change (technology, demographics) rather than the cycle, and **sector rotation** is the practice of shifting between cyclical and defensive sectors as the economy moves through the cycle. Cyclical sectors tend to lead in early recovery; defensive sectors hold up in contraction.

<Note>
"Cyclical vs defensive" is about **earnings sensitivity to the cycle**, not about whether the industry is growing. A fast-growing technology industry can still be cyclical if customers cut spending in downturns.
</Note>

## 3. The Industry Life Cycle

Industries tend to pass through five stages. The stage helps predict growth, margins, competition and capital needs.

| Stage | Growth | Competition and pricing | Profits and investment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Embryonic** | Slow; the product is new | Few firms, high prices, unproven demand | Losses, heavy investment; high risk of failure |
| **Growth** | Rapid | New entrants but demand outpaces supply, prices fall as scale grows; little price competition | Profits rise, heavy capacity investment; low risk of failure for leaders |
| **Shakeout** | Slowing | Intense competition, price cuts, excess capacity | Profits squeezed; weak firms fail or merge; cost cutting |
| **Mature** | Slow, near GDP growth | Few surviving firms, stable shares, high barriers to entry, less price competition | Stable margins and cash flow; low capital needs, high payouts |
| **Decline** | Negative (substitutes, changing tastes) | Excess capacity, price wars, consolidation | Falling profits; capacity closes and disinvestment |

Not every industry follows the sequence neatly. A mature industry may be renewed by innovation, the stage varies across regions, and the life cycle is a framework rather than a forecast. The analyst should also keep in mind that growth does not guarantee returns: if a growing industry is competitive, profits can be competed away.

## 4. Porter's Five Forces

Michael Porter's framework explains **industry profitability** by assessing five competitive forces. The stronger the forces, the lower the industry's long-run returns.

| Force | Stronger (worse for the industry) when | Weaker (better) when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Threat of new entrants** | Low barriers: little capital needed, no scale advantages, easy distribution access, no brand loyalty or switching costs, light regulation | High barriers: large economies of scale, patents, strong brands, high switching costs, licences, capital intensity |
| **2. Bargaining power of suppliers** | Few suppliers, unique or differentiated inputs, high switching costs, threat of forward integration | Many suppliers, standard inputs, easy switching |
| **3. Bargaining power of buyers** | Few large buyers, undifferentiated products, low switching costs, buyer can integrate backward, price-sensitive | Many small buyers, differentiated products, high switching costs |
| **4. Threat of substitutes** | Close substitutes with attractive price-performance and low switching cost | No close substitutes |
| **5. Intensity of rivalry** | Many equal-sized rivals, slow growth, high fixed costs, undifferentiated products, high exit barriers | Few rivals, a clear leader, fast growth, differentiation |

### Using the framework

- Strong forces **cap prices and raise costs**, so margins and returns on capital stay low (for example, airlines face powerful buyers, intense rivalry and powerful suppliers such as aircraft makers and fuel).
- Weak forces allow **pricing power** and above-average returns (for example, a patented pharmaceutical or a dominant payment network).
- The forces are not static: they shift with technology, regulation and the industry life cycle, so the analyst should consider how they are likely to change over the forecast horizon.

### Threat of entry and barriers

Barriers to entry are the main structural defence of incumbents. Key sources are **economies of scale**, **brand and customer loyalty**, **capital requirements**, **access to distribution**, **proprietary technology or patents**, **government licences** and **network effects**. High barriers support sustainable economic profit; low barriers mean excess returns attract entrants until they vanish.

## 5. External Influences on Industries

Beyond the five forces, the analyst should consider factors outside the industry that shape demand and costs (often grouped as PESTLE).

| Factor | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Macroeconomic** | GDP growth, interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, commodity prices, credit availability |
| **Technological** | Innovation that creates new products, lowers costs or makes existing products obsolete |
| **Demographic** | Age structure, population growth, urbanisation, income distribution |
| **Governmental / legal** | Tax, subsidies, tariffs, regulation, antitrust, licensing |
| **Social** | Changing tastes and lifestyles, health awareness, attitudes to work |
| **Environmental** | Climate policy, resource scarcity, sustainability demands, physical climate risk |

The analyst assesses each factor's **direction, size and timing** for the industry: a rate cut lifts housing demand, an ageing population lifts healthcare demand, and carbon pricing raises costs for heavy emitters.

## 6. Competitive Strategy

A company's **competitive strategy** is how it plans to earn returns above its cost of capital against rivals. Porter describes two generic ways of competing, each applied on a broad or narrow scope:

| Strategy | Idea | Sources | Risks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cost leadership** | Be the lowest-cost producer and earn high margins at industry prices (or win share by cutting prices) | Scale, efficient processes, low-cost inputs, tight overhead control | Competitors copy or leapfrog, quality perception suffers, a price war erodes margins |
| **Differentiation** | Offer something unique so customers pay a premium | Brand, quality, design, service, technology | Premium does not cover extra cost, imitators close the gap |
| **Focus (niche)** | Apply either cost leadership or differentiation to a narrow segment | Specialised knowledge of a segment | Niche is too small or attracts larger entrants |

A company that is "stuck in the middle", neither the cheapest nor clearly differentiated, usually earns weak returns.

### Evaluating a company's position

Analysts examine:

- **Market share** and its trend: the share held by the company in its industry.
- **Relative cost position** and margin versus peers.
- **Sustainability of the competitive advantage** (the "moat"): barriers to imitation, scale and customer lock-in.
- **Pricing power**: ability to raise prices without losing volume.
- **Quality of strategy execution** and the likelihood that rivals respond.

## 7. Industry Concentration

**Industry concentration** measures how the market is divided among firms. It indicates the likely **intensity of competition and pricing power**. A concentrated industry (few large firms) usually has less price competition and higher margins than a fragmented one, although regulators may scrutinise it.

### Concentration ratio

The **N-firm concentration ratio** is the sum of the market shares of the $N$ largest firms:

$$CR_N = s_1 + s_2 + \dots + s_N$$

### Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)

The **HHI** squares each firm's market share (in percent) and sums across all firms in the industry:

$$HHI = \sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i^2$$

It ranges from near 0 (many tiny firms) to 10,000 (a monopoly with 100% share). It gives more weight to large firms than a concentration ratio does. Antitrust authorities typically treat markets with HHI above about 2,500 as highly concentrated, although thresholds vary by regulator and over time.

### Worked example: concentration

Five firms share an industry: 35%, 25%, 20%, 10% and 10%.

1. **Three-firm concentration ratio**: $CR_3 = 35 + 25 + 20 = 80\%$.
2. **HHI**: $35^2 + 25^2 + 20^2 + 10^2 + 10^2 = 1{,}225 + 625 + 400 + 100 + 100 = 2{,}450$.
3. If the 10% firms merge to form a 20% firm: new HHI $= 1{,}225 + 625 + 400 + 400 = 2{,}650$, an increase of **200** (equal to $2 \times 10 \times 10$, twice the product of the merging shares).
4. Interpretation: the industry is already concentrated, and the merger moves it above the usual 2,500 threshold, which would invite regulatory attention.

Concentration measures should be used with care: they ignore potential entry, imports, differentiation and how the market is defined (a narrow market looks more concentrated than a wide one).

## 8. Putting It Together

A sound industry and competitive analysis answers, in order:

1. **What is the industry and who are the peers?** (classification and peer group)
2. **How sensitive is it to the cycle, and where is it in its life cycle?** (growth, margin and capital-needs outlook)
3. **How attractive is its structure?** (five forces, barriers, concentration)
4. **What external changes are coming?** (macro, technology, demographics, regulation, social, environmental)
5. **How is the company positioned?** (strategy, market share, cost position, moat)
6. **What does this imply for forecasts?** (revenue growth, margins, capex, return on capital) and so for valuation.

## Key takeaways

- Commercial systems (GICS, ICB, TRBC) classify by **principal business activity**; peer groups should share demand drivers, cost structure and correlation of returns.
- **Cyclical** industries have earnings that swing with the economy; **defensive** industries have stable demand.
- The **life cycle** runs embryonic, growth, shakeout, mature, decline; growth, competition and profit patterns differ in each.
- **Porter's five forces**: new entrants, supplier power, buyer power, substitutes and rivalry. Strong forces depress industry returns.
- Competitive advantage comes from **cost leadership**, **differentiation** or **focus**; being stuck in the middle is weakest.
- **Concentration** is measured by $CR_N$ and the $HHI = \sum s_i^2$; high concentration generally means more pricing power and less price competition.
- The conclusions feed forecasts of growth, margins and returns on capital.
