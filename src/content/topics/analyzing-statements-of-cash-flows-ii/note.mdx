# Analyzing Statements of Cash Flows II

The first chapter on this statement covered how it is built. This chapter covers how to **analyze** it: reading the pattern of operating, investing and financing cash flows, common-size statements, **free cash flow** measures and **cash flow ratios**. The same data are used throughout the worked example in Section 6.

---

## 1. Reading the Statement: Patterns and Quality

Look at the sign and size of CFO, CFI and CFF together, over several years.

| Pattern | Typical interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| **CFO positive, large relative to net income** | Earnings convert to cash; check that growth in payables is not what is propping CFO up |
| **CFO persistently below net income** | Possible low earnings quality: rising receivables or inventory, aggressive revenue recognition |
| **CFO positive and covers capex and dividends** | Self-funding: growth and payouts do not need external capital |
| **CFO negative, funded by debt or equity issuance (CFF positive)** | Common for start-ups or distressed firms; sustainability depends on reaching positive CFO |
| **CFI positive from asset sales, CFO weak** | May be selling assets to survive; not a repeatable source of cash |
| **CFF strongly negative with positive CFO** | Mature firm returning cash through debt repayment, dividends or buybacks |

Questions to ask:

1. What are the **main sources** of cash: operations, borrowing, or asset sales? Operations should be the dominant, recurring source.
2. Is **CFO** trending with net income? A widening gap is a warning sign.
3. How much **capital expenditure** is needed to maintain, versus expand, the business?
4. Are dividends and buybacks **covered** by operating cash flow?

A **non-recurring** item (a legal settlement, a large working capital release) can distort a single year's CFO, so analysts look at several periods.

## 2. Common-Size Cash Flow Statements

A **common-size** cash flow statement expresses each line as a percentage of a base, which makes companies and years comparable. There are two methods.

| Method | Base | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Total inflows / total outflows** | Each inflow as a percentage of **total cash inflows**; each outflow as a percentage of **total cash outflows** | Shows the *mix* of funding sources and uses of cash |
| **Revenue** | Each item as a percentage of **net revenue** | Shows how much of each unit of sales becomes (or consumes) cash; comparable to common-size income statements |

Common-size analysis shows trends: for instance, a falling CFO as a percentage of revenue, or a rising share of cash inflows coming from borrowing.

## 3. Free Cash Flow

**Free cash flow** is cash available to capital providers after the firm has paid for the investment it needs. Two versions matter:

- **FCFF** (free cash flow to the firm): cash available to **all** capital providers (debt and equity) after operating costs, taxes, and investment.
- **FCFE** (free cash flow to equity): cash available to **common shareholders** after also paying debt service and after net borrowing.

Let FCInv be fixed capital investment (capital expenditure net of proceeds from asset sales), WCInv the investment in net working capital, NCC non-cash charges (mainly depreciation), $t$ the tax rate, and $Int$ the interest expense.

### FCFF

From **CFO** (U.S. GAAP, with interest paid in CFO):

$$FCFF = CFO + Int(1-t) - FCInv$$

Interest is added back **net of tax** because FCFF is the cash flow before payments to lenders, and CFO has been reduced by interest paid. Only the after-tax amount is added back, because the tax saving on interest stays in the firm's taxes.

Under **IFRS**, interest paid may instead be classified in **financing**, so CFO is already stated before interest. In that case CFO is higher by the full interest paid, and only the tax shield needs adjusting:

$$FCFF = CFO_{\text{IFRS}} - Int \cdot t - FCInv \quad (\text{interest paid in CFF})$$

Textbook shortcuts often write this simply as $CFO - FCInv$, which ignores the interest tax shield; the equations above stay consistent with the other forms below.

Equivalent forms starting from other lines:

$$FCFF = NI + NCC + Int(1-t) - FCInv - WCInv$$

$$FCFF = EBIT(1-t) + Dep - FCInv - WCInv$$

$$FCFF = EBITDA(1-t) + Dep \cdot t - FCInv - WCInv$$

### FCFE

$$FCFE = CFO - FCInv + \text{Net borrowing} \quad (\text{interest paid in CFO})$$

$$FCFE = FCFF - Int(1-t) + \text{Net borrowing}$$

where net borrowing $=$ debt issued $-$ debt repaid. FCFE is what remains for shareholders after the firm has met its debt obligations and funded its investment; it may be paid out as dividends and buybacks or retained.

### Why they matter

Free cash flow measures are the cash inputs to discounted cash flow valuation (see the equity valuation chapters). Negative FCFF is not necessarily bad for a growing firm that invests heavily, but it must be financed externally.

## 4. Cash Flow Performance Ratios

These ratios measure how well the firm generates cash from its operations.

| Ratio | Formula | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cash flow to revenue** | $CFO / \text{Net revenue}$ | Cash generated per unit of sales |
| **Cash return on assets** | $CFO / \text{Average total assets}$ | Cash earned on the asset base |
| **Cash return on equity** | $CFO / \text{Average shareholders' equity}$ | Cash earned for owners |
| **Cash to income** | $CFO / \text{Operating income}$ | How well operating income converts into cash |
| **Cash flow per share** | $\dfrac{CFO - \text{Preferred dividends}}{\text{Weighted average shares}}$ | Operating cash per common share |

A **cash to income** ratio well below 1.0 over several years suggests low earnings quality, or a growing business that ties up cash in working capital. Note that CFO can be raised temporarily by delaying payments to suppliers, so check the underlying working capital changes.

---

## 5. Cash Flow Coverage Ratios

These ratios measure whether cash flow is sufficient to meet obligations and investment needs. Higher is better.

| Ratio | Formula | Measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Debt coverage** | $CFO / \text{Total debt}$ | Fraction of debt that one year of CFO could repay |
| **Interest coverage** | $\dfrac{CFO + \text{Interest paid} + \text{Taxes paid}}{\text{Interest paid}}$ | Ability to pay interest from pre-interest, pre-tax cash flow |
| **Reinvestment** | $CFO / \text{Cash paid for long-term assets}$ | Ability to fund capital expenditure internally |
| **Debt payment** | $CFO / \text{Cash paid for long-term debt repayment}$ | Ability to meet scheduled principal repayments |
| **Dividend payment** | $CFO / \text{Dividends paid}$ | Cushion for dividends |
| **Investing and financing** | $CFO / \text{Cash outflows for investing and financing}$ | Ability to cover all investing and financing outflows without external capital |

The interest coverage ratio adds interest and taxes back to CFO because CFO is measured **after** them under U.S. GAAP; the numerator is cash flow available before interest and tax.

Limits of these ratios:

- A ratio is a single year; use trends and peers.
- Reliance on CFO ignores that capex and dividends can be cut in a downturn, whereas debt payments cannot.
- Classification differences (IFRS versus U.S. GAAP) change CFO, so adjust before comparing.

## 6. Worked Example

All figures are in millions, year-end. A company reports (U.S. GAAP):

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Net revenue | 2,000 |
| EBITDA | 480 |
| Depreciation | 100 |
| EBIT (operating income) | 380 |
| Interest expense (= interest paid) | 40 |
| Income tax (= taxes paid), 30% of 340 | 102 |
| Net income | 238 |
| Investment in net working capital (WCInv) | 18 |
| Capital expenditure (FCInv; no asset sales) | 260 |
| Long-term debt issued / repaid | 110 / 60 |
| Dividends paid (common 60, preferred 20) | 80 |

Weighted average shares: 100 million. Average total assets: 3,200. Average equity: 1,600. Total debt: 800.

### Step 1: CFO and the cash flow statement

$$CFO = NI + Dep - WCInv = 238 + 100 - 18 = 320$$

$$CFI = -260, \qquad CFF = +110 - 60 - 80 = -30$$

$$\Delta\text{Cash} = 320 - 260 - 30 = +30$$

### Step 2: FCFF four ways

1. From CFO: $320 + 40(1-0.30) - 260 = 320 + 28 - 260 = 88$
2. From net income: $238 + 100 + 28 - 260 - 18 = 88$
3. From EBIT: $380(0.70) + 100 - 260 - 18 = 266 + 100 - 278 = 88$
4. From EBITDA: $480(0.70) + 100(0.30) - 260 - 18 = 336 + 30 - 278 = 88$

All four give **FCFF $= 88$**.

### Step 3: FCFE

$$FCFE = CFO - FCInv + \text{Net borrowing} = 320 - 260 + (110 - 60) = 110$$

Check: $FCFF - Int(1-t) + \text{Net borrowing} = 88 - 28 + 50 = 110$. ✓

### Step 4: Performance ratios

| Ratio | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash flow to revenue | $320 / 2{,}000$ | 16.0% |
| Cash return on assets | $320 / 3{,}200$ | 10.0% |
| Cash return on equity | $320 / 1{,}600$ | 20.0% |
| Cash to income | $320 / 380$ | 0.84 |
| Cash flow per share | $(320 - 20) / 100$ | 3.00 |

### Step 5: Coverage ratios

| Ratio | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Debt coverage | $320 / 800$ | 0.40 |
| Interest coverage | $(320 + 40 + 102) / 40 = 462 / 40$ | 11.55 |
| Reinvestment | $320 / 260$ | 1.23 |
| Debt payment | $320 / 60$ | 5.33 |
| Dividend payment | $320 / 80$ | 4.00 |
| Investing and financing | $320 / (260 + 60 + 80) = 320 / 400$ | 0.80 |

The investing and financing ratio is below 1.0, which means CFO alone did not cover every investing and financing outflow; the company needed the 110 of new debt (net change in cash was still +30). Reinvestment above 1.0 shows capex is covered by CFO.

### Step 6: Common-size (inflows/outflows method)

Inflows: CFO 320 and debt issued 110, total 430. Outflows: capex 260, debt repaid 60 and dividends 80, total 400.

| Inflow | % of inflows | Outflow | % of outflows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CFO | $320/430 = 74.4\%$ | Capex | $260/400 = 65.0\%$ |
| Debt issued | $110/430 = 25.6\%$ | Debt repaid | $60/400 = 15.0\%$ |
| | | Dividends | $80/400 = 20.0\%$ |

Operations supply about three-quarters of the cash, and capital expenditure is the largest use.

### Effect of an IFRS choice

If the same company classified the 40 of interest paid in **financing** under IFRS, CFO would be $320 + 40 = 360$ and CFF would be $-30 - 40 = -70$. The cash change is still $360 - 260 - 70 = +30$. FCFF is unchanged at $360 - 40(0.30) - 260 = 360 - 12 - 260 = 88$. Only the classification moves, which is why CFO-based ratios (for example, cash flow to revenue of $360/2{,}000 = 18.0\%$ instead of 16.0%) must be put on a consistent basis before comparing companies.

---

## Key takeaways

- Analyze CFO, CFI and CFF **together over time**; CFO should come mainly from operations and track net income.
- **Common-size** statements use total inflows/outflows or revenue as the base.
- $FCFF = CFO + Int(1-t) - FCInv$; $FCFE = CFO - FCInv + \text{Net borrowing}$. If interest paid is already in financing (IFRS), CFO excludes interest, so only the interest tax shield needs adjusting.
- FCFF can also be built from net income, EBIT or EBITDA and gives the same answer when inputs are consistent.
- **Performance ratios** (cash flow to revenue, cash return on assets and equity, cash to income, cash flow per share) show cash generation; **coverage ratios** (debt, interest, reinvestment, debt payment, dividend, investing and financing) show cash sufficiency.
- Interest coverage uses $(CFO + \text{interest} + \text{taxes}) / \text{interest}$.
- Adjust for IFRS versus U.S. GAAP classification before comparing companies.
