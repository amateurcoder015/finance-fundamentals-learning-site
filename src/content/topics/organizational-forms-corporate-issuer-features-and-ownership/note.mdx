# Organizational Forms, Corporate Issuer Features, and Ownership

A **corporate issuer** is a company that raises capital by issuing financial securities such as shares and bonds. How the business is legally organized determines who owns it, who is liable for its debts, how its profits are taxed, how it can raise capital, and how easily owners can exit. This chapter compares the main organizational forms, the features of corporations as issuers, public versus private ownership, and the differing claims of shareholders and lenders.

---

## 1. Organizational Forms of Business

| Form | Ownership and control | Liability of owners | Taxation | Continuity and transfer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sole proprietorship** | One individual owns and runs the business; no legal separation between owner and business | **Unlimited** personal liability | Profit is taxed as the owner's personal income (**pass-through**) | Ends with the owner; hard to transfer |
| **General partnership** | Two or more partners share ownership and management | **Unlimited, joint and several** liability for each general partner | Pass-through to the partners | Tied to the partners; changes in partners can dissolve it |
| **Limited partnership (LP)** | **General partners** manage and carry unlimited liability; **limited partners** contribute capital and do not manage | General partners: unlimited. Limited partners: **limited to the capital they invested** | Generally pass-through | Limited partnership interests are transferable only as the agreement allows |
| **Corporation** | Shareholders own; a board of directors oversees; managers run daily operations | **Limited** to the capital invested | The company itself is generally taxed on profits, and shareholders are taxed again on dividends (**double taxation**) in many jurisdictions | Perpetual existence; shares are transferable |
| **Limited liability company (LLC) or similar hybrid** | Owners ("members") may manage directly or appoint managers | Limited | Often pass-through, depending on jurisdiction and election | Flexible, set by the operating agreement |

The exact names and tax rules vary by country, but the trade-offs are consistent:

- More **owner liability** and a simpler structure at the sole proprietorship end.
- More **limited liability**, easier **access to capital**, and more formal governance and disclosure at the corporation end.

<Note>
Choosing a form is a trade-off among liability, taxation, control, ease of raising capital and ease of transferring ownership. No single form wins on every dimension.
</Note>

## 2. Features of a Corporate Issuer

A **corporation** is a separate legal person that can own assets, sign contracts, borrow, sue and be sued in its own name. Its main features are as follows.

### Legal personality and limited liability

- The corporation's debts belong to the corporation. If it fails, shareholders can lose **at most what they paid for their shares**; their personal assets are protected.
- Creditors can claim only the corporation's assets, which is why lenders examine the company's cash flows and collateral closely.
- Courts may "pierce the corporate veil" in cases of fraud, but this is an exception.

### Separation of ownership and control

- Shareholders elect the **board of directors**, which appoints senior **management**. Owners are often not the day-to-day decision-makers.
- This separation allows professional management and a broad base of owners, but it creates a **principal–agent problem**: managers (agents) may not always act in the interest of shareholders (principals). Governance mechanisms such as an independent board, audits, and compensation tied to results aim to reduce this conflict.

### Taxation

- In many jurisdictions profits are taxed at the **corporate level**, and distributions to shareholders are taxed **again** as dividend income. Interest paid on debt is often tax-deductible, whereas dividends are not.
- Pass-through forms avoid this second layer, but owners pay tax on their share of profits even if the cash is retained in the business.

### Access to capital and continuity

- A corporation can raise equity from many investors by issuing shares and can borrow by issuing bonds or loans, so it can fund larger projects than a sole proprietor or partnership.
- It has **perpetual life**: ownership changes without ending the company, and shares can be sold without the company's consent, which increases their **liquidity**.

### Costs of the corporate form

- Formation, ongoing legal and reporting requirements, board and governance costs, and, if taxed twice, a heavier tax burden.

## 3. Public and Private Companies

| Feature | **Public company** | **Private company** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shares** | Listed and traded on an exchange or other public market | Not publicly traded; held by a limited number of investors; transfers are restricted |
| **Raising equity** | Can sell shares to the general public through an **initial public offering (IPO)** and later offerings | Raises money privately from founders, family, **venture capital**, **private equity**, or strategic investors |
| **Disclosure** | Extensive, regular and audited reporting under securities regulation and exchange rules | Limited; information is shared with owners and lenders as agreed |
| **Liquidity** | Shares can be bought and sold readily at a market price | Illiquid; a sale often depends on a negotiated deal, an IPO or acquisition |
| **Valuation** | Observable market price | Estimated through valuation methods or negotiated transactions |
| **Ownership** | Often widely dispersed; includes institutional and retail investors | Often concentrated among founders, management or a few investors |
| **Costs and control** | Higher compliance, listing and investor-relations costs; scrutiny of short-term results; takeover possible | Lower reporting costs and more flexibility to pursue long-term strategy; limited access to large pools of capital |

### Why companies go public, or go private

- **Go public** to raise large amounts of capital cheaply, provide liquidity and an exit for founders and early investors, create a share price as a currency for acquisitions and employee compensation, and enhance visibility.
- **Go private** (for example, through a **buyout** by private equity) to reduce listing and compliance costs, escape short-term market pressure, restructure away from public scrutiny, or concentrate ownership.

## 4. Ownership Structures

### Dispersed and concentrated ownership

- **Dispersed ownership**: many small shareholders, none with control. Management may dominate decisions, so the principal–agent problem is more pronounced.
- **Concentrated ownership**: a **controlling shareholder** (a family, founder, parent company or the state) holds enough votes to direct the company. This can align management with owners, but the controlling holder's interests can conflict with those of **minority shareholders**.

### Share classes and voting rights

- **Common shares** carry a residual claim and normally vote on major matters.
- Some companies issue **multiple share classes** (for example, Class A with one vote and Class B with ten votes), so founders can keep control while holding a smaller fraction of the economic ownership. This creates a gap between **voting control** and **economic interest**, which increases the risk to minority holders.
- **Preference shares** usually receive fixed dividends with priority over common shares, but often have limited or no voting rights.

### Types of shareholders

- **Retail investors**, **institutional investors** (mutual funds, pension funds, insurers), **founders and management**, **strategic investors** (other companies), and **governments**.
- Institutional investors can influence governance through voting and engagement.

## 5. Claims of Shareholders and Lenders

Corporate issuers fund themselves with a combination of **equity** (owners' capital) and **debt** (borrowed capital). The two groups hold very different claims.

| Feature | **Lenders (creditors, bondholders)** | **Shareholders (equity holders)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Claim on cash flows** | **Fixed**, contractual interest and principal | **Residual**: whatever is left after all other obligations |
| **Priority in liquidation** | Paid **before** shareholders (secured and senior debt first) | Paid last, only if assets remain |
| **Return potential** | Limited to the promised interest and principal | Unlimited upside, but can fall to zero |
| **Risk** | Lower; risk is mainly default and credit risk | Higher; absorbs losses first |
| **Voting and control** | Generally none unless the loan terms (**covenants**) are breached | Vote on directors and major decisions |
| **Main interest** | Safety: the borrower's ability to pay | Growth and profit: the value of the shares |
| **Tax treatment for issuer** | Interest is often tax-deductible | Dividends are paid from after-tax profit |

Because of these differences, lenders prefer the company to take **less risk** and preserve cash for payments, whereas shareholders, with limited downside and unlimited upside, can favor **riskier, higher-return** projects. This **conflict of interest** is why loan agreements contain covenants that restrict additional debt, dividend payments and asset sales.

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example A: Double taxation versus pass-through

A business earns 1,000,000 before tax. The corporate tax rate is 25%, and the dividend tax rate is 20%. An owner of a pass-through business would pay personal tax at 30% on the same profit. All after-tax profit is distributed.

**Corporation**
1. Corporate tax $= 0.25 \times 1{,}000{,}000 = 250{,}000$; after-tax profit $= 750{,}000$
2. Dividend tax $= 0.20 \times 750{,}000 = 150{,}000$; shareholders receive $600{,}000$
3. Total tax $= 250{,}000 + 150{,}000 = 400{,}000$, which is an effective rate of $400{,}000 / 1{,}000{,}000 = 40\%$

**Pass-through (partnership or sole proprietorship)**
1. Personal tax $= 0.30 \times 1{,}000{,}000 = 300{,}000$; owner keeps $700{,}000$

With these rates, the corporate form leaves 100,000 less in the owner's hands. The corporation still offers limited liability and easier access to capital, which may outweigh the tax cost.

### Example B: Limited versus unlimited liability

A business fails with 800,000 in assets (which can be sold at book value) and 1,300,000 of debts. The owners invested 200,000 in total.

- **Sole proprietorship or general partner**: creditors can claim personal assets for the shortfall of $1{,}300{,}000 - 800{,}000 = 500{,}000$, so the owner can lose the 200,000 invested **plus** up to 500,000 more.
- **Corporation (or limited partner)**: the shareholders' loss is limited to the 200,000 invested; the creditors bear the 500,000 shortfall.

### Example C: Voting control versus economic interest

A company has 100 million Class A shares (one vote each) held by the public and 5 million Class A shares held by the founders. The founders also hold 10 million Class B shares (ten votes each). Class A and Class B shares have the same economic rights.

1. Total shares $= 100 + 5 + 10 = 115$ million; founders hold $5 + 10 = 15$ million.
2. Founders' economic interest $= 15 / 115 = 13.04\%$
3. Total votes $= (100 + 5) \times 1 + 10 \times 10 = 105 + 100 = 205$ million
4. Founders' votes $= 5 \times 1 + 10 \times 10 = 105$ million
5. Founders' voting power $= 105 / 205 = 51.22\%$

The founders control the company with a majority of the votes while owning about 13% of the economics.

### Example D: Residual claim in liquidation

A firm is liquidated for 900,000. It owes 600,000 to secured lenders and 200,000 to unsecured lenders, and it has 500,000 in capital contributed by shareholders.

1. Secured lenders are paid 600,000, leaving 300,000.
2. Unsecured lenders are paid 200,000, leaving 100,000.
3. Shareholders receive the residual 100,000, which is a loss of 400,000 on their 500,000 investment.

## Key takeaways

- **Sole proprietorships** and **general partnerships** have unlimited liability and pass-through taxation; **limited partners**, **LLC members** and **corporate shareholders** have limited liability; corporations are separate legal persons with perpetual life and transferable shares.
- Corporations often face **double taxation**, but gain limited liability, access to capital and liquidity.
- Separation of ownership and control creates **principal–agent** conflicts, addressed through governance.
- **Public** companies have listed shares, extensive disclosure, liquidity and observable prices; **private** companies have limited disclosure, restricted transfer and lower compliance cost.
- Ownership may be **dispersed** or **concentrated**; **dual-class shares** separate voting control from economic interest.
- **Lenders** hold fixed, senior claims and want safety; **shareholders** hold the residual claim with unlimited upside, so their interests can conflict.
