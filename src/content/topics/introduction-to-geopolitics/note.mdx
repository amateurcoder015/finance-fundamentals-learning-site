# Introduction to Geopolitics

**Geopolitics** is the study of how geography, politics, power and national interests shape the behaviour of states and the relationships between them. For an investor it matters because political decisions (a tariff, a sanction, an election result, a conflict) change cash flows, discount rates and the rules under which companies and markets operate. This chapter covers the actors in geopolitics, cooperation versus competition, globalization and nationalism, the tools of geopolitics, the types of geopolitical risk, and a framework for judging how a risk could affect an investment portfolio.

---

## 1. What Geopolitics Is and Who the Actors Are

Geopolitics looks at three questions: *who* holds power, *what* they want, and *how* they pursue it. The answers are shaped by lasting features such as geography (access to ports and resources, borders), demographics, economic strength, political systems and history.

### Actors

| Actor | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nation-states** | Governments with sovereign authority over a territory; the main actors | Governments, central banks, militaries |
| **Non-state actors** | Organisations that influence events without being a state | Multinational companies, international organisations (UN, WTO, IMF), NGOs, terrorist and criminal groups |
| **Individuals** | Persons whose decisions carry large consequences | Heads of state, influential leaders, activists |

States act in their **national interest**, usually described as security, economic prosperity, and political stability and influence. Different states weigh these goals differently, and the same state may weigh them differently over time, which is why geopolitical outcomes are hard to predict.

<Note>
Geopolitics is not the same as politics. Domestic politics concerns decisions *within* a country (budgets, regulation). Geopolitics concerns relations *between* countries and the balance of power among them, although the two interact: a domestic election can change a country's foreign policy.
</Note>

## 2. Cooperation Versus Competition

States interact along a spectrum from cooperation to competition.

| | **Cooperation** | **Competition** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | Shared gains; solve common problems | Relative gains; advance one's own position |
| Forms | Alliances, trade agreements, treaties, joint institutions | Rivalry for resources, markets, technology, territory or influence |
| Typical tools | Negotiation, free-trade areas, common standards | Tariffs, sanctions, military build-up, technology controls |
| Market effect | Lower trade costs, stable rules, lower risk premia | Higher uncertainty, supply-chain disruption, higher risk premia |

Cooperation can be **bilateral** (two states) or **multilateral** (many states, often through an institution). Most relationships mix both: two countries may be strategic rivals and still be each other's large trading partners.

Whether the world is trending toward cooperation or competition shapes the whole investment environment. A cooperative phase tends to favour open trade and low-cost global supply chains; a competitive phase tends to favour domestic production, security of supply and national champions.

## 3. Globalization and Nationalism

**Globalization** is the growing integration of economies through the cross-border movement of goods and services, capital, people, technology and ideas.

**Benefits** (broadly): lower prices, greater choice, access to larger markets and capital, specialisation by comparative advantage, and faster spread of technology. **Costs**: job losses and wage pressure in exposed industries, uneven gains within and between countries, dependence on foreign suppliers, and faster transmission of shocks.

**Nationalism** (and its economic form, protectionism or economic nationalism) puts national interests ahead of international integration. It can appear as trade barriers, restrictions on foreign investment, subsidies for domestic industry, or demands to bring production back home (**reshoring**).

| | **Globalization** | **Nationalism / protectionism** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trade | Open, rules-based | Tariffs, quotas, local-content rules |
| Capital | Free cross-border flows | Foreign-ownership limits, capital controls |
| Supply chains | Efficient, global, just-in-time | Resilient, shorter, regionalised ("friend-shoring") |
| Typical investor impact | Margin gains from low-cost sourcing; broad diversification | Higher input costs; domestic winners and foreign losers |

The two forces are cyclical rather than permanent. Public support for each rises and falls with economic conditions, perceived fairness and security concerns. Investors track which force is dominant because it influences trade volumes, inflation, currency values and corporate margins.

## 4. Tools of Geopolitics

States use a range of tools to pursue their interests. They can be grouped into three families.

### National security tools

Instruments aimed at defence and strategic advantage:

- **Military force** and the threat of it (deterrence), including alliances such as collective-defence treaties.
- **Intelligence and espionage**, and **cyber operations** against infrastructure, companies or governments.
- **Diplomacy** and security guarantees; arms sales and export of defence technology.

Effect on markets: spikes in volatility, higher commodity prices where supply is threatened (energy, grains), flight to safe assets, and rising defence and cyber-security spending.

### Economic tools

Instruments that use trade and investment as leverage:

| Tool | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Tariffs** | Taxes on imports; raise domestic prices and protect local producers |
| **Quotas and embargoes** | Limits on, or a ban of, specified trade |
| **Export controls** | Restrict sale of sensitive goods or technology (for example advanced chips) |
| **Subsidies and state aid** | Support domestic industries against foreign rivals |
| **Foreign-investment screening** | Limits foreign ownership of strategic assets |
| **Economic sanctions** | Penalties on a target country, sector, company or individual |

### Financial tools

Instruments that use the financial system as leverage:

- **Financial sanctions**: freezing assets, banning transactions, cutting a bank off from international payment systems.
- **Capital controls**: limits on money entering or leaving a country.
- **Restrictions on investing in, or lending to**, specified companies or countries; limits on access to capital markets.
- **Currency and reserve policy**: use of reserve-currency status, or of large reserve holdings, as leverage.

<Hl>Economic and financial tools are often preferred over force</Hl> because they are cheaper and reversible, but they have side effects. Tariffs raise costs for domestic consumers and importers; sanctions disrupt trade and funding for the imposing country's firms too; and targets can adapt by finding new partners or suppliers. Retaliation can turn a single measure into an escalating cycle.

## 5. Geopolitical Risk

**Geopolitical risk** is the risk that political events, conflict or policy decisions disrupt the normal functioning of economies and markets, harming investment returns. It can be classified by how predictable the risk is.

| Type | Definition | Predictability | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Event risk** | A *scheduled* event with a known date but uncertain outcome | Timing known, outcome unknown | Elections, referendums, summits, policy deadlines |
| **Exogenous risk** | A *sudden, unexpected* shock from outside the markets | Neither timing nor occurrence known | Terrorist attack, surprise invasion, major cyber attack |
| **Thematic risk** | A *slow-building* trend that unfolds over years | Direction visible, path uncertain | Great-power rivalry, trade fragmentation, resource scarcity, demographic shifts |

Because event risk is scheduled, investors can prepare by building scenarios in advance. Exogenous shocks cannot be forecast, so the focus is on resilience (diversification, liquidity). Thematic risks require long-term positioning, since their effects accumulate.

## 6. Assessing the Impact on Investments

A practical framework runs in four steps.

1. **Identify the risk and the actors.** What is the issue, who can act, and what are their interests and constraints?
2. **Assess likelihood, magnitude and timing.** How probable is each outcome, how large would the economic effect be, and how quickly would it arrive and persist?
3. **Trace the transmission channels.** Through which route does the event reach asset prices?
4. **Respond.** Hedge, diversify, resize positions, or accept the risk and be paid for it in return.

### Transmission channels

| Channel | How a geopolitical event reaches portfolios |
| --- | --- |
| **Trade and supply chains** | Tariffs, embargoes, blockades disrupt input supply and raise costs |
| **Commodity prices** | Conflict in producing regions moves oil, gas, metals, food |
| **Inflation and growth** | Higher input prices and uncertainty raise inflation and cut output |
| **Currencies and capital flows** | Safe-haven flows to reserve currencies; capital flight from risky countries |
| **Interest rates and risk premia** | Higher country, equity and credit risk premia; central bank reaction |
| **Company-level effects** | Revenue, cost and asset exposure by region; sanctions compliance; expropriation risk |

Investors usually build a **scenario analysis** rather than a single forecast, assigning probabilities to a few distinct outcomes and estimating the asset impact in each.

### Worked example: probability-weighted scenarios

An equity portfolio manager studies the outcome of trade negotiations between two large economies. Estimated impact on the portfolio over the next year:

| Scenario | Probability | Portfolio impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Escalation (new tariffs and retaliation) | 25% | −12% |
| Status quo (talks continue, no change) | 55% | +2% |
| Agreement (tariffs reduced) | 20% | +6% |

1. Check probabilities: $25\% + 55\% + 20\% = 100\%$ ✓.
2. Expected impact:

$$E[\text{impact}] = 0.25\times(-12\%) + 0.55\times(2\%) + 0.20\times(6\%)$$

3. $= -3.0\% + 1.1\% + 1.2\% = -0.7\%$.

The expected effect is only slightly negative, but the **distribution** is skewed: the downside scenario (−12%) is twice as large as the best upside (+6%). The manager may reduce exposure to the sectors most dependent on cross-border trade or buy protection, even though the probability-weighted figure looks mild.

### Worked example: tariff cost at company level

A manufacturer has revenue of 2,000 and EBIT of 300 (15% margin). It imports inputs costing 600, and a new 15% tariff applies to them. It can raise selling prices enough to recover 40% of the extra cost, with no loss of volume.

1. Extra cost: $600 \times 15\% = 90$.
2. Recovered through price: $40\% \times 90 = 36$.
3. Cost absorbed: $90 - 36 = 54$.
4. New EBIT: $300 - 54 = 246$ (equivalently $300 + 36 - 90$).
5. Change in EBIT: $54 / 300 = 18\%$ decline.

A cost shock equal to 4.5% of revenue ($90/2{,}000$) cuts operating profit by 18% because margins are thin relative to the shock. The result is very sensitive to the pass-through assumption: with no pricing power (0% recovered) EBIT falls by $90/300 = 30\%$.

### Investment implications

- **Exposure analysis**: where does a company earn revenue, source inputs and hold assets? Concentration in one country or supply route is a vulnerability.
- **Diversification** across regions, sectors and asset classes reduces dependence on any one outcome; safe-haven assets (high-quality government bonds, some currencies, gold) can cushion shocks, although correlations can change under stress.
- **Risk premia**: investors should demand higher expected returns (or a lower valuation) for assets with higher geopolitical risk, but should avoid overreacting. Markets often recover quickly from shocks that do not change fundamentals, while thematic shifts can change fundamentals permanently.
- **Monitor and update**: geopolitical situations change quickly, so revisit probabilities as new information arrives.

## Key takeaways

- **Geopolitics** concerns how states pursue national interests; the main actors are **nation-states, non-state actors and individuals**.
- Relations range from **cooperation** (alliances, trade agreements) to **competition** (rivalry, sanctions, arms build-ups), and most relationships contain both.
- **Globalization** integrates trade, capital and people; **nationalism / protectionism** pushes the other way. The balance between them shifts over time and drives trade, inflation and margins.
- Tools fall into **national security**, **economic** (tariffs, quotas, export controls, sanctions) and **financial** (asset freezes, capital controls, payment-system restrictions) families.
- Risk types: **event risk** (scheduled, outcome unknown), **exogenous risk** (sudden, unexpected) and **thematic risk** (slow-building trend).
- Assess **likelihood, magnitude and timing**, trace the **transmission channel**, use **scenario analysis**, and manage exposure through diversification and position sizing.
