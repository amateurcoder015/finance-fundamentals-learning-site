An **option** is one of the most versatile financial instruments in modern capital markets. Unlike futures and forward contracts—which mandate a binding transaction—options introduce flexibility by separating financial rights from financial obligations.

## 1. What is an Option? (Options vs. Futures)

An **option** is a financial derivative contract that grants its buyer the **RIGHT**, but **NOT the obligation**, to buy or sell a specified quantity of an underlying asset at a pre-determined price on or before a specified expiration date.

### The Fundamental Distinction: Options vs. Futures

| Feature | Futures / Forward Contract | Option Contract |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Obligation** | Binding obligation on **both** buyer and seller | Obligation on the **seller only**; buyer holds a choice |
| **Upfront Fee** | None (only margin collateral deposits) | Buyer pays non-refundable **Premium** to seller |
| **Downside Risk** | Symmetric 1-to-1 risk for both parties | Capped at premium paid for buyer; asymmetric |

Because the buyer has the choice to walk away if the market moves unfavorably, this privilege must be purchased by paying an upfront fee called the **Option Premium**.

## 2. The Two Types of Options: Calls and Puts

All option contracts belong to one of two fundamental categories:

### 1. Call Option (Right to BUY)
A **Call Option** grants the buyer the right to **BUY** the underlying asset at the agreed strike price.
- **Bullish Outlook**: Investors purchase calls when they expect the underlying asset's price to **rise**.
- **Intuition**: If the market price jumps to \$150, holding a call to buy at \$100 allows you to purchase the asset far below market value.

### 2. Put Option (Right to SELL)
A **Put Option** grants the buyer the right to **SELL** the underlying asset at the agreed strike price.
- **Bearish Outlook / Hedging**: Investors purchase puts when they expect the underlying asset's price to **fall** (or to protect an existing stock portfolio against market crashes).
- **Intuition**: If the market price collapses to \$60, holding a put to sell at \$100 allows you to exit your position far above market value.

## 3. The Two Sides of Every Option Trade

Every option transaction requires two counterparties with opposing rights and risk profiles:

### 1. Option Buyer / Holder (Long Position)
- **Pays** the option premium to the seller at trade inception.
- **Acquires the RIGHT** to exercise or abandon the contract.
- **Risk Profile**: Maximum downside loss is strictly limited to the premium paid ($-\text{Premium}$). Upside profit potential is large or unlimited.

### 2. Option Seller / Writer (Short Position)
- **Receives** the option premium upfront.
- **Assumes the OBLIGATION** to fulfill the trade if the buyer chooses to exercise.
- **Risk Profile**: Maximum profit is capped at the premium received ($+\text{Premium}$). Downside loss is large or unlimited.

## 4. Key Options Terminology

To navigate options trading and analysis, master these core definitions:

- **Strike Price (Exercise Price, $K$)**: The fixed, agreed-upon price per share at which the buyer can exercise their right to buy or sell the asset.
- **Option Premium**: The market price paid by the buyer to the seller for acquiring the option contract.
- **Expiration Date (Expiry)**: The final date on which the option contract is valid; after expiry, the option becomes worthless.
- **European vs. American Exercise Style**:
  - **European-Style Option**: Can only be exercised on its exact expiration date.
  - **American-Style Option**: Can be exercised at any time up to and including its expiration date.

## 5. Moneyness: ITM, ATM, and OTM

**Moneyness** describes the intrinsic profitability of an option if it were exercised immediately at current market prices, comparing the current **Spot Price ($S$)** to the **Strike Price ($K$)**.

```
In-the-Money (ITM)    → Exercising yields positive intrinsic value
At-the-Money (ATM)    → Spot price equals Strike price (S = K)
Out-of-the-Money (OTM) → Exercising yields zero intrinsic value (worthless if expired today)
```

### 1. Call Option Moneyness Rules
- **In-the-Money (ITM Call)**: $S > K$ (Spot price is *above* strike price). You can buy below current market price.
- **At-the-Money (ATM Call)**: $S = K$ (Spot price *equals* strike price).
- **Out-of-the-Money (OTM Call)**: $S < K$ (Spot price is *below* strike price). Buying at strike would cost more than current market price.

### 2. Put Option Moneyness Rules
- **In-the-Money (ITM Put)**: $S < K$ (Spot price is *below* strike price). You can sell above current market price.
- **At-the-Money (ATM Put)**: $S = K$ (Spot price *equals* strike price).
- **Out-of-the-Money (OTM Put)**: $S > K$ (Spot price is *above* strike price). Selling at strike would receive less than current market price.

## 6. Worked Example: Moneyness Classification

Suppose a stock currently trades at a **Spot Price ($S$) of \$100**. The table below classifies Call and Put options across three different strike prices ($K$):

| Strike Price ($K$) | Call Option ($S = \$100$) | Put Option ($S = \$100$) | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **\$90 Strike** | **In-the-Money (ITM)** | **Out-of-the-Money (OTM)** | Call allows buying at \$90 (Save \$10); Put allows selling at \$90 (Worse than spot). |
| **\$100 Strike** | **At-the-Money (ATM)** | **At-the-Money (ATM)** | Strike equals current spot price exactly ($S = K$). |
| **\$110 Strike** | **Out-of-the-Money (OTM)** | **In-the-Money (ITM)** | Call allows buying at \$110 (Worse than spot); Put allows selling at \$110 (Gain \$10). |

### Key Rule of Thumb
For any given spot and strike price, if a **Call is In-the-Money**, the corresponding **Put at the same strike is Out-of-the-Money** (and vice versa).
