# Basics of Portfolio Planning and Construction

Portfolio management starts with **planning**: before any security is bought, the manager needs a written, agreed description of what the client wants and what limits apply. That document is the **investment policy statement (IPS)**. From it flow the **strategic asset allocation** and, in the execution step, the **construction** of the actual portfolio. This chapter covers the IPS, the main approaches to asset allocation and portfolio construction, and how environmental, social and governance (ESG) considerations enter the process.

---

## 1. The Investment Policy Statement

An **investment policy statement** is a written planning document that governs the relationship between the client (the asset owner) and the manager. It is prepared for individuals and institutions alike.

Why an IPS is valuable:

- It forces the client and manager to **agree on objectives and constraints** before markets move.
- It gives the manager a **benchmark for judging** decisions and results, and protects the client from the manager drifting away from the mandate.
- It guards against **emotional, ad hoc decisions** in stressed markets, because the response is already written down.
- It must be **reviewed periodically** and whenever the client's circumstances change materially.

### Typical IPS contents

| Section | What it records |
| --- | --- |
| Introduction | Background on the client and the purpose of the portfolio |
| Statement of duties and responsibilities | Roles of the client, the manager and any custodian |
| Procedures | How the IPS is updated and how conflicts or exceptions are handled |
| Objectives | **Return** and **risk** objectives |
| Constraints | **Liquidity**, **time horizon**, **tax**, **legal and regulatory**, **unique circumstances** |
| Investment guidelines | Permitted and prohibited assets, use of leverage, derivatives and short selling |
| Evaluation and review | Benchmarks, reporting frequency, how performance is judged |
| Appendices | Strategic asset allocation, rebalancing policy |

## 2. Objectives: Risk and Return

### Risk objectives

**Risk tolerance** combines two different ideas, and the **lower** of the two governs the portfolio:

| Component | Meaning | Depends on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ability to take risk** | The client's capacity to absorb losses without failing to meet goals. Largely objective | Wealth relative to spending needs, time horizon, income stability, liabilities |
| **Willingness to take risk** | The client's psychological comfort with volatility and loss. Subjective | Personality, past experience, attitude to uncertainty |

A client with high ability but low willingness (or the reverse) should be treated as having the **lower** risk tolerance overall. Risk objectives can be **absolute** (for example, "no more than a 10% loss in any year", a maximum standard deviation or value at risk) or **relative** (for example, **tracking risk** of 2% against a benchmark).

### Return objectives

Return objectives can be framed in several ways:

- **Capital preservation**: keep the real or nominal value of the portfolio; low return, low risk.
- **Capital appreciation**: grow the portfolio's value over time.
- **Current income**: generate cash flow to spend.
- **Total return**: a combination of appreciation and income.
- **Absolute return**: a stated return target regardless of markets. **Relative return**: outperform a benchmark by a stated margin.

A required return is often built up from the pieces the portfolio must cover:

$$\text{Required return} \approx \text{Spending rate} + \text{Inflation} + \text{Costs}$$

The exact figure compounds the components: $(1+\text{spending})(1+\text{inflation})(1+\text{costs}) - 1$.

<Note>
A client's **desired** return may exceed what is consistent with their risk tolerance. The IPS must resolve this conflict. The risk objective constrains the return objective, not the other way round.
</Note>

## 3. Constraints

Constraints are the practical limits on what the portfolio can do.

- **Liquidity**: the need for cash to pay expenses, fund withdrawals or meet liabilities. Higher liquidity needs push the portfolio toward cash and marketable securities and away from illiquid private assets.
- **Time horizon**: how long until the money is needed. Longer horizons generally allow more risk and illiquidity; a horizon may be **multi-stage** (for example, accumulation then retirement).
- **Tax**: the client's tax status affects after-tax returns, the choice of vehicles and the cost of rebalancing. Taxable and tax-exempt investors will hold different portfolios.
- **Legal and regulatory**: laws, rules and mandates, such as concentration limits for a pension plan or an insurer's solvency requirements.
- **Unique circumstances**: anything else particular to the client, such as ethical or ESG exclusions, concentrated holdings in an employer's stock, or special needs of dependants.

| Client | Typical risk and constraint profile |
| --- | --- |
| Young professional, stable income | High ability to take risk, long horizon, low liquidity needs |
| Retiree living off the portfolio | Lower ability, shorter horizon, high liquidity and income needs |
| Defined-benefit pension plan | Liability-driven, return must cover fixed future payments, strict legal limits |
| University endowment | Perpetual horizon, spending rule, high tolerance for illiquid assets |
| Life insurer | Long, predictable liabilities, regulatory capital rules, low risk tolerance |

## 4. Approaches to Asset Allocation

The **strategic asset allocation (SAA)** sets long-term target weights for each asset class, based on the IPS and capital market expectations. It is the main driver of long-run risk and return and is changed only when objectives, constraints or long-term expectations change.

### Asset-only approach

The allocation is chosen from the **assets' risk, return and correlations** alone, without modelling liabilities explicitly. It suits individuals and endowments, often by choosing the point on an efficient frontier that fits the client's risk tolerance.

### Liability-relative approach

The allocation is built around **liabilities that must be met**, such as pension benefits or insurance claims. The goal is to keep assets sufficient to fund liabilities, so risk is measured by the **funded ratio** and the **surplus**:

$$\text{Funded ratio} = \frac{\text{Market value of assets}}{\text{Present value of liabilities}}, \qquad \text{Surplus} = \text{Assets} - \text{PV of liabilities}$$

A funded ratio below 100% means a shortfall. Methods include **liability-driven investing** (matching the cash flows or interest rate sensitivity of assets to liabilities) and **cash flow matching**.

### Goals-based approach

The portfolio is divided into **sub-portfolios (buckets)**, each aimed at a particular goal with its own time horizon and required probability of success. For example:

| Bucket | Goal | Typical assets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Security | Essential spending and emergencies | Cash, short-term high-quality bonds |
| Lifestyle | Maintaining standard of living | Balanced bonds and equities |
| Aspirational | Legacy, philanthropy, luxury goals | Equities, alternatives |

Each bucket's risk is matched to how important and how near its goal is, with higher-priority goals funded with lower-risk assets. The total may be less efficient in a mean-variance sense, but it maps clearly to what the client cares about.

### Strategic versus tactical allocation

- **Strategic asset allocation**: long-term policy weights (the "policy portfolio" or "benchmark" allocation).
- **Tactical asset allocation (TAA)**: short-term, active **deviations** from the policy weights to exploit perceived mispricing or near-term views. TAA adds **active risk** and must stay within ranges set in the IPS.

## 5. Portfolio Construction Approaches

### Top-down versus bottom-up

| Approach | Starting point | Process |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Top-down** | Macroeconomic and capital market views | Decide asset class, country, sector weights first, then select securities to implement them |
| **Bottom-up** | Individual securities | Pick securities on their own merits (valuation, quality), then let the portfolio's weights result from those choices |

### Active, passive and combinations

- **Passive management** holds a benchmark's constituents, aiming to match it at **low cost**. It rests on the view that markets are broadly efficient.
- **Active management** tries to **outperform** through security selection and timing, at higher fees. Active risk (**tracking risk**) is accepted for the chance of positive **alpha**.
- **Core-satellite** combines a low-cost passive or enhanced-index **core** with a smaller set of active **satellite** positions. It controls total fees while giving active managers a place to add value.
- **Factor-based (smart beta)** portfolios tilt toward characteristics such as value, size, momentum, quality or low volatility, using rules-based methods at lower cost than discretionary active management. They sit between pure passive and pure active.
- **Risk parity** sets weights so that each asset class (or factor) contributes an **equal share of portfolio risk**, rather than equal capital. This usually means large allocations to lower-volatility assets such as bonds, often with leverage.

<Hl>Active share and tracking risk</Hl> measure how different an active portfolio is from its benchmark. A portfolio close to its benchmark has low tracking risk, and its fees should be low to match.

### Implementation vehicles and costs

Execution decisions include whether to use **pooled vehicles** (mutual funds, ETFs), **separately managed accounts**, or **direct securities**, and whether to implement exposure with **derivatives**. Costs (fees, bid-ask spreads, market impact, taxes) reduce the net return and should be weighed against expected benefits.

## 6. ESG Considerations

**Environmental, social and governance (ESG)** factors can affect risk, return and client preferences. ESG can be incorporated in several ways, and the IPS should state which, if any, apply.

| Approach | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Negative screening** (exclusionary) | Excludes sectors or companies (for example, tobacco or weapons) based on values or norms |
| **Positive screening / best-in-class** | Includes the companies with the best ESG ratings, often within each sector |
| **Thematic investing** | Targets themes such as clean energy, water or gender diversity |
| **ESG integration** | Systematically incorporates material ESG factors into traditional financial analysis and valuation |
| **Engagement / active ownership** | Uses voting and dialogue with companies to change behaviour |
| **Impact investing** | Seeks measurable social or environmental benefit **alongside** a financial return |

Exclusions reduce the opportunity set and can create **tracking risk** against an unscreened benchmark. ESG integration aims to improve risk-adjusted returns by capturing material risks that conventional analysis may miss.

## 7. Worked Examples

### Example A: Required return

A retired investor holds 2,000,000 and spends 80,000 a year. Expected inflation is 3.0% and total investment costs are 0.5% a year.

1. Spending rate $= 80{,}000 / 2{,}000{,}000 = 4.0\%$
2. Additive approximation: $4.0\% + 3.0\% + 0.5\% = 7.5\%$
3. Exact compounding: $(1.04)(1.03)(1.005) - 1 = 1.076556 - 1 = 7.66\%$

The portfolio must earn about **7.66%** nominal to sustain spending that rises with inflation and cover costs. If this exceeds what the client's risk tolerance allows, the IPS must be revisited (reduce spending, lower the target, or accept more risk).

### Example B: Core-satellite blend

An investor puts 70% in a passive core (expected gross return 8.0%, cost 0.10%) and 30% in an active satellite (expected gross return 10.5%, cost 1.00%).

1. Core net return $= 8.0\% - 0.10\% = 7.90\%$
2. Satellite net return $= 10.5\% - 1.00\% = 9.50\%$
3. Portfolio net return $= 0.70 \times 7.90\% + 0.30 \times 9.50\% = 5.53\% + 2.85\% = \mathbf{8.38\%}$
4. Check by cost: weighted cost $= 0.70 \times 0.10\% + 0.30 \times 1.00\% = 0.37\%$. Gross $= 0.70 \times 8.0\% + 0.30 \times 10.5\% = 8.75\%$. Net $= 8.75\% - 0.37\% = 8.38\%$ ✓

### Example C: Value added by tactical allocation

Strategic weights are 50% equities, 40% bonds, 10% alternatives. The manager tilts to 55% equities, 35% bonds, 10% alternatives. Over the year equities return 12% and bonds 4%.

1. Active weights: equities $+5\%$, bonds $-5\%$, alternatives $0\%$
2. Value added $= 0.05 \times 12\% + (-0.05) \times 4\% + 0 = 0.60\% - 0.20\% = \mathbf{0.40\%}$

The tilt added 0.40% because the overweighted asset outperformed the underweighted one. Had bonds beaten equities, the same tilt would have lost money.

### Example D: Funded ratio

A pension plan has assets of 950 million and the present value of its liabilities is 1,000 million.

1. Funded ratio $= 950 / 1{,}000 = 95\%$
2. Surplus $= 950 - 1{,}000 = -50$ million (a deficit)

A liability-relative manager would focus on closing the gap and on keeping assets' interest rate sensitivity close to the liabilities', rather than on maximizing return against a market index.

## Key takeaways

- The **IPS** records objectives (return and risk) and constraints (liquidity, horizon, tax, legal and regulatory, unique), and guides every later decision.
- **Risk tolerance** = the lower of **ability** and **willingness** to take risk.
- Required return is roughly **spending + inflation + costs**; desired return must be consistent with risk tolerance.
- **SAA** sets long-term policy weights; **TAA** is a limited, active deviation from them.
- Allocation approaches: **asset-only**, **liability-relative** (funded ratio, surplus), and **goals-based** (buckets).
- Construction approaches: **top-down vs bottom-up**, **passive vs active**, **core-satellite**, **factor-based** and **risk parity**.
- ESG can be applied through screening, thematic, integration, engagement or impact approaches, and should be set out in the IPS.
