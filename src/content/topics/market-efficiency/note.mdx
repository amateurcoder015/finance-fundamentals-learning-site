# Market Efficiency

A market is **informationally efficient** when security prices fully and quickly reflect all available relevant information. If that holds, no investor can consistently earn **abnormal (risk-adjusted excess) returns** just by trading on that information, and market prices are the best estimate of **intrinsic value** (the value of an asset given a hypothetically complete understanding of its investment characteristics).

The idea matters for every equity decision: it tells you whether stock picking, chart reading or insider knowledge can pay, and how much to spend on active management.

---

## 1. The Concept of Market Efficiency

### Market value versus intrinsic value

- **Market value**: the price at which an asset trades today.
- **Intrinsic value**: the price a fully informed investor would pay.

In an efficient market the two are equal (or any gap is random and cannot be exploited). In an inefficient market, price may deviate from intrinsic value, and **fundamental analysis** might find mispriced securities.

### Abnormal return

Efficiency is tested against a model of **expected (required) return**, for example the capital asset pricing model:

$$E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i\,[E(R_m) - R_f]$$

$$\text{Abnormal return} = \text{Actual return} - \text{Expected return}$$

An abnormal return is also called **alpha** or **excess return** (risk-adjusted). A market is efficient with respect to a strategy if, **after adjusting for risk and net of transaction costs**, the strategy cannot earn a positive abnormal return consistently.

### Factors that affect efficiency

| Factor | Effect on efficiency |
| --- | --- |
| **Number of market participants and depth of analyst coverage** | More participants and analysts raise efficiency |
| **Information availability and financial disclosure** | Better, timelier disclosure raises efficiency |
| **Limits to trading** (short-sale restrictions, price limits, trading halts) | Reduce efficiency, since negative views cannot be expressed |
| **Transaction costs and information-acquisition costs** | Efficiency requires prices to move until further trading is unprofitable; costs let some mispricing persist |
| **Liquidity and market structure** | Illiquid, thinly traded, small-cap and frontier markets tend to be less efficient |

The **arbitrage** process is the engine of efficiency: informed traders who spot mispricing buy undervalued and sell overvalued securities, pushing prices back toward intrinsic value. Costs and trading limits restrict arbitrage.

## 2. Forms of Market Efficiency (Fama)

The forms are defined by the **information set** that is already in prices.

| Form | Information reflected in prices | Implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Weak form** | All **past market data**: historical prices, volume | Technical analysis (chart patterns, trend rules) cannot earn abnormal returns |
| **Semi-strong form** | All **publicly available information**: past prices plus financial statements, news, announcements, analyst reports | Neither technical nor **fundamental analysis** of public information earns abnormal returns |
| **Strong form** | **All information**, public **and private** (including insider information) | Even insiders cannot earn abnormal returns |

The forms are **nested**: strong includes semi-strong, which includes weak. If the weak form fails, so do the semi-strong and strong forms. Evidence suggests that major developed equity markets are broadly **semi-strong form efficient** with some exceptions, while the **strong form** generally does not hold, which is why **insider trading is illegal** and does earn abnormal returns.

## 3. Testing Market Efficiency

### Weak form tests
- **Autocorrelation / time-series tests**: do past returns predict future returns?
- **Technical trading rule tests**: simulate rules (moving averages, breakouts), adjusting for risk and transaction costs.

### Semi-strong form tests: event studies
An **event study** measures the **abnormal return** around the release of public information (earnings, dividend change, merger, stock split, index inclusion). In a semi-strong efficient market, prices adjust **immediately** on the announcement and show **no further drift** afterwards. The **cumulative abnormal return (CAR)** is the sum of the abnormal returns across the event window.

### Strong form tests
Analyse the returns to **insiders** (using insider trading reports) and to **professional fund managers**. If insiders consistently earn abnormal returns, the strong form fails.

### The joint hypothesis problem
Any test of efficiency is a **joint test**: of market efficiency **and** of the asset pricing model used for expected returns. A finding of abnormal returns may mean the market is inefficient, **or** that the model mismeasures risk. This is why efficiency can never be proven conclusively.

### Worked example 1: abnormal return

Stock A has beta $\beta = 1.2$. The risk-free rate is 3% and the expected market return is 9%. Over the year the stock returned 13%.

1. Expected return $= 3\% + 1.2 \times (9\% - 3\%) = 3\% + 7.2\% = 10.2\%$.
2. Abnormal return $= 13\% - 10.2\% = +2.8\%$.

The stock outperformed its risk-adjusted expectation by 2.8%. In an efficient market, one such result is expected by chance; efficiency is questioned only if such returns persist across many securities and periods **after costs**.

### Worked example 2: event study

A company announces a surprise dividend increase. A CAPM-based market model gives these daily abnormal returns around the announcement (Day 0):

| Day | Abnormal return |
| --- | --- |
| -2 | +0.1% |
| -1 | +0.2% |
| 0 | +2.9% |
| +1 | +0.1% |
| +2 | -0.1% |
| +3 | +0.2% |

- CAR over days $-2$ to $+3$ $= 0.1 + 0.2 + 2.9 + 0.1 - 0.1 + 0.2 = +3.4\%$.
- CAR from $0$ to $+3$ $= 2.9 + 0.1 - 0.1 + 0.2 = +3.1\%$.

Almost all the reaction (+2.9%) occurs on the announcement day, and the post-announcement abnormal returns are small and mixed in sign, consistent with **semi-strong efficiency**. A **sustained positive drift** after the announcement would signal slow price adjustment, which contradicts semi-strong efficiency.

## 4. Market Anomalies

An **anomaly** is a pattern in returns that contradicts the efficient market hypothesis, i.e., apparently predictable abnormal returns.

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Time-series (calendar) anomalies** | **January effect** (small-cap stocks earn unusually high returns in January), turn-of-the-month effect, day-of-the-week and weekend effects, momentum and overreaction |
| **Cross-sectional anomalies** | **Size effect** (small-cap stocks outperform after risk adjustment), **value effect** (low price-to-book or low P/E stocks outperform) |
| **Other** | Closed-end fund discounts, earnings surprises followed by drift (post-earnings-announcement drift), IPO underpricing |

**Momentum** (stocks that did well recently keep doing well over the following months) and **overreaction** (long-term losers later rebound) both seem to contradict weak-form efficiency.

### Why anomalies need caution

- Many disappear after they are **published** and traded on, or after **transaction costs** and **risk adjustment**.
- **Data mining**: testing many patterns on the same data will find some "significant" result by chance.
- Results depend on the **sample period**, the **model of expected returns** (joint hypothesis) and may reflect **unmeasured risk** rather than mispricing.
- Many anomalies concentrate in small, illiquid stocks where trading is costly.

A pattern is an **exploitable** inefficiency only if it persists, is large enough to cover trading costs and risk, and can be traded in size.

### Worked example 3: is the anomaly exploitable?

A strategy buys small-cap value stocks and earns a gross return 2.5% per year above the risk-adjusted benchmark. Annual trading volume equals 120% of the portfolio's value, and all-in trading cost (commissions, spread, market impact) is 1.0% of the value traded.

1. Annual trading cost $= 120\% \times 1.0\% = 1.2\%$ of portfolio value.
2. Management and research cost $= 0.6\%$.
3. Net abnormal return $= 2.5\% - 1.2\% - 0.6\% = +0.7\%$.

The pattern survives costs but only by 0.7%, a small margin that could vanish with estimation error in the benchmark. If trading cost were 2.0% of the value traded, trading cost would be $120\% \times 2.0\% = 2.4\%$ and the net abnormal return would be $2.5 - 2.4 - 0.6 = -0.5\%$: not exploitable.

## 5. Behavioral Finance and Efficiency

Traditional finance assumes investors are rational, risk averse and maximize expected utility. **Behavioral finance** documents systematic departures from rationality:

- **Loss aversion**: losses hurt more than equivalent gains please, so investors hold losers too long and sell winners too soon (the **disposition effect**).
- **Overconfidence**: too much trading, underestimated risk.
- **Herding**: investors follow the crowd, which can produce momentum and bubbles.
- **Mental accounting**, **anchoring** and **representativeness**: investors misjudge probabilities and base values on arbitrary reference points.
- **Overreaction and underreaction** to news.
- **Information cascades**: investors copy earlier decisions rather than act on their own information.

Behavioral biases may explain anomalies, but **limits to arbitrage** (cost, risk and short-sale restrictions) can stop rational traders from correcting the mispricing. Other researchers argue that the anomalies are compensation for **risk** or artifacts of **data mining**, so the evidence is contested. **Bubbles** (prices rising far above intrinsic value) and **crashes** are often cited as challenges to market efficiency, but it is hard to know a price is a bubble in real time, so they are not clear proof of inefficiency.

## 6. Implications for Investors

| If the market is... | Implication |
| --- | --- |
| **Weak-form efficient** | Technical analysis has no value |
| **Semi-strong efficient** | Fundamental analysis of public information cannot earn abnormal returns; use a **passive (indexing)** approach, or manage for tax, costs and the investor's own risk profile |
| **Strong-form efficient** | Even private information is worthless |
| **Inefficient** (in places) | **Active management** can add value, especially in less efficient segments such as small-cap and emerging markets; skilled managers can beat net of fees only if mispricing exceeds their costs |

**Active versus passive**: active managers incur research and trading costs and higher fees, so they must generate enough alpha to cover them. The more efficient the market, the stronger the case for **low-cost passive investing**. Even with efficient markets, portfolio managers still add value by matching a portfolio to the client's risk, return, tax and liquidity needs.

**Practical analyst advice**: do not assume a finding of "mispricing" is correct. Ask what the market might know that you do not, and whether the apparent gain survives costs and risk.

## Key takeaways

- An informationally efficient market's prices fully reflect available information, so investors cannot earn consistent **risk-adjusted** abnormal returns net of costs.
- Abnormal return = actual return − expected return from a pricing model (e.g., CAPM: $R_f + \beta(R_m - R_f)$).
- **Weak form**: past prices and volume reflected (technical analysis fails). **Semi-strong**: all public information reflected (fundamental analysis of public data fails). **Strong form**: all information, including private (insiders cannot profit). Forms are nested.
- Event studies test semi-strong efficiency: price adjusts immediately at the announcement, with no drift.
- Tests are **joint hypothesis** tests of efficiency and the asset pricing model, so efficiency is never proven.
- Anomalies (January, size, value, momentum, overreaction) must be judged against data mining, risk, transaction costs and publication effects.
- Behavioral biases can explain apparent inefficiencies, but limits to arbitrage keep them from being easy to exploit.
- The greater the efficiency of a market, the stronger the case for low-cost passive investing; active management is more promising in less efficient, less liquid segments.
