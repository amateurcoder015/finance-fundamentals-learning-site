# Credit Analysis for Government Issuers

Governments are the largest borrowers in the bond market, and their debt is often treated as the benchmark for the lowest credit risk. That is only partly true. A government that borrows in its own currency can usually avoid default by raising taxes or creating money, but a government that borrows in foreign currency, or a city that cannot print money, can and does default. This chapter covers how analysts judge the credit quality of **sovereign** issuers (national governments) and of **non-sovereign** government issuers: sub-sovereign governments, quasi-government agencies and supranational organisations.

---

## 1. Why Government Credit Analysis Is Different

Corporate credit analysis asks whether cash flows will cover debt service and whether collateral and covenants protect the lender. Government credit analysis has two special features.

1. **Ability to pay** depends on the economy, the tax base and the budget, but also on powers that a company lacks: the power to tax, to regulate, and (for a sovereign with its own currency) to issue money.
2. **Willingness to pay** matters as much as ability. A sovereign is not subject to bankruptcy courts, and creditors have limited legal recourse. A government may be able to pay but choose not to, for example if servicing foreign debt is politically unpopular. Analysts therefore weigh political stability, institutions and the history of past defaults alongside the numbers.

<Note>Ability to pay asks "can the government pay?" and willingness to pay asks "will it?". Both are required. A sovereign with strong finances but weak institutions or a record of repudiation can still be a poor credit.</Note>

## 2. Sovereign Credit Analysis

### 2.1 Local versus foreign currency debt

Sovereign credit ratings are usually issued **separately for local-currency debt and foreign-currency debt**.

| Feature | Local-currency debt | Foreign-currency debt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ability to repay | The government can raise taxes or have the central bank create money | The government must earn or borrow foreign currency; it cannot print it |
| Typical default risk | Lower | Higher |
| Typical rating | Equal to or higher than the foreign-currency rating | Equal to or lower than the local-currency rating |
| Main risk beyond default | Inflation and currency depreciation (printing money to repay erodes real value) | Exchange-rate and balance-of-payments crises |

Historically sovereign defaults on foreign-currency debt have been more frequent than defaults on local-currency debt. Local-currency debt is not risk-free: a government may repay in full nominal terms but through inflation that destroys the real value of the bonds.

### 2.2 Key factors in sovereign credit quality

Rating agencies and analysts evaluate a set of broad factors. The groupings below follow the common framework.

| Factor | What the analyst examines |
| --- | --- |
| **Institutions and political risk** | Stability and predictability of policy, rule of law, effectiveness of the government and courts, transparency, corruption, social cohesion, geopolitical risk, track record of honouring debt |
| **Economic prospects** | Size and diversification of the economy, growth rate and potential growth, GDP per capita, demographics, dependence on one commodity or sector, labour and capital productivity |
| **International investment position and external debt** | The country's net claims on the rest of the world; amount of external (foreign-owned or foreign-currency) debt relative to GDP and exports; share of debt that is short term |
| **Fiscal strength** | Government debt relative to GDP, the primary and overall budget balance, debt affordability (interest cost relative to revenue), contingent liabilities, structure and maturity of debt, ageing-related pension and health costs |
| **Monetary flexibility** | Whether the central bank is independent and credible, can set rates and act as lender of last resort, has an inflation target, and whether the currency is a reserve currency |
| **External liquidity** | Size of foreign-exchange reserves, the current-account balance, and exposure to sudden stops in capital inflows; reserve currency status |

A few comments on the metrics:

- **Debt-to-GDP** (gross or net) measures the burden of debt relative to the size of the economy. Higher is weaker, but the level that is sustainable depends on the interest rate, growth, and the investor base. A country that borrows in its own currency from domestic holders can carry much higher debt than one that relies on foreign lenders.
- **Interest burden** (interest paid divided by government revenue) shows how much of the budget is committed to creditors.
- **Primary balance** is the budget balance excluding interest payments. A primary surplus means revenue exceeds non-interest spending, leaving something to service debt.
- **Foreign-exchange reserves** are often measured as months of import cover, or relative to short-term external debt.
- A **reserve currency** issuer, or one with deep local capital markets and a credible central bank, has greater room to borrow and so a stronger credit profile for a given debt level.

### 2.3 Debt sustainability

A government's debt-to-GDP ratio rises when the interest rate on debt exceeds nominal GDP growth and falls with primary surpluses. A common simplified identity for the debt ratio is:

$$d_t = d_{t-1}\times\frac{1+r}{1+g} - pb_t$$

where $d$ is debt as a share of GDP, $r$ is the average nominal interest rate on the debt, $g$ is nominal GDP growth, and $pb$ is the primary balance as a share of GDP (positive for a surplus). The primary surplus needed to keep the ratio constant (so $d_t = d_{t-1}$) is:

$$pb^{*} = d_{t-1}\times\frac{r-g}{1+g}$$

**Worked example.** A country has debt of 60% of GDP, an average interest rate of 4% and nominal GDP growth of 2%.

1. Growth factor of the ratio: $\dfrac{1+r}{1+g} = \dfrac{1.04}{1.02} = 1.019608$.
2. Debt before any primary balance: $60\% \times 1.019608 = 61.176\%$ of GDP.
3. If the government runs a primary **surplus** of 1.0% of GDP: $d_t = 61.176\% - 1.0\% = \mathbf{60.176\%}$. The ratio still rises, because the surplus is too small.
4. Debt-stabilising primary surplus: $pb^{*} = 60\% \times \dfrac{0.04-0.02}{1.02} = 60\% \times 0.019608 = \mathbf{1.176\%}$ of GDP.
5. If the primary surplus equals 1.176%, the ratio stays at 60%. If growth were 5% instead (so $r<g$), the ratio would fall even with a small primary deficit (for example 0.5% of GDP: $60\% \times 1.04/1.05 + 0.5\% = 59.93\%$).

The key insight: **when $r > g$, the government needs a primary surplus just to hold the ratio steady**, and the higher the debt, the larger that surplus. When $g > r$, debt can be stable or falling even without surpluses.

### 2.4 External position: a reserves example

Foreign-currency debt depends on the country's access to foreign exchange. One simple adequacy test is reserves expressed in months of imports.

**Worked example.** A country holds reserves of 120 billion USD and imports 480 billion USD of goods and services a year.

1. Monthly imports $= 480 / 12 = 40$ billion USD.
2. Import cover $= 120 / 40 = \mathbf{3}$ months.

Analysts also compare reserves to **short-term external debt** (debt maturing within a year). If reserves are smaller than the debt that must be rolled over or repaid, a loss of market confidence can trigger a currency and debt crisis.

### 2.5 Sovereign default and restructuring

Because sovereigns cannot be liquidated, defaults end in negotiation. Typical outcomes are a maturity extension, a lower coupon or a principal reduction (a **haircut**). **Collective action clauses (CACs)** in bond contracts allow a qualified majority of holders to agree a restructuring that binds the minority, reducing the holdout problem. The **pari passu** clause says a sovereign will not treat one group of creditors better than another. Recovery depends heavily on the political and economic context, so recoveries vary widely.

## 3. Non-Sovereign Government Issuers

Non-sovereign issuers are government-related borrowers below or alongside the central government.

### 3.1 Sub-sovereign (local and regional) governments

States, provinces, cities and municipalities issue bonds, often called **municipal bonds** in the United States. They do not control a national currency, so their ability to pay depends on their tax base and budget. They generally issue two main types of bond.

| Feature | General obligation (GO) bonds | Revenue bonds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source of repayment | The issuer's general taxing power and overall revenue | Cash flows from a specific project (toll road, airport, hospital, water system) |
| Legal backing | Full faith and credit of the issuer | Only the project revenue (and any reserve funds), not the issuer's general revenue |
| Key analysis | Tax base, tax collection, economy, debt burden, budget flexibility | Project economics, demand, rates and charges, coverage ratio, covenants |
| Typical risk | Lower than revenue bonds of the same issuer | Higher: depends on the project's success |

For **GO bonds**, the analyst studies the local economy and tax base (wealth, employment, property values), the issuer's debt burden relative to its revenue or the value of local property, pension and other obligations, budget flexibility and the history of balanced budgets. Political limits on taxation and legal debt limits also matter.

For **revenue bonds**, the central measure is the **debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)**:

$$\text{DSCR} = \frac{\text{Net revenue available for debt service}}{\text{Debt service (interest + principal)}}$$

A DSCR below 1.0 means project cash flow is not enough to pay debt service. Bond covenants frequently require a minimum DSCR (for example 1.2 or more) and a debt service reserve fund.

**Worked example.** A toll road produces revenue of 48 million and operating expenses of 30 million. Annual debt service is 12 million.

1. Net revenue available for debt service $= 48 - 30 = 18$ million.
2. $\text{DSCR} = 18 / 12 = \mathbf{1.50}$.
3. Revenue could fall until net revenue equals 12 million, that is revenue of 42 million (a fall of $6/48 = 12.5\%$ with costs unchanged) before coverage drops to 1.00.

Operating leverage matters here: because expenses are largely fixed, a 12.5% fall in revenue cuts net revenue by a third (from 18 to 12), a much bigger proportional fall.

### 3.2 Quasi-government entities

**Quasi-government (agency) issuers** are created by governments to carry out public policy, such as housing finance agencies, development banks and state-owned infrastructure bodies. Their credit quality depends on:

- **The strength of government support**: a legal guarantee, an implicit expectation of support, or neither.
- **The entity's own finances**: if it is self-financing, its standalone profile matters more.
- **The government's ownership, public-policy importance and track record** of supporting the entity.

A bond with an **explicit government guarantee** has credit quality close to the government's own. Without one, the bond's rating usually sits below the sovereign's, reflecting the chance of support not being given.

### 3.3 Supranational agencies

**Supranationals** are multilateral institutions set up by several countries, for example the World Bank, the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the European Investment Bank and regional development banks. They lend to member countries for development and stability and finance themselves by issuing bonds. Credit strength typically comes from:

- **Shareholder support**: callable capital commitments from highly rated member governments.
- **A preferred creditor status** that makes borrowers service the institution's loans before other debts.
- **Strong capital, liquidity and conservative lending policies.**

Many supranationals carry very high ratings and their bonds trade at small spreads to government benchmarks.

## 4. Putting It Together

| Issuer | Repayment source | What drives risk | Typical position vs sovereign |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sovereign, local currency | Taxes, central bank money creation | Inflation, institutions, fiscal strength | Benchmark |
| Sovereign, foreign currency | Tax revenue converted into FX, reserves, external borrowing | External position, reserves, willingness to pay | Equal or lower than local-currency rating |
| Sub-sovereign GO | Local taxes | Tax base, budget, debt burden | Usually at or below the sovereign |
| Sub-sovereign revenue | Project cash flows | DSCR, demand, covenants | Below GO of the same issuer |
| Quasi-government | Own revenue plus government support | Degree of support | Depends on guarantee |
| Supranational | Member support, capital | Shareholder strength, capital | Often among the highest |

---

## Key takeaways

- Government credit analysis weighs both **ability to pay** and **willingness to pay**; sovereigns cannot be forced into bankruptcy.
- Sovereign ratings usually differ for **local-currency** and **foreign-currency** debt; local-currency debt is rated equal to or higher because the government can tax and create money, but it carries inflation risk.
- Sovereign credit factors: institutions and political risk, economic prospects, international investment position and external debt, fiscal strength, monetary flexibility, and external liquidity.
- The debt ratio evolves as $d_t = d_{t-1}(1+r)/(1+g) - pb_t$. When $r > g$ a primary surplus is needed to stabilise debt; stabilising surplus $= d(r-g)/(1+g)$.
- **GO bonds** rely on taxing power; **revenue bonds** rely on project cash flow, assessed with the **debt service coverage ratio**.
- **Quasi-government** credit depends on the strength of government support; **supranationals** rely on member-government capital and preferred creditor status.
- Sovereign restructurings are negotiated; **collective action clauses** bind holdout creditors.
